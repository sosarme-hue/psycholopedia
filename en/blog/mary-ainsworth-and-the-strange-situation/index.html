Mary Ainsworth and the 'Strange Situation'

Twenty minutes of observation in a playroom changed how we understand the bond between babies and those who care for them.

Talking about "secure attachment" is almost an everyday thing today. Behind the phrase is the work of a psychologist who spent hours watching mothers and babies at home before bringing them into the laboratory.

From Toronto to Uganda

Mary Dinsmore Salter, later Ainsworth, was born in Ohio in 1913 and trained at the University of Toronto. In the 1950s she worked in London with John Bowlby, who was developing attachment theory. She then moved with her husband to Uganda, where she observed Ganda families for months. She saw that babies used their mother as a "secure base" from which to explore and to return to when frightened.

Twenty minutes in a room

Later, at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore, she designed a short procedure for observing attachment in babies of about one year: the Strange Situation, described with Silvia Bell in 1970. In a room with toys, the baby goes through several episodes of about three minutes: playing with the mother, a stranger coming in, the mother leaving and returning, the baby left briefly alone. The most revealing part was not how much the baby cried when left alone but how it reacted to the reunion.

Three patterns, then four

  • Secure: the baby seeks the mother on her return, calms down and goes back to playing.
  • Avoidant: seems unmoved and avoids contact at the reunion.
  • Ambivalent or resistant: seeks contact but finds it hard to settle and seems angry.

Later Mary Main and Judith Solomon described a fourth pattern, disorganised, with contradictory or fearful behaviour, more common in situations of maltreatment or serious family difficulty.

What we know today

Caregiver sensitivity, that is, noticing the baby's signals and responding promptly and appropriately, is associated with secure attachment, though the link is moderate. Attachment is not destiny: it can change with circumstances, and no parent needs to be perfect. As the paediatrician Donald Winnicott put it, it is enough to be "good enough".

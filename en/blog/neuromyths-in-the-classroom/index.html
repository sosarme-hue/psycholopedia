Neuromyths in the classroom

Learning styles, left and right brain, the 10% of the brain… Many teachers believe ideas that research does not support.

Neuromyths are ideas about the brain that sound scientific but are not. Many are born from real research that has been oversimplified or misunderstood, and they spread because they are appealing and easy to apply.

A revealing study

In 2012 Sanne Dekker and colleagues asked teachers in the UK and the Netherlands about a range of statements on the brain. More than 90% believed that pupils learn better when they receive information in their preferred style: visual, auditory or kinaesthetic. Most strikingly, the teachers most interested in neuroscience believed more myths, probably because they had been exposed to more poor-quality popular material.

The most widespread

  • Learning styles: people have preferences, but matching teaching to them does not improve learning. What works is matching the teaching method to the content.
  • Left brain and right brain: the hemispheres have some specialisation, but they always work together. There are no one-hemisphere people.
  • We only use 10% of our brain: imaging shows activity throughout the brain, though not all at once.
  • "Brain gym" exercises claimed to improve coordination between hemispheres: there is no evidence they work.

What is supported

The psychology of learning offers well-supported strategies: practising recall instead of rereading, spacing study over time, mixing types of problems, explaining in your own words and combining text and images when both add something. They are less eye-catching than neuromyths, but they work for almost every pupil.

References

  1. Dekker, S., Lee, N. C., Howard-Jones, P. y Jolles, J. (2012). Neuromyths in education: prevalence and predictors of misconceptions among teachers. Frontiers in Psychology, 3, 429.
  2. Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D. y Bjork, R. (2008). Learning styles: concepts and evidence. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 9(3), 105–119.
  3. Dunlosky, J. et al. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4–58.

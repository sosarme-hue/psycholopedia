The holidays are not always happy

The holidays bring joy to many, and nostalgia, stress or loneliness to others. What is true in the clichés and what can help.

Lights, family gatherings, meals and presents. December is presented as the happiest month of the year, but not everyone experiences it that way. For many people, the holidays also bring nostalgia, stress, loneliness or family tension.

The empty chairs

Celebrations make absences more visible. Those who have lost a loved one often feel the loss more keenly at this time of year, especially the first year. The same happens to people living far from their families, those going through a separation and those with no one to celebrate with. The pressure to be cheerful can make that sadness feel guilty.

The stress of perfection

Organising meals, juggling diaries, buying presents and spending more than planned all create tension. Then there are the expectations: that the gathering will be perfect and everyone will get on. Long-standing family arguments do not disappear because it is Christmas.

A common myth

It is often said that suicides rise at Christmas. Studies on the seasonality of suicide do not support this: in many countries December is one of the months with the fewest cases, and peaks tend to appear in spring and early summer. Repeating the myth does not help, though that does not change the fact that the holidays are hard for many people.

What usually helps

  • Adjust your expectations: an imperfect celebration is still worth it.
  • Make room to remember those who are missing, with a toast, a candle or a story.
  • Set limits on spending and commitments.
  • Think of anyone who may be alone: a call or an invitation means a lot.
  • Keep up routines of sleep, exercise and moderation with alcohol.

If the distress is intense or you have thoughts of harming yourself, seek help. In Spain, the 024 line is free and open 24 hours, Christmas included; elsewhere, contact your local crisis line.

References

  1. Woo, J.-M., Okusaga, O. y Postolache, T. T. (2012). Seasonality of suicidal behavior. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 9(2), 531–547.
  2. Holt-Lunstad, J., Smith, T. B. y Layton, J. B. (2010). Social relationships and mortality risk: a meta-analytic review. PLoS Medicine, 7(7), e1000316.

Unwanted loneliness is a health matter too

Being alone and feeling lonely are not the same. What research says and what each of us can do.

In the surgery it shows in the details: the patient who stretches out the visit because it is their only conversation of the day, or the one whose symptoms ease when someone listens. Unwanted loneliness is common, and not only among older people.

Being alone is not feeling lonely

Psychology distinguishes social isolation, which is objective (few relationships, little contact), from loneliness, which is subjective: the gap between the relationships we have and those we would like. Some people with few relationships do not feel lonely, and some surrounded by people do.

Why it matters

A meta-analysis by Julianne Holt-Lunstad and colleagues (2010), with data from more than 300,000 people, found that stronger social relationships were associated with a clearly higher likelihood of survival during follow-up. These are association studies and the relationship is complex, but the size of the effect led to social relationships being treated as a public health factor.

What can be done

  • Pick up a contact you have been meaning to: a short call counts.
  • Join something regular (a group, volunteering, a local activity), where relationships grow on their own over time.
  • Pay attention to those around us: asking with genuine interest is a small, effective intervention.

If loneliness comes with persistent sadness or thoughts that life is not worth living, ask for help. In Spain, the 024 line is open 24 hours; elsewhere, contact your local helpline.

References

  1. Holt-Lunstad, J., Smith, T. B. y Layton, J. B. (2010). Social relationships and mortality risk: a meta-analytic review. PLoS Medicine, 7(7), e1000316.
  2. Cacioppo, J. T. y Patrick, W. (2008). Loneliness: Human Nature and the Need for Social Connection. Nueva York: Norton.

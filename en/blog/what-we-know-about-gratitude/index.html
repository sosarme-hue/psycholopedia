What we know (and don't) about gratitude

Writing down what we are grateful for has become fashionable. Studies show real effects, but more modest than some books promise.

Gratitude journals have become very popular. The idea is simple: every day or week, write down a few things you are grateful for. Does it work?

The classic study

In 2003 Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough asked students to spend ten weeks writing down five things they were grateful for, five hassles or five neutral events. Those who wrote about gratitude felt somewhat better about their lives, were more optimistic about the coming week and reported exercising more.

What the reviews say

Later meta-analyses confirm that gratitude interventions slightly improve wellbeing and mood. But the effect is small, and it shrinks when compared with similar activities, such as writing about anything positive. It is no substitute for treatment for depression or anxiety.

A curious detail: some studies suggest that writing once a week works as well as or better than doing it daily, perhaps because the task does not become routine.

How to try it

  • Now and then, write down three specific things that went well and why.
  • Vary it: do not always repeat the same things.
  • Express your thanks to someone else, with a letter or a message. This tends to have a bigger effect, both on the giver and the receiver.
  • Do not force it: if it becomes an obligation or a way of denying real problems, it stops helping.

Gratitude is not a magic solution, but it is a simple, free tool with no side effects for paying attention to what is going right.

References

  1. Emmons, R. A. y McCullough, M. E. (2003). Counting blessings versus burdens: an experimental investigation of gratitude and subjective well-being in daily life. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 84(2), 377–389.
  2. Dickens, L. R. (2017). Using gratitude to promote positive change: a series of meta-analyses investigating the effectiveness of gratitude interventions. Basic and Applied Social Psychology, 39(4), 193–208.

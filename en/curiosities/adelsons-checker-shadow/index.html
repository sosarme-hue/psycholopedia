Your perception surprises you

Adelson's checker shadow

Two squares of exactly the same grey that the brain refuses to see as equal.

In 1995, Edward Adelson of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology published an image of a chessboard with a cylinder casting a shadow across it. Square A, outside the shadow, looks dark. Square B, inside the shadow, looks light. In fact, they are exactly the same shade of grey.

The brain corrects for the shadow

If you cover the surroundings or join the squares with a grey strip, you can see they are the same. Even so, when the strip is removed, the illusion returns.

The visual system is not trying to measure the light reaching the eye but to work out the colour of the surface. Because it knows shadows darken things, it "lightens" what is in shadow. This is the same colour constancy that lets us recognise a yellow banana under any lighting, and the same one behind the blue-or-gold dress debate.

References

  1. Adelson, E. H. (2000). Lightness perception and lightness illusions. En M. Gazzaniga (ed.), The New Cognitive Neurosciences (2.ª ed., pp. 339–351). MIT Press.

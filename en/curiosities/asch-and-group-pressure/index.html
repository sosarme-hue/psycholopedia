Experiments that changed questions

Asch and group pressure: what is usually forgotten

The line experiment is famous for conformity. But most answers were independent.

In the early 1950s Solomon Asch brought together groups of students for a supposed perception test: they had to say which of three lines matched a reference line in length. The answer was obvious. But every group member except one was in league with the researcher and, in some rounds, all gave the same wrong answer aloud.

What is usually told

In the critical rounds, about a third of the real participant's answers matched the group's error, and three out of four participants gave in at least once. That is the figure that entered the textbooks as proof of the power of conformity.

What is usually forgotten

  • About two thirds of answers were correct, against the majority. Roughly a quarter of participants never gave in.
  • If just one person in the group gave the correct answer, conformity dropped sharply.
  • Many participants said afterwards that they knew the right answer but did not want to stand out. The pressure was social, not perceptual.
  • Later replications have found differences by country, period and task.

Asch himself was as interested in independence as in conformity. His experiment shows both.

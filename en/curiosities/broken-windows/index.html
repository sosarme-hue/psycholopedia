What the original study did not say

The broken windows theory

An abandoned car in the Bronx and another in Palo Alto inspired a theory that changed New York policing. The evidence is more nuanced.

In 1969, Philip Zimbardo left a car with no number plates and its bonnet up on a street in the Bronx, New York, and an identical one in Palo Alto, California. The Bronx car was stripped within a day. The Palo Alto car stayed untouched for a week, until Zimbardo himself smashed a window; then other people began to wreck it.

From anecdote to policy

In 1982, James Q. Wilson and George Kelling argued in an article that signs of disorder, such as broken windows or graffiti, invite more disorder and more serious crime. In the 1990s, New York police focused on pursuing minor offences, and crime fell.

But it also fell in cities that did not adopt that policy, and there are many other possible explanations. Some field experiments, such as those by Kees Keizer in the Netherlands, show that disorder increases behaviours like littering. However, it is not clear that targeting minor offences reduces serious crime, and the strategy has been criticised for falling mainly on poor neighbourhoods and minorities.

References

  1. Wilson, J. Q. y Kelling, G. L. (1982). Broken windows. The Atlantic Monthly, 249(3), 29–38.
  2. Keizer, K., Lindenberg, S. y Steg, L. (2008). The spreading of disorder. Science, 322(5908), 1681–1685.
  3. Braga, A. A., Welsh, B. C. y Schnell, C. (2015). Can policing disorder reduce crime? Journal of Research in Crime and Delinquency, 52(4), 567–588.

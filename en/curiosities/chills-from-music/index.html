Psychology in film, literature and music

Chills from music

That shiver with a song activates the same pleasure circuits as food or sex.

Your hair stands on end or a shiver runs down your spine at a particular moment in a song. In English this is called chills or frisson. It happens to many people, though not everyone.

Pleasure and anticipation

In 2001, Anne Blood and Robert Zatorre recorded musicians' brain activity while they listened to pieces that gave them chills. Regions linked to reward lit up. In 2011, the same group showed that dopamine is released both at the moment of the chill and in the seconds of anticipation beforehand.

Chills tend to come with unexpected changes: a voice coming in, a shift in volume or a change of key. People higher in openness to experience tend to feel them more. Some people feel no pleasure at all from music, a condition called specific musical anhedonia.

References

  1. Blood, A. J. y Zatorre, R. J. (2001). Intensely pleasurable responses to music correlate with activity in brain regions implicated in reward and emotion. PNAS, 98(20), 11818–11823.
  2. Salimpoor, V. N. et al. (2011). Anatomically distinct dopamine release during anticipation and experience of peak emotion to music. Nature Neuroscience, 14(2), 257–262.

Psychology in film, literature and music

Funes the Memorious and the man who could not forget

Borges imagined someone who remembered everything and could not think. Luria met a real man with a similar memory.

In the story Funes the Memorious (1942), Jorge Luis Borges describes Ireneo Funes, a young man who, after falling from a horse, remembers absolutely everything: every leaf of every tree he has seen, every time he saw it. But that prevents him from thinking, because, Borges writes, "to think is to forget differences, to generalise, to abstract".

A real Funes

The Soviet neuropsychologist Alexander Luria studied Solomon Shereshevsky for thirty years, a journalist who never took notes. He recalled very long lists of words or numbers years later. He had intense synaesthesia, linking every sound with images and tastes. But he struggled to understand metaphors and to forget what he no longer needed. Luria told his story in The Mind of a Mnemonist (1968).

Today hyperthymesia, an extraordinary autobiographical memory, has been described in very few people. Their cases, like Funes's, remind us that forgetting is not just a failure: it is also necessary for thinking.

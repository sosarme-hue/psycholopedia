The story behind an idea

H. M., the man who could not form new memories

A 1953 operation took away his ability to remember anything new and taught science how memory works.

Henry Molaison, known for decades only as "H. M.", had severe epilepsy. In 1953, at the age of 27, the neurosurgeon William Scoville removed parts of both medial temporal lobes, including much of the hippocampus. His seizures improved, but Henry could no longer form new memories of facts and events.

What he taught us

The psychologist Brenda Milner studied him for years. Henry forgot people within minutes, yet he improved with practice at a mirror-drawing task, even though he did not remember having done it before.

This showed that there are different memory systems: the one that stores facts and experiences depends on the hippocampus, and the one that stores skills does not. Henry died in 2008, and his brain was cut into thousands of sections for further study.

References

  1. Scoville, W. B. y Milner, B. (1957). Loss of recent memory after bilateral hippocampal lesions. Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery and Psychiatry, 20(1), 11–21.
  2. Corkin, S. (2013). Permanent Present Tense. Basic Books.

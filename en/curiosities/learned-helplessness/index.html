What the original study did not say

Learned helplessness, fifty years on

Dogs that stopped trying to escape. Its discoverer admitted in 2016 that he had understood it the wrong way round.

In 1967, Martin Seligman and Steven Maier exposed dogs to shocks they could not avoid. Later, in a box where jumping a low barrier was enough to escape, many did not even try: they lay down and endured it. They called this learned helplessness: the animals had learned that nothing they did made any difference.

A theory of depression

The idea became an influential model of depression: passivity would be learned. The studies are also remembered for the suffering they caused the animals.

In 2016, Maier and Seligman published a review based on neuroscience: passivity in the face of distress would be the brain's default response. What is learned is control: discovering that our actions work activates circuits in the prefrontal cortex that inhibit that response. In other words, it is not helplessness that is learned, but hope.

References

  1. Seligman, M. E. y Maier, S. F. (1967). Failure to escape traumatic shock. Journal of Experimental Psychology, 74(1), 1–9.
  2. Maier, S. F. y Seligman, M. E. (2016). Learned helplessness at fifty. Psychological Review, 123(4), 349–367.

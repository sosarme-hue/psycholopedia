Experiments that changed questions

"How fast were the cars going when they smashed?"

Changing one verb in a question was enough for people to remember broken glass that was not there.

In 1974, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed students films of traffic accidents. Then they asked how fast the cars were going when they "contacted", "hit" or "smashed into" each other. The stronger the verb, the higher the estimated speed.

Glass that was not there

A week later they asked: "Did you see any broken glass?" There was none in the film. But those who had heard "smashed" said yes more than twice as often as the control group.

The experiment opened a huge line of research on the misinformation effect. Loftus went on to show that whole memories of events that never happened can be implanted, such as being lost in a shopping mall as a child. Her work has influenced how witnesses are questioned around the world.

References

  1. Loftus, E. F. y Palmer, J. C. (1974). Reconstruction of automobile destruction. Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 13(5), 585–589.
  2. Loftus, E. F. y Pickrell, J. E. (1995). The formation of false memories. Psychiatric Annals, 25(12), 720–725.

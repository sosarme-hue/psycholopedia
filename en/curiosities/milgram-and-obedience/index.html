Experiments that changed questions

Milgram and obedience to authority

How far will an ordinary person go when someone in a lab coat tells them to continue?

In 1961, at Yale University, Stanley Milgram asked volunteers to act as "teachers" and give electric shocks to a "learner" every time he made a mistake. The shocks were fake and the learner was an actor, but the participants did not know. In the best-known version, about 65% went all the way to the 450-volt switch.

What is often forgotten

Milgram ran more than twenty variations. Obedience dropped sharply when the experimenter gave orders by telephone, when the learner was in the same room or when other "teachers" refused. Many participants experienced intense strain, and the study became one of the reasons for today's ethical rules.

Later reviews of the archives, such as Gina Perry's, showed that the experimenter sometimes improvised and that some participants suspected the set-up. Even so, the central result has been replicated in milder versions: pressure from a legitimate authority is powerful.

References

  1. Milgram, S. (1963). Behavioral study of obedience. Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 67(4), 371–378.
  2. Burger, J. M. (2009). Replicating Milgram: Would people still obey today? American Psychologist, 64(1), 1–11.

Your perception surprises you

Not recognising faces: prosopagnosia

About one in fifty people struggles to recognise faces. At the other end are the "super-recognisers".

In 1947, the German neurologist Joachim Bodamer described patients who, after brain injury, could not recognise faces, not even their own in a mirror, although they could see well and recognised people by their voices. He called it prosopagnosia, from the Greek prosopon, face.

Not just from injury

Over time it emerged that many people have had it all their lives, without any injury. Developmental prosopagnosia is estimated to affect about 2% of the population. Many do not know and think they are just absent-minded. They get by recognising people by voice, hair, clothes or gait. The neurologist Oliver Sacks had it.

At the other end are super-recognisers, described in 2009: people able to recognise a face seen just once, years later. Some police forces, such as London's, employ them to identify suspects in footage.

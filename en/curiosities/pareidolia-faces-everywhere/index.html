Your perception surprises you

Pareidolia: faces in plugs and on the Moon

We see faces in clouds, toast and house fronts. The brain prefers to err on the side of seeing too many.

A plug with "eyes" and a "mouth", a face on a Martian rock photographed by the Viking probe in 1976, or a figure on a slice of toast. Seeing faces or other meaningful shapes in random patterns is called pareidolia.

Better one face too many

Recognising faces quickly was vital for our ancestors: mistaking a shadow for a face costs little, but missing someone lurking could cost dearly. The brain has regions specialised for faces that respond even to these "false faces".

A 2014 study even found activity in those areas when participants believed they saw a face in images of pure noise. And in 2022 it was found that we tend to see more male than female faces in objects. Far from being a fault, it shows how sensitive our visual system is to social cues.

References

  1. Liu, J. et al. (2014). Seeing Jesus in toast. Cortex, 53, 60–77.
  2. Wardle, S. G. et al. (2022). Illusory faces are more likely to be perceived as male than female. PNAS, 119(5), e2117413119.

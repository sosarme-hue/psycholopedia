The story behind an idea

Ringelmann and the rope: the more people, the less each one pulls

A French agricultural engineer discovered that we put in less effort in a group.

In the late nineteenth century, the French engineer Max Ringelmann measured how hard groups of different sizes pulled on a rope. A person alone pulled with full strength, but in groups of eight, each contributed considerably less than when pulling alone.

Social loafing

Part of the drop is due to poor coordination. But in 1979, Bibb Latané and colleagues asked blindfolded people to shout or clap, making them believe they were in a group when they were actually alone. They still put in less effort: this was called social loafing.

It happens when each person's contribution cannot be seen. It shrinks when individuals are identifiable, when the task matters, and in small, close-knit groups. That is why it helps to assign specific, named tasks in group work.

References

  1. Kravitz, D. A. y Martin, B. (1986). Ringelmann rediscovered. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 50(5), 936–941.
  2. Latané, B., Williams, K. y Harkins, S. (1979). Many hands make light the work. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 37(6), 822–832.

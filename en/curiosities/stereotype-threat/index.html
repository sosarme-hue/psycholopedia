What the original study did not say

Stereotype threat, put to the test

Being reminded of a stereotype before a test would worsen performance. The first studies were striking; the large ones, more modest.

In 1995, Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson asked Black and white students at Stanford to take a difficult verbal test. When it was presented as a measure of intellectual ability, Black students did worse; when presented as a simple exercise, the differences disappeared. They called it stereotype threat: the fear of confirming a negative stereotype about your group worsens performance.

Much influence

Hundreds of studies followed, for example with women taking maths tests. The idea influenced education and equality programmes.

But meta-analyses have found signs of publication bias: studies with positive results were published more often. In 2019, a large study of more than 2,000 secondary-school girls in the Netherlands found no effect in maths. It may exist in certain situations, but its size seems smaller than was believed. Educational inequalities have many other causes that remain real.

References

  1. Steele, C. M. y Aronson, J. (1995). Stereotype threat and the intellectual test performance of African Americans. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 69(5), 797–811.
  2. Flore, P. C., Mulder, J. y Wicherts, J. M. (2019). The influence of gender stereotype threat on mathematics test scores of Dutch high school students. Comprehensive Results in Social Psychology, 3(2), 140–174.

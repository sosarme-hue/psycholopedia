Your perception surprises you

The Stroop effect: when reading gets in the way

Say the colour of the ink aloud, not the word. It sounds easy until you try.

In 1935 the American psychologist J. Ridley Stroop published a very simple experiment. He asked people to say aloud the ink colour in which some words were printed. When the word named a different colour ("RED" printed in blue), they took noticeably longer and made more mistakes.

Try it

Press "Start", say aloud the ink colour of each word as fast as you can, then press "Done". First list A, then list B.

Why does it happen?

For an adult who can read, reading is almost automatic: we cannot help reading a familiar word in front of us. Naming the colour takes more attention. When the two pieces of information clash, the brain has to hold back the automatic response, and that takes time. As early as 1886 James McKeen Cattell had noticed that we read words faster than we name colours.

What is it used for?

The Stroop task is one of the most widely used in experimental psychology and neuropsychology to study attention and inhibitory control. There are versions with numbers, emotional words or pictures. Children who do not yet read fluently show little of the effect.

References

  2. MacLeod, C. M. (1991). Half a century of research on the Stroop effect. Psychological Bulletin, 109(2), 163–203.

Your perception surprises you

Synaesthesia: coloured letters and tasting sounds

For some people, A is red or Tuesday is green. It is not imagination: it is stable and measurable.

In synaesthesia, a stimulus in one sense automatically triggers an experience in another. The most common form is seeing letters or numbers in colour, but some people taste flavours when they hear words or see the days of the week arranged in space.

More common than it seems

A 2006 study estimated that around 4% of the population has some kind of synaesthesia. A classic test is to ask for the colours of letters and repeat the question months later: synaesthetes answer almost identically, while people making it up change a lot.

It tends to run in families and has been linked with more connections between sensory areas of the brain. The writer Vladimir Nabokov described his coloured letters, and his wife and son were synaesthetes too. It is not a disorder: most people experience it as neutral or pleasant.

References

  1. Simner, J. et al. (2006). Synaesthesia: the prevalence of atypical cross-modal experiences. Perception, 35(8), 1024–1033.
  2. Ward, J. (2013). Synesthesia. Annual Review of Psychology, 64, 49–75.

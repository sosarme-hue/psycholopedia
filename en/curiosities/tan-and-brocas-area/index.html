The story behind an idea

"Tan": the patient who named Broca's area

He could say only one syllable. His brain showed where speech is produced.

In 1861, the French physician Paul Broca saw Louis Victor Leborgne, a man who had been unable to speak for years. He understood what was said to him but could only pronounce the syllable "tan", so in the hospital he was known by that name.

One lesion, one function

When Leborgne died, Broca examined his brain and found a lesion at the back of the left frontal lobe. Together with similar cases, he argued that this area was essential for producing language. Today it is called Broca's area, and damage to it causes an aphasia in which speech is effortful but comprehension is fairly good.

It was one of the first solid pieces of evidence that different functions depend on specific regions. A few years later, Carl Wernicke described another area, linked to comprehension. Leborgne's brain is preserved in Paris.

References

  1. Broca, P. (1861). Remarques sur le siège de la faculté du langage articulé. Bulletin de la Société Anatomique de Paris, 6, 330–357.
  2. Domanski, C. W. (2013). Mysterious "Monsieur Leborgne". Journal of the History of the Neurosciences, 22(1), 47–52.

Your perception surprises you

The cocktail party effect: hearing your name through the noise

At a party we follow one conversation and ignore the rest, but if someone says our name, we hear it.

In 1953, the British engineer Colin Cherry wondered how we manage to follow one conversation in a room full of voices. He called it the cocktail party problem and studied it with headphones: each ear received a different message and the person had to repeat one of them aloud.

What slips through

Of the ignored message, people remembered almost nothing: neither its content nor whether it switched language. They noticed only basic changes, such as the voice switching from male to female. But in 1959 Neville Moray found that if the ignored message included the participant's name, about a third of people noticed it.

This shows that the brain partly processes what we are not attending to and lets through what matters to us. People with hearing loss and hearing aid users often find these settings especially hard, because separating voices requires hearing in detail.

References

  1. Cherry, E. C. (1953). Some experiments on the recognition of speech, with one and with two ears. Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 25(5), 975–979.
  2. Moray, N. (1959). Attention in dichotic listening. Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 11(1), 56–60.

Experiments that changed questions

The friendship paradox: your friends have more friends than you

It is a mathematical quirk that explains why we often feel others have a richer social life.

In 1991, the sociologist Scott Feld published an article with a striking title: "Why your friends have more friends than you do". It is not a matter of bad luck but of maths.

Why it happens

People with many friends appear on lots of people's friend lists; those with few appear on few. So if you pick someone at random and look at their friends, you are more likely to land on very sociable people. On average, our friends have more friends than we do, even if none of us is especially lonely.

It has consequences. On social media, it can feed the feeling that others lead more active lives. And it has practical uses: in 2009, Nicholas Christakis and James Fowler followed Harvard students and their friends during a flu outbreak, and the friends fell ill about two weeks earlier than the randomly chosen group. Monitoring friends can help detect outbreaks sooner.

References

  1. Feld, S. L. (1991). Why your friends have more friends than you do. American Journal of Sociology, 96(6), 1464–1477.
  2. Christakis, N. A. y Fowler, J. H. (2010). Social network sensors for early detection of contagious outbreaks. PLoS ONE, 5(9), e12948.

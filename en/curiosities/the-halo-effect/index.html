The story behind an idea

The halo effect

If someone seems attractive or likeable, we tend to assume they are also clever and honest.

In 1920, Edward Thorndike analysed army officers' ratings of their soldiers on qualities such as intelligence, loyalty and physique. He noticed that the scores were too closely linked: if a soldier was rated highly on one thing, he tended to be rated highly on everything.

One impression colours the rest

Thorndike called it the halo effect: an overall positive, or negative, impression spreads to unrelated traits. Later studies have shown, for example, that attractive people are credited with more positive qualities, and that the same piece of work is rated more highly when it is thought to come from someone prestigious.

It affects job interviews, school marking and court judgements. One way to reduce it is to rate each aspect separately, using criteria set before meeting the person.

References

  1. Thorndike, E. L. (1920). A constant error in psychological ratings. Journal of Applied Psychology, 4(1), 25–29.
  2. Nisbett, R. E. y Wilson, T. D. (1977). The halo effect. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 35(4), 250–256.

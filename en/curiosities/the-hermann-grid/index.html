Your perception surprises you

The Hermann grid and its ghost spots

Grey spots appear at the crossings of a white grid on a black background and vanish when you look at them.

In 1870, the physiologist Ludimar Hermann described how, when you look at a grid of white lines on a black background, greyish spots appear at the crossings. Curiously, the spot disappears at the crossing you look at directly and is seen only at those around it.

An explanation that was not enough

For decades it was explained by lateral inhibition in the retina: cells receiving light at the centre of a crossing are surrounded by more white than those in a street, and that surrounding light dampens them. At the centre of gaze, where the cells are smaller, the effect does not occur.

But in 1997 the scintillating grid was described, with white dots at the crossings, in which flickering black dots appear, and it was found that if the lines are drawn wavy the illusion disappears. This does not fit the retinal explanation well and suggests that visual areas of the brain are also involved.

References

  1. Schrauf, M., Lingelbach, B. y Wist, E. R. (1997). The scintillating grid illusion. Vision Research, 37(8), 1033–1038.
  2. Geier, J. et al. (2008). Straightness as the main factor of the Hermann grid illusion. Perception, 37(5), 651–665.

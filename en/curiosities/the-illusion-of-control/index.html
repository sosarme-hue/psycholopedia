Experiments that changed questions

The illusion of control: the ticket you chose

People who chose their lottery number asked four times as much to sell it as those given one at random.

In 1975, the psychologist Ellen Langer sold lottery tickets to office workers. Some were allowed to choose their ticket; others were simply handed one. Days later, before the draw, someone asked them how much they would sell it for.

Choosing does not change luck

Those who had chosen their ticket asked on average about $8.67; those who had been given one, under $2. But the chance of winning was exactly the same. Langer called it the illusion of control: we believe we influence outcomes that depend on chance, especially when there is choice, familiarity or active involvement.

It shows up in people who throw dice harder to get a high number or always play the same numbers. In gambling it can feed problem gambling. And beyond it, it sometimes leads us to underestimate the role of luck in our successes and in other people's failures.

References

  1. Langer, E. J. (1975). The illusion of control. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 32(2), 311–328.

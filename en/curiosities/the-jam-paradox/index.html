What the original study did not say

The jam paradox: does too much choice paralyse?

A famous study with 24 jams suggested that choosing among many options holds us back. Later reviews found an average effect of almost zero.

In 2000, Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper set up a tasting stand in a California supermarket. On some days they offered 24 kinds of jam and on others only 6. The big stand attracted more people, but only 3% of those who stopped bought a jar. At the small one, 30% did.

The "paradox of choice"

The study inspired Barry Schwartz's book The Paradox of Choice (2004) and was cited in marketing, design and politics: fewer options would make us decide more and better.

But in 2010, a meta-analysis of 50 experiments found that, on average, the effect of having many options was practically zero. Sometimes it appeared, sometimes not, and in some cases the opposite happened. Later reviews suggest that overload appears under specific conditions: when options are hard to compare, when we are unsure what we want, or when we are in a hurry.

References

  1. Iyengar, S. S. y Lepper, M. R. (2000). When choice is demotivating. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 79(6), 995–1006.
  2. Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R. y Todd, P. M. (2010). Can there ever be too many options? Journal of Consumer Research, 37(3), 409–425.
  3. Chernev, A., Böckenholt, U. y Goodman, J. (2015). Choice overload: a conceptual review and meta-analysis. Journal of Consumer Psychology, 25(2), 333–358.

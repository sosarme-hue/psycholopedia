Experiments that changed questions

Milgram's lost letters

Stamped letters were left in the street. Whether someone posted them depended on who they were addressed to.

In 1965, Stanley Milgram and colleagues devised a way to measure attitudes without asking. They left hundreds of sealed, stamped, addressed letters on pavements, in phone booths and shops in New Haven, as if someone had lost them. The only thing that changed was the recipient.

Whom do we help?

Letters addressed to a private individual or to a medical research association arrived in about 70% of cases. Those addressed to "Friends of the Communist Party" or "Friends of the Nazi Party" arrived only one time in four.

The lost letter technique allows a discreet measure of how a community views a group or a cause, because people do not know they are taking part in a study. It has been used to study attitudes towards minorities, political parties or social issues, and to compare neighbourhoods or cities.

References

  1. Milgram, S., Mann, L. y Harter, S. (1965). The lost-letter technique: a tool of social research. Public Opinion Quarterly, 29(3), 437–438.

Myths that sound scientific

The Myers-Briggs: popular but unreliable

Millions of people take it at work. But many get a different type if they retake it weeks later.

The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) sorts people into 16 types based on four pairs of opposites, such as extraversion or introversion, and thinking or feeling. It was created in the 1940s by Katharine Cook Briggs and her daughter Isabel Briggs Myers, inspired by Carl Jung's ideas. Neither had training in psychology.

The problems

The main one is reliability: in some studies, about half of people get a different type when retaking the test a few weeks later. It also splits into two categories traits that are actually continuous: most people are near the middle, not at an extreme. And it predicts job performance poorly.

Psychologists prefer models such as the Big Five personality traits, measured as dimensions and with more support. The MBTI can be a way to start conversations about preferences, but it should not be used to select staff or to decide who you are.

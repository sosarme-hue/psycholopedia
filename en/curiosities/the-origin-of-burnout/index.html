The story behind an idea

The origin of burnout

A psychologist working at a free clinic in New York burned out and gave it a name. Today the WHO recognises it as an occupational phenomenon.

In the early 1970s, the psychologist Herbert Freudenberger worked by day in his practice and by night as a volunteer at a free clinic for people with addictions in New York. After a while he felt exhausted, irritable and unmotivated. In 1974 he described this state among the volunteers and called it burnout.

Three dimensions

The psychologist Christina Maslach studied it in depth and in 1981 created a questionnaire to measure it. She defined it by three components: emotional exhaustion, detachment or cynicism towards the work and the people served, and a sense of inefficacy.

In 2019, the World Health Organization included it in its international classification as an occupational phenomenon, not as an illness: the result of chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. That is why solutions depend not only on the individual but also on workload, control and support within the organisation.

References

  1. Freudenberger, H. J. (1974). Staff burn-out. Journal of Social Issues, 30(1), 159–165.
  2. Maslach, C. y Leiter, M. P. (2016). Understanding the burnout experience. World Psychiatry, 15(2), 103–111.

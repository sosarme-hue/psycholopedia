The story behind an idea

The placebo effect: when expectation heals (a little)

A pill with no active ingredient can ease symptoms. The interesting part is when and why.

The word placebo comes from the Latin for "I shall please". In medicine it means a treatment with no specific pharmacological effect that nevertheless brings improvement. During the Second World War, the anaesthetist Henry Beecher saw soldiers find relief from injections of saline, and in 1955 he published The Powerful Placebo.

What we know today

Placebo works mainly on subjective symptoms: pain, nausea, fatigue or anxiety. It does not shrink tumours or lower cholesterol. Part of the improvement credited to it comes from the fact that many symptoms get better on their own.

For pain there are measurable mechanisms: expectation can activate the brain's own opioid systems. Context matters too: colour, price or the clinician's attention change the response. Its opposite, the nocebo effect, explains why reading a list of side effects can make them appear.

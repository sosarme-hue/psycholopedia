Experiments that changed questions

The visual cliff: are babies afraid of heights?

Glass over a fake drop showed that crawling babies perceive depth. And that they look at their mother before crossing.

The psychologist Eleanor Gibson said the idea came to her at the Grand Canyon, worried that her daughter might go near the edge. In 1960, with Richard Walk, she built the visual cliff: a table covered with thick glass. Under one half was a chequered board stuck to the glass; under the other, the same pattern a metre lower, as if there were a drop.

Crawling towards mother

Babies aged 6 to 14 months were placed in the centre and their mother called them from one side or the other. Almost all crossed the "shallow" side, but very few ventured onto what looked like a chasm, even though they could touch the glass.

In 1985 a detail was added: with the drop adjusted to be ambiguous, most babies crossed if their mother smiled, and none did if she looked afraid. This is social referencing: from very early on, babies use their caregivers' expressions to decide whether something is dangerous.

References

  1. Gibson, E. J. y Walk, R. D. (1960). The 'visual cliff'. Scientific American, 202(4), 64–71.
  2. Sorce, J. F. et al. (1985). Maternal emotional signaling: its effect on the visual cliff behavior of 1-year-olds. Developmental Psychology, 21(1), 195–200.

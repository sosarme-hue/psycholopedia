Your perception surprises you

The waterfall illusion

After watching a waterfall for a while, the rocks beside it seem to rise. A Scottish traveller described it in 1834.

In 1834, Robert Addams was gazing at the Falls of Foyers in Scotland. After staring at the falling water for a while, he looked at the rocks beside it and they seemed to be slowly rising. Aristotle had already described something similar when watching a river's current.

Tired neurons

This is the motion aftereffect. The visual system has neurons specialised in detecting movement in particular directions. After watching downward movement for a long time, the neurons for that direction tire. When you then look at something still, those for the opposite direction dominate and you see an upward movement that is not there.

The curious thing is that the rocks seem to move yet do not change position: a contradiction showing that the brain processes motion and position separately. You can check it by watching the rolling credits of a film for a while and then looking at the wall.

References

  1. Addams, R. (1834). An account of a peculiar optical phænomenon seen after having looked at a moving body. London and Edinburgh Philosophical Magazine, 5, 373–374.
  2. Mather, G., Verstraten, F. y Anstis, S. (eds.) (1998). The Motion Aftereffect. MIT Press.

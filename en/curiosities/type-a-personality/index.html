What the original study did not say

Type A personality and the heart

Two cardiologists described impatient, competitive people as more prone to heart attacks. Part of the research was funded by the tobacco industry.

In the 1950s, the cardiologists Meyer Friedman and Ray Rosenman described a behaviour pattern they called Type A: competitive, impatient, always-in-a-hurry and hostile people. According to their studies, they were at greater risk of coronary heart disease than calm, Type B people.

What happened next

The idea became hugely popular. But later, larger studies did not confirm that the Type A pattern as a whole increased risk. What did hold up was a link with one of its components, hostility, and with chronic stress, depression and isolation.

In 2012, research using internal tobacco industry documents showed that the industry had funded much of the research on personality and heart disease, with an interest in presenting stress and personality as alternatives to smoking as a cause. That does not invalidate the science, but it is a reminder of the importance of knowing who pays for studies.

References

  1. Friedman, M. y Rosenman, R. H. (1959). Association of specific overt behavior pattern with blood and cardiovascular findings. JAMA, 169(12), 1286–1296.
  2. Petticrew, M. P., Lee, K. y McKee, M. (2012). Type A behavior pattern and coronary heart disease: Philip Morris's 'crown jewel'. American Journal of Public Health, 102(11), 2018–2025.

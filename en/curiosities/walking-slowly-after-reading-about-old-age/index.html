What the original study did not say

Does reading about old age make you walk more slowly?

A famous 1996 study said yes. When it was repeated with automatic timers, the effect appeared only when the researchers expected it.

In 1996, John Bargh and colleagues asked students to make sentences from lists of words. For some, the lists included words related to old age, such as "Florida", "wrinkle" or "bingo". Afterwards, they discreetly timed how long it took them to walk down the corridor. Those who had read the words walked more slowly.

The 2012 replication

The study became a classic example of priming, or unconscious activation. In 2012, a Belgian team repeated it, timing participants with infrared sensors instead of a hand-held stopwatch. They found no effect. It appeared only when the experimenters knew which group was supposed to walk more slowly and did the timing themselves.

The case became one of the symbols of the replication crisis in psychology. Basic priming, where one word makes related words easier to recognise, is well established. What does not hold up is the claim that it changes complex behaviour so strikingly.

References

  1. Bargh, J. A., Chen, M. y Burrows, L. (1996). Automaticity of social behavior. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 71(2), 230–244.
  2. Doyen, S. et al. (2012). Behavioral priming: it's all in the mind, but whose mind? PLoS ONE, 7(1), e29081.

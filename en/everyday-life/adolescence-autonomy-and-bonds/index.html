Lifespan

Adolescence: autonomy and bonds

More than a rebellious phase, adolescence is a period of intense social learning, with a changing brain and changing sleep.

The popular image of a teenager is someone impulsive, shut away in their room and constantly at odds with their family. Research offers a more nuanced picture: most adolescents keep good relationships with their parents, and many of the changes of this stage serve a purpose.

Gaining autonomy without breaking the bond

In adolescence friends become more important and the search for one's own identity intensifies. Pulling away a little from the family is part of learning to manage as an adult. Studies of parenting styles suggest that combining warmth with clear rules, explained and negotiable according to age, tends to help.

Peers matter a lot

In an experiment by Laurence Steinberg and Margo Gardner, adolescents and adults played a driving video game. With friends watching, adolescents took considerably more risks; in adults the effect was much smaller. It is not that they cannot judge danger: the social context changes the decision.

A brain under construction

The brain keeps maturing well into the twenties. Some systems involved in reward and emotion become very sensitive in early adolescence, while networks for control and planning mature more slowly. It is a useful simplification, as long as it is not used to explain every behaviour.

Arguing is part of growing up

Conflicts with parents increase in early adolescence, mostly over everyday matters such as clothes, curfews or tidiness. They are usually frequent but minor, and are part of negotiating autonomy. Laurence Steinberg's research shows that what protects most is not avoiding conflict but a style that combines warmth, clear and explained rules, and respect for the teenager's opinion.

Mental health in adolescence

About half of mental disorders start before age 18. Some changes are normal, such as wanting more privacy or spending more time with friends, but it is worth seeking advice if sadness or irritability lasting weeks appears, or withdrawal from friends, a sharp drop in school performance, changes in eating, self-harm or substance use. Asking with interest and without judging, and doing so often, opens more doors than a one-off interrogation.

Sleep shifts later

During adolescence the body clock tends to shift later: it is hard to fall asleep early and wake early. Mary Carskadon and other researchers have documented this. Combined with early school start times and screens at night, many adolescents sleep less than they need.

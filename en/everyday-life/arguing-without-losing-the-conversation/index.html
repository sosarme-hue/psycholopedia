Couples

Arguing without losing the conversation

Couples argue. What research looks at is not whether they argue but how they do it and how they repair afterwards.

Every long relationship has disagreements: about money, chores, family, free time or expectations. Many of these issues are never fully resolved, and that is fine. What makes the difference is how they are talked about.

What has been observed

Teams such as John Gottman's have recorded and analysed thousands of couple conversations. Some patterns are linked to more distress over time: opening the conversation with an attack, criticising the person rather than the behaviour, contempt (mockery, sarcasm, signs of superiority), defensiveness and stonewalling. Andrew Christensen and others have described the demand–withdraw pattern: one partner pushes harder and harder and the other pulls further away, each move feeding the other.

The opposite has been observed too: couples who keep talking make repair attempts (a little humour, acknowledging something, asking for a break) and the other person accepts them.

Practical ideas

  • Talk about a specific behaviour and how it affects you ("when you're late without letting me know, I worry") instead of labelling ("you're inconsiderate").
  • Ask for a break when tension rises too far, and agree when you will pick it up again.
  • Summarise what the other person said before you reply.
  • Tell apart the problems that can be solved from those that can only be managed.
Arguing is not the same as being afraid. If a relationship involves threats, control over money or contacts, humiliation or violence, it is not a communication problem. Seek help from specialised services; in Spain you can call 016 (free and does not appear on the bill).

References

  2. Christensen, A. y Heavey, C. L. (1990). Gender and social structure in the demand/withdraw pattern of marital conflict. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 59(1), 73–81.

Loss and transitions

Emigrating: the grief of leaving your country

Moving country means many losses at once. Keeping your own culture while opening up to the new one usually works best.

Emigrating can be an opportunity and, at the same time, one of the most demanding experiences of a lifetime. You leave behind family, friends, language, landscape, food and a way of being in the world, sometimes all at once.

A multiple grief

The psychiatrist Joseba Achotegui speaks of migratory grief with seven losses: family and friends, language, culture, homeland, social status, contact with one's own group and the physical risks of the journey. When conditions are very harsh, such as forced loneliness, fear or lack of papers, he described a picture of intense stress that he called Ulysses syndrome. It is not a mental illness, but an understandable response to an extreme situation.

Ways of adapting

The psychologist John Berry proposed that people arriving in another culture combine two questions: do I keep my culture of origin? and do I engage with the new one? This gives four strategies: integration (both), assimilation (giving up your own), separation (staying only within your own) and marginalisation (neither). In general, integration is linked to better wellbeing, although it also depends on how the host society receives newcomers.

What usually helps

  • Learn the language as soon as possible, without abandoning your own.
  • Find community, both from your country and local.
  • Keep in touch with your people, without living only for news from home.
  • Give yourself time: adaptation takes years and has ups and downs.
  • Seek help if sadness or anxiety settle in.

References

  1. Berry, J. W. (1997). Immigration, acculturation, and adaptation. Applied Psychology, 46(1), 5–34.
  2. Achotegui, J. (2004). Emigrar en situación extrema: el síndrome del inmigrante con estrés crónico y múltiple (síndrome de Ulises). Norte de Salud Mental, 21, 39–52.

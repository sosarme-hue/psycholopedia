Sleep

Why do I have nightmares so often?

Stress and trauma make them more frequent. A simple technique, rewriting the dream while awake, reduces them.

You wake with a racing heart after dreaming that someone is chasing you, that you are falling or that you are late for something important. Occasional nightmares are normal. But when they recur almost every week, they can affect your rest and mood.

Why they happen

Nightmares are more frequent in times of stress, with lack of sleep, with some medications and when giving up alcohol. After a traumatic experience they may replay, more or less faithfully, what happened. Some people also have a more stable tendency to dream intensely.

One idea is that dreams play a part in processing emotions. When that process gets stuck, the same distressing plot can repeat over and over, and fear of the nightmare also makes sleep harder.

Rewriting the dream

Imagery rehearsal therapy is based on a simple idea. During the day, the person chooses a recurring nightmare, writes it down and changes the plot however they like: it can have a different ending, a different setting or an absurd twist. They then mentally rehearse the new version for a few minutes a day.

In 2001, Barry Krakow and colleagues tested it in women who had survived sexual assault and had chronic nightmares. Those who received the treatment greatly reduced how often they had nightmares and improved their sleep and post-traumatic stress symptoms.

Medicines and substances

Some nightmares have a chemical cause. Certain drugs, such as some antidepressants, beta blockers or medicines for Parkinson's, can trigger them or make them more intense, as can suddenly stopping alcohol or some sleeping pills, because REM sleep "rebounds". If nightmares began after a change in treatment, it is worth discussing it with the doctor before stopping any medicine on your own.

Nightmares and night terrors in children

In childhood it helps to tell two things apart. In a nightmare, the child wakes up frightened, remembers the dream and seeks comfort; it usually happens in the second half of the night. In a night terror, which occurs in the first hours, the child screams or thrashes about, seems awake but does not respond, and remembers nothing in the morning. During a night terror it is enough to make sure the child does not get hurt, without trying to wake them. Both usually disappear with age.

What usually helps

  • Regular sleep times and less alcohol.
  • Avoid distressing content just before bed.
  • Try rewriting a recurring nightmare and rehearsing it.
  • Talk about what is worrying you during the day.
If nightmares follow a trauma or stop you sleeping, a mental health professional can help.

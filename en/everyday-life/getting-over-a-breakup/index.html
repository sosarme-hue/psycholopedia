Couples

Getting over a breakup

A breakup really hurts, but most people recover, and some come out with a clearer sense of who they are.

When an important relationship ends, it is normal to feel sadness, anger, anxiety, trouble sleeping or the constant urge to check the other person's social media. A breakup is a loss, even though the person is still alive, and the brain processes it intensely.

Who am I now?

Part of the pain has to do with identity. In a long relationship we share friends, routines, plans and even the way we see ourselves. In 2010, Erica Slotter and colleagues found that after a breakup many people feel they no longer know quite who they are, and that this confusion is associated with more sadness.

The good news is that most people recover with time. A 2007 study found that many people also described positive changes after the breakup: more confidence, independence or clarity about what they want. And in 2015, a study found that reflecting on the breakup in an orderly way, in repeated interviews, helped people regain a sense of identity.

Staying friends?

Many couples set out to stay friends after a break-up. It works better when the relationship had begun as a friendship, when the break-up was mutual and when neither person hopes to get back together. In the first months, frequent contact tends to slow recovery for whoever is hurting more. A period of no contact is not spite but a way of giving attachment room to loosen.

When you keep going back

On-again, off-again relationships, in which a couple breaks up and gets back together several times, are common. René Dailey's studies found that overall they are associated with lower satisfaction, more uncertainty and more conflict than stable relationships. Getting back together can make sense if something important has changed; if it is only out of fear of being alone, the cycle tends to repeat.

What usually helps

  • Allow yourself to grieve, without rushing to "be fine".
  • Limit contact and social-media checking, at least for a while: looking at their profile keeps the wound open.
  • Rebuild your "self": take up your own hobbies, friendships and plans again.
  • Write about what happened, trying to make sense of it, not just going over the pain.
  • Lean on other people, without isolating yourself.
If after several weeks the distress does not ease, stops you leading a normal life or thoughts of self-harm appear, it is worth seeking professional help.

Digital life

I can't stop reading bad news

Endlessly scrolling through distressing news feels like control, but it increases stress. Limiting it helps.

A war, a disaster, a crisis. You open your phone to see what has happened and, an hour later, you are still reading worse and worse news. There is even a word for it: doomscrolling.

Why it is hard to stop

Our brains are primed to pay attention to threats: detecting danger has been useful for survival. Bad news grabs more attention than good news, and the media and social networks know it. On top of that, carrying on reading gives a sense of control: if I am informed, I will be prepared. But rarely does what we read change what we can do.

The cost to mental health

In 2014, Alison Holman and colleagues studied thousands of Americans after the Boston Marathon bombing. People who had spent six hours or more a day following the news in the media had more symptoms of acute stress than some who had been at the scene of the attack. Repeated exposure to shocking images has an effect of its own.

The world is improving more than it seems

The news covers the exceptional and the sudden, not slow changes. That is why many people believe the world is doing worse than the data show. Hans Rosling asked thousands of people in different countries about extreme poverty, vaccination or life expectancy, and most answered worse than if they had guessed at random, almost always out of pessimism. Occasionally checking the news against long-term data helps you see the bigger picture.

Avoiding the news

The Reuters Institute's Digital News Report shows that around four in ten people say they avoid the news sometimes or often, mainly because it affects their mood or makes them feel powerless. You do not have to choose between overload and switching off completely: checking the news once or twice a day from a reliable source, preferring reading to videos and also looking for solutions-focused news lets you stay informed without sinking.

What usually helps

  • Choose specific times to catch up on news, once or twice a day.
  • Prefer text to videos with shocking images.
  • Use reliable sources and avoid the continuous stream of social media.
  • Turn off breaking-news alerts.
  • Take action if you can: donating, helping or talking about it reduces helplessness.
  • Do not read the news in bed.

Sexuality

I don't feel like having sex: is something wrong with me?

Desire does not always strike out of the blue. In many people it appears after things begin, in response to intimacy.

It is one of the most common questions about sexuality: "I don't feel like it the way I used to." Many people experience this with guilt or worry, especially in long relationships. It helps to know that desire works in more than one way.

Spontaneous and responsive desire

For a long time, sexual response was described as a fixed sequence: first desire appears, then arousal and then orgasm. In 2000, the physician Rosemary Basson proposed a different model, based on her experience with women in long relationships.

According to Basson, in many people desire does not appear spontaneously but as a response: they start from an openness, out of a wish for intimacy or closeness, and desire emerges afterwards, once touching begins and they feel aroused. This responsive desire is normal and common in stable relationships. It is not second-class desire.

What dampens it

Desire suffers with stress, tiredness, unresolved conflict, routine, low self-esteem, some medications (such as certain antidepressants) and hormonal changes such as after childbirth or the menopause.

What usually helps

  • Do not wait for spontaneous urges to seek intimacy, if you want closeness.
  • Create contexts that make arousal easier: time, rest, unhurried intimacy.
  • Talk as a couple about what you enjoy, without blame.
  • Look at stress and underlying conflicts.
If lack of desire distresses you, mention it to your doctor: there are sometimes physical or medication-related causes, and sex therapy is effective.

References

  1. Basson, R. (2000). The female sexual response: a different model. Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy, 26(1), 51–65.
  2. Meana, M. (2010). Elucidating women's (hetero)sexual desire: definitional challenges and content expansion. Journal of Sex Research, 47(2–3), 104–122.

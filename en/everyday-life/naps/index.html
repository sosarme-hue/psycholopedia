Sleep

Are naps good for you?

A short nap improves alertness and mood. Long ones can leave you groggy and steal sleep at night.

The siesta has a reputation as a Spanish custom, but it is practised in many places and throughout history. And there is a biological reason: in the early afternoon, the internal clock produces a small dip in alertness, whether or not you have eaten.

Short beats long

In 2006, Amber Brooks and Leon Lack compared naps of 5, 10, 20 and 30 minutes in people who had slept little. The 10-minute nap improved alertness and performance fastest, and its effects lasted more than two hours. The 30-minute nap also helped, but was followed by a period of grogginess.

That grogginess is called sleep inertia. It appears when we wake from deep sleep, which we reach after about 20 or 30 minutes. That is why an hour-long nap can leave us worse off than before.

When it may not suit you

In people with insomnia, a long or late nap reduces sleep pressure and can make it harder to fall asleep at night. And in older people, needing very long and frequent naps sometimes reflects poor-quality night-time sleep, which is worth checking.

Naps and the heart

A Greek study of more than 23,000 adults, led by Androniki Naska, found that those who napped regularly had lower coronary mortality, especially working men. More recently, a Swiss study found fewer cardiovascular events in people who took one or two naps a week. These are observational studies, so they do not prove that napping is the cause, but they suggest an occasional nap need not be harmful.

Coffee and a nap when driving

Jim Horne and Louise Reyner studied sleepy drivers in a simulator. The most effective combination for reducing sleepiness was having a coffee and then taking a nap of about fifteen minutes: caffeine takes about twenty minutes to take effect, so it kicks in just as you wake up. On long journeys it is advisable to stop every two hours, and when sleepy at the wheel the only safe solution is to stop and rest.

What usually helps

  • Between 10 and 20 minutes, with an alarm.
  • Early in the afternoon, not after five or so.
  • Sitting or reclining, rather than in bed if you find it hard to wake up.
  • A "coffee nap": drink a coffee just before a short nap, because caffeine starts to work as you wake.

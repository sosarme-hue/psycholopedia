Habits

Why do New Year's resolutions fail?

It is not a lack of willpower. The way a goal is framed has a big influence on whether it is met.

Every January, millions of people resolve to exercise more, eat better, stop smoking or save money. By the end of February many have already given up. Do we simply lack willpower?

What the studies say

Not everyone fails. In a classic 2002 study by John Norcross, about half of those who made resolutions were still keeping them six months later, a higher proportion than among people with the same goals who did not frame them as resolutions.

In 2020, a Swedish study with more than 1,000 participants found that resolutions framed as approaching something ("start walking every day") were kept more than those framed as avoiding something ("stop eating sweets"): about 59% versus 47% after a year. Those who received some support and guidance on how to frame them also did better.

The "fresh start" effect

New Year, a birthday or even a Monday act as temporal landmarks that encourage us to change. Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis called this the fresh start effect: internet searches for the word "diet" and gym visits rise on those dates. They are good moments to begin, as long as the resolution comes with a concrete plan.

Approach goals

A 2020 Swedish study of more than a thousand people found that those who framed their resolutions as something to achieve ("start walking half an hour a day") were more successful a year later than those who framed them as something to avoid ("stop eating sweets"). People who received moderate support and advice on overcoming obstacles also did better.

What usually helps

  • Frame the goal positively and concretely: what you will do, when and where.
  • Start small: five minutes of daily exercise beats an hour that never happens.
  • Tie it to an existing routine, such as "after my coffee, I go for a walk".
  • Keep a simple record of progress.
  • Do not give up after a slip. Missing a day does not ruin a habit; what matters is getting back to it.
  • Get support: share the goal or do it with someone.

Digital life

Our attention in a world of screens

Switching tasks has a cost. What is known about interruptions, notifications and multitasking, and what is still debated.

You answer a message while writing an email, glance at a notification in the middle of a meeting, go back to your document and need a moment to remember where you were. That feeling has a name and has been measured.

The cost of switching

Laboratory experiments show that when we alternate between two tasks we are slower and make more mistakes than when we do each one separately. This is the task-switching cost. What we call multitasking is usually, in fact, jumping quickly from one thing to another.

In studies of office workers, Gloria Mark and her team found that frequent interruptions were linked to more stress and pressure, although people sometimes compensated by working faster. Another experiment found that receiving a notification, even without looking at it, worsened performance on an attention task.

What is still debated

Some striking findings, such as the claim that a phone merely lying on the table reduces available mental capacity, have not been consistently replicated. And the effect of social media on wellbeing seems to depend a great deal on the kind of use, the person and their age: studies find associations, generally small, and argue about the direction of cause.

Practical ideas

  • Batch messages at set times instead of replying instantly.
  • Turn off non-urgent notifications during tasks that need concentration.
  • When you interrupt a task, jot down the next step so it is easier to pick up again.

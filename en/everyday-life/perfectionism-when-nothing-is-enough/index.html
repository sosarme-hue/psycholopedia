Self-esteem and identity

Perfectionism: when nothing is ever enough

Wanting to do things well is good. Demanding perfection and punishing yourself for every slip has costs.

You check an email five times before sending it, you do not start a project because you lack time to make it perfect, or a B feels like a fail. Perfectionism is often presented as a virtue, but it can become a burden.

A rising trait

In 2019, Thomas Curran and Andrew Hill analysed data from more than 40,000 university students in the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom between 1989 and 2016. All three forms of perfectionism they measured had increased, especially socially prescribed perfectionism: the feeling that others expect us to be perfect.

Researchers distinguish between having high standards, which can be useful, and perfectionistic concerns: intense fear of making mistakes, harsh self-criticism and a sense that nothing is ever enough. The second form is associated with anxiety, depression, eating disorders and burnout. It can also lead to procrastination: if I cannot do it perfectly, better not to start.

Two faces of perfectionism

Researchers distinguish between high standards, wanting to do things well, and perfectionistic concerns, intense fear of mistakes, harsh self-criticism and the feeling that it is never enough. The first can go with good performance; the second is linked to anxiety, depression, eating disorders, insomnia and burnout. The problem is not aiming high but making one's worth depend on getting there.

Experiments with mistakes

One cognitive behavioural therapy technique for perfectionism is to run small "experiments": sending an email with a minor typo, handing in work that is good but not perfect, leaving something unfinished for a day. Afterwards you look at what actually happened and compare it with what you feared. The consequences are almost always much smaller than expected, and the experience teaches more than any argument.

What usually helps

  • Define what "good enough" means for each task before you start.
  • Put time limits on checking.
  • Run small experiments: send something with a minor error and see what happens. Almost always, nothing serious.
  • Value progress, not just the end result.
When perfectionism causes a lot of suffering, specific cognitive behavioural therapy has shown good results.

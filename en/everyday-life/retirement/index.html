Loss and transitions

Retirement: rest or emptiness?

Most people adapt well, but not everyone equally. Those who retire with plans and relationships fare better.

For years you dream of it: free time, travel, grandchildren, hobbies. But when it arrives, some people feel lost. Without work, a routine, a role and many daily contacts disappear.

Not everyone experiences it the same way

In 2007, the psychologist Mo Wang followed thousands of American retirees for years. She found there was no single pattern. Around 70% maintained stable wellbeing through the transition. A quarter experienced a dip at first and then recovered. And a small group clearly improved, often because they were leaving a stressful job.

It went worse for those who retired involuntarily or unexpectedly, through redundancy or illness, those with financial problems and those in a difficult relationship. It went better for those with good health, plans and an active social network.

More than free time

Work provides structure, purpose, recognition and relationships. People who retire need to find other sources of all that: volunteering, study, a serious hobby, caring for others or part-time work.

Helping others feels good

Volunteering is one of the activities that fits best after retirement: it provides structure, social contact and a sense of being useful. A meta-analysis by Morris Okun found that older people who volunteered had a lower risk of mortality, even taking their previous health into account. The benefits seem greater when it is done for altruistic reasons and with moderate commitment, without becoming a new exhausting obligation.

The couple after work

Retirement also changes life as a couple: more hours together, new divisions of chores and sometimes different rhythms. Phyllis Moen found that marital conflict was more frequent at the stage when one partner had retired and the other was still working, especially when the man retired first. Talking beforehand about expectations, personal space and the division of housework helps avoid friction.

What usually helps

  • Prepare for retirement before it arrives, personally and not just financially.
  • Keep a routine, with set times and activities.
  • Look after friendships outside work and make new ones.
  • Find something meaningful that gives you reasons to get up.
  • Talk as a couple about how time and space at home will be shared.

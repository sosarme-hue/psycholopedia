Study

Study more or study better?

Rereading and highlighting feel effective but are not very. Two strategies with strong support: testing yourself and spacing your study.

Most students revise by rereading and highlighting. It is understandable: when you reread a text it feels familiar and you seem to know it already. But that feeling of fluency is misleading.

What the research says

In 2013 a team led by John Dunlosky reviewed ten common study techniques. Two stood out for their effectiveness across ages, subjects and kinds of exam:

  • Testing yourself (retrieval practice): trying to remember without looking, asking questions, using flashcards or explaining the topic aloud. Each time we pull something from memory, we strengthen it.
  • Spacing your study (distributed practice): several sessions days apart work better than one long one, even with the same total time.

Other techniques, such as self-explanation (asking yourself why something is so) or mixing different types of problems, were rated as moderately useful. Rereading and highlighting were among the least useful when used on their own.

The rereading trap

Rereading notes and highlighting are the techniques students use most and among the least effective. They give a feeling of mastery because the text feels familiar, but recognising something is not the same as being able to recall it unaided. In 2013, John Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed ten study techniques and only two received the top rating: practice testing and spacing sessions out.

Mix it up and explain

Two other well-supported strategies are interleaved practice, mixing different types of problems instead of doing many of the same kind in a row, and self-explanation, asking yourself why what you read is true or how it relates to what you already know. Both take more effort and feel less productive at the time, but they leave more lasting learning. Robert Bjork calls them "desirable difficulties".

How to apply it

  1. After reading a section, close the book and write down what you remember.
  2. Compare with the text and note what was missing.
  3. Come back to the topic two or three days later, and again the following week.
  4. Sleep: sleep helps consolidate what you have learned.

Testing yourself takes more effort than rereading, so it feels like slower progress. That difficulty is exactly what makes it work.

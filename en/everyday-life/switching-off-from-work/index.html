Work

Why is it hard to switch off from work?

Thinking about work after you get home prevents recovery. Switching off is not laziness: it is part of performance.

You get home, but your head is still at the office: the unanswered email, tomorrow's meeting, what your boss said. You answer work messages on the sofa, and by Sunday afternoon you are already thinking about Monday.

Why switching off matters

The work psychologist Sabine Sonnentag has spent years studying recovery after the working day. One of her most solid findings is the importance of psychological detachment: stopping thinking about work outside work. People who manage it have less exhaustion, better mood and sleep better, and perform better the next day.

The paradox is that the more stress there is at work, the harder it is to switch off, just when it is most needed. That is why researchers speak of a spiral: stress prevents recovery, and lack of recovery makes stress weigh more heavily.

A right

In Spain, the 2018 Data Protection Act recognises a right to digital disconnection: employees are entitled not to respond to communications outside working hours. Several other countries have similar rules.

What usually helps

  • Close the day: noting outstanding tasks before leaving helps you "let them go".
  • Silence work notifications outside working hours.
  • Do something absorbing: sport, cooking, music or meeting friends.
  • Create transition rituals, such as changing clothes or going for a walk.
  • Talk to the organisation if the workload makes switching off impossible.

References

  1. Sonnentag, S. y Fritz, C. (2015). Recovery from job stress: the stressor-detachment model as an integrative framework. Journal of Organizational Behavior, 36(S1), S72–S103.
  2. Ley Orgánica 3/2018, de Protección de Datos Personales y garantía de los derechos digitales, artículo 88.

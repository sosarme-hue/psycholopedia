Sexuality

How to talk to your children about sex

Early, little by little and naturally is best. Talking about it does not bring sex forward; it makes it safer.

Many mothers and fathers dread "the talk" about sex: they do not know when to start, what to say or how to answer awkward questions. Sometimes they wait so long that, by the time it happens, the children have already found out by other, often unreliable, means.

Better an ongoing conversation

Sex education is not a single talk but many conversations over the years, adapted to age. Young children can be taught the correct names for body parts, that their body belongs to them and that some secrets should not be kept. Later come the changes of puberty, relationships, consent, contraception or pornography.

What the evidence says

In 2018, UNESCO reviewed comprehensive sexuality education programmes around the world. It concluded that they do not bring forward the start of sexual activity or increase its frequency. On the contrary, they often delay first sex and encourage the use of condoms and contraceptives. They also help children recognise abuse and seek help.

Pornography arrives earlier

A 2020 Save the Children report found that Spanish adolescents start watching pornography at around age 12 on average, almost always on their phones and often without looking for it. Many use it as a source of information about sex. The problem is that it usually shows relationships without affection, without condoms and sometimes violent or without consent. Talking about it without being shocked, explaining that it is commercial fiction and not a model of what relationships are like, is part of sex education today.

Images, phones and consent

A meta-analysis by Sheri Madigan estimated that around one in seven adolescents has sent sexual images and one in four has received them. Young people should know that forwarding someone else's intimate image without permission causes harm and can be a crime, that nobody should pressure them to send photos, and that they can tell a trusted adult if something makes them uncomfortable, without fear of losing their phone.

What usually helps

  • Use everyday opportunities: a scene in a series, a pregnancy in the family, a news story.
  • Answer what they ask, in simple words and without giving more than they ask for.
  • Say "I don't know, let's look it up together" when needed.
  • Talk about feelings, respect and consent too, not just biology.
  • Keep the door open: what matters is that they know they can ask without being judged.

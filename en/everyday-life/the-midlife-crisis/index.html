Lifespan

Does the midlife crisis exist?

Wellbeing seems to dip somewhat in midlife, but the dramatic crisis of the cliché is uncommon.

The sports car, the radical change of life, the feeling that time is running out. The midlife crisis is a cliché of film and literature. How much truth is there in it?

Where the idea comes from

The psychoanalyst Elliott Jaques coined the phrase in 1965, after studying the lives of artists who changed direction or went into crisis around the age of 35 or 40. According to him, midlife brings awareness of one's own death and forces a rethink of life.

What the data say

In 2008, the economists David Blanchflower and Andrew Oswald analysed surveys of half a million people from dozens of countries. They found that wellbeing followed a U-shaped curve: higher in youth and old age, and lowest around 45 to 50. This pattern has been repeated in many countries, although the dip is small.

Other studies qualify the idea. In 2020, Nancy Galambos and colleagues reviewed longitudinal studies that followed the same people over time, and did not always find the U. And the dramatic crisis, with a break-up and total change of life, is experienced only by a minority, often triggered by specific events such as divorce, illness or redundancy rather than by age itself.

Why this stage can be hard

Midlife piles up demands: children, elderly parents, work, a mortgage. It is also a time of taking stock of what was expected and what one has. But many people say that in midlife they feel more confident and wiser than ever.

Leaving something for those who follow

Erik Erikson described midlife as the stage of generativity: the wish to care for, teach and leave something valuable to the next generations, through children, work, the community or creative work. Dan McAdams developed ways of measuring it and found that more generative people tend to have greater well-being and sense of purpose. Many "crises" at this stage are resolved not by changing everything but by redirecting energy towards what one wants to pass on.

Even apes

A study by Alexander Weiss, with Andrew Oswald among the authors, asked zookeepers in several countries to rate the well-being of more than 500 chimpanzees and orangutans. The result showed a U-shaped curve similar to the human one, with a low point at the age equivalent to our midlife. If confirmed, it suggests that part of the dip at this stage may have biological roots and not just be down to mortgages, children or careers.

What usually helps

  • Review your expectations without comparing yourself to what you "should" have achieved.
  • Find meaning in what you already have and in new, realistic projects.
  • Look after your health, your relationship and your friendships.
  • Seek help if the unease turns into persistent sadness.

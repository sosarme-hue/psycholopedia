Digital life

Video games: when should you worry?

Playing a lot is not an addiction. The worry is losing control and the game crowding out everything else.

Your child spends the afternoon on the console, or you are the one who cannot put down the game on your phone. Is it a problem or just another hobby?

What the WHO classification says

Since 2019, the WHO's international classification of diseases (ICD-11) has included gaming disorder. But it is not defined by hours of play; it is defined by three features lasting at least a year:

  • Impaired control over when, how much or how one plays.
  • Increasing priority given to gaming over other interests and daily activities.
  • Continuing to play despite negative consequences, with significant impairment in personal, family, school or work life.

How many people have it?

In 2021, a meta-analysis by Michelle Stevens and colleagues estimated that it affects about 3% of gamers, more among adolescent boys. The vast majority of people who play, even a lot, do not have a disorder. Problematic gaming is also frequently linked to anxiety, depression or ADHD, which are sometimes the underlying cause.

Video games also have positive sides: shared leisure, challenges, creativity and, in some cases, improved visual and spatial skills.

Loot boxes

Many games sell "loot boxes": packages bought with real money that contain random items. Their mechanics resemble gambling: you pay without knowing what you will get and sometimes win a rare prize. David Zendle and Paul Cairns found that the more players spent on them, the more symptoms of problem gambling they had. It is worth reviewing the payment settings on phones and consoles and talking to children about how they work.

They have benefits too

Video games are not only a risk. C. Shawn Green and Daphne Bavelier found that action game players showed better visual attention than non-players, and that training non-players with these games improved that ability. A review by Isabela Granic also describes benefits in problem solving, managing frustration and social relationships in cooperative games. What matters is the balance with sleep, study and other activities.

What usually helps

  • Take an interest in the game: ask, play together, understand what appeals.
  • Agree clear rules about time and place, rather than banning.
  • Keep an eye on sleep and schoolwork, which are the first warning signs.
  • Offer attractive alternatives, especially activities with other people.
  • Beware of in-game purchases and loot boxes, which resemble gambling.
If gaming is seriously damaging daily life, see a doctor or psychologist.

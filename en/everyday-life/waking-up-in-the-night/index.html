Sleep

I wake up in the night and can't get back to sleep

Waking at night is normal. What prolongs wakefulness is worrying about not sleeping.

It is half past three. You have woken up and your mind starts racing: work, a conversation you need to have, counting the hours left until the alarm. The harder you try to fall asleep, the more awake you are.

Waking up is normal

Sleep is organised in cycles of about 90 minutes, and between cycles it is common to wake briefly. Most of those awakenings are not remembered. With age they become more frequent and somewhat longer.

The historian Roger Ekirch documented in 2001 that in pre-industrial Europe it was common to speak of a "first sleep" and a "second sleep", separated by a period of wakefulness in the middle of the night when people prayed, talked or thought. Waking in the middle of the night is not necessarily a fault.

Why it is hard to get back to sleep

What turns an awakening into an hour lying awake is usually arousal: worrying about the next day, looking at the clock, getting angry about not sleeping. That arousal pushes sleep away. It is one of the mechanisms that maintain insomnia, according to the 2017 European guidelines.

Don't look at the clock

When you wake at night it is tempting to check the time and work out how long is left. In an experiment by Nicole Tang, people with insomnia who were asked to watch a clock next to the bed worried more and took longer to fall asleep than those who could not see it. Turning the clock round, keeping your phone out of reach and not doing the sums reduces that spiral.

Alcohol is deceptive

A drink at night may help you fall asleep sooner, but it disrupts the second half of the night. As the body clears the alcohol, sleep becomes lighter, REM sleep is reduced and awakenings increase. A review by Irshaad Ebrahim confirmed this pattern even at moderate doses. It is also worth seeing a doctor if early waking comes with sadness or loss of interest, which may be a sign of depression, or with loud snoring and pauses in breathing, which may point to sleep apnoea.

What usually helps

  • Do not check the time: turn the clock round and keep your phone away.
  • If about 20 minutes pass, get up, go to another dimly lit room and do something calm until sleepiness returns.
  • Park your worries: writing them down before bed, or at that moment, helps you let go of them.
  • Do not compensate by staying in bed much longer in the morning: keeping your getting-up time consolidates sleep.
If you wake very early almost every day, with low mood and unable to get back to sleep, mention it to your doctor: it can be a symptom of depression.

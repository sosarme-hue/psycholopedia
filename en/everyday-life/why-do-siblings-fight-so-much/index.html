Family

Why do siblings fight so much?

Sibling fights are normal and can teach negotiation. What worsens them most is the sense of unequal treatment.

They argue over the TV remote, who sits in the front of the car or who started it. For many families, sibling fights are one of the biggest daily drains.

What is normal and what is not

The sibling relationship is often the longest in life, and in childhood it mixes affection, rivalry and lots of time together. Conflicts are frequent, especially when siblings are close in age. That is not necessarily bad: arguing with a sibling is practice in negotiating, standing your ground and making up.

What does worsen the relationship, according to many studies reviewed by Susan McHale and colleagues, is differential treatment: when children perceive that one gets more attention, affection or privileges without a reason they understand. The point is not to treat them identically in everything, but for them to see the treatment as fair given their age and needs.

Being treated differently

Children pay close attention to how they are treated compared with their siblings. Studies by Judy Dunn and Susan McHale show that perceiving that parents favour another sibling is associated with worse sibling relationships and more emotional problems, including in adulthood. The point is not to treat everyone exactly the same, since they have different ages and needs, but for different treatment to be explained and seen as fair.

Siblings who care

The sibling relationship is also a source of support. Having a sibling one gets on with protects against the effects of family conflict and bullying, and in old age siblings often become important companions. Encouraging siblings to cooperate on shared tasks or games, and not just separating them when they argue, helps build that bond.

What usually helps

  • Do not act as judge in every fight. If there is no risk, it may be better to let them sort it out or help them negotiate.
  • Teach conflict resolution: listening to the other, saying what you want and looking for options.
  • Avoid comparisons such as "look how well your brother does it".
  • Spend time alone with each child.
  • Always step in if there is physical aggression, humiliation or a clear imbalance of power: that is no longer a normal fight.

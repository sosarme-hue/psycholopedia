Emotions

Why do we keep going over a worry?

Worrying can help us anticipate problems, but it can also become a loop that solves nothing.

To worry is to think about something that might go wrong. Up to a point it is useful: it helps us foresee, prepare and take precautions. The trouble starts when the worry repeats over and over without reaching any decision.

Worry and rumination

Psychology distinguishes two kinds of repetitive thinking. Worry looks to the future ("what if I get fired?"). Rumination, studied by Susan Nolen-Hoeksema, looks to the past or to one's own distress ("why does this happen to me?"). Both have something in common: they feel like doing something, but they seldom lead to a plan.

Thomas Borkovec proposed that worry can work as a form of avoidance: thinking about a problem in words briefly reduces the emotional arousal that picturing it in detail would cause. In the short term it relieves; in the long term it keeps the cycle going.

Some useful questions

  • Can this be solved now? If so, the next step is to make the solution concrete. If not, going over it will not bring it closer.
  • Am I solving or circling? If nothing new has come up after ten minutes, it is probably the latter.
  • Postponing worry: setting aside a fixed time of day to think about it and, outside that time, jotting it down and going back to what you were doing. The technique has some support, though it does not work equally for everyone.
  • Writing down what worries you helps some people organise their thoughts and separate the probable from the merely possible.
If worry takes up much of the day for months, is hard to control and comes with tension, tiredness or sleep problems, it may be an anxiety disorder, which has effective treatments. Talk to your doctor or a psychology professional.

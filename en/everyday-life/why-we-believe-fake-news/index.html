Culture and society

Why do we believe fake news?

More through inattention than gullibility. Stopping to consider whether something is true before sharing greatly reduces its spread.

A message in the family group chat, an outrageous video or an incredible headline. We share it and, hours later, it turns out to be false. Why do we fall for it?

Lies travel further

In 2018, Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral analysed some 126,000 rumours spread on Twitter over more than ten years. False news travelled further and faster than true news, especially political news. And not because of bots: people were sharing them, probably because falsehoods are more novel and provoke more surprise or outrage.

Inattention, not just ideology

It is often thought that we believe fake news that fits our views. That plays a part, but Gordon Pennycook and David Rand showed in 2019 that people who reason more, whatever their ideology, are better at telling true from false headlines. The problem is often lack of attention: we share without stopping to think whether it is true.

Inoculating against fake news

In 2022, Jon Roozenbeek and colleagues tested short videos that taught the most common manipulation techniques, such as emotional language or false dilemmas. They worked like a psychological vaccine: people who watched them recognised those techniques better afterwards.

What usually helps

  • Pause before sharing and ask yourself: is it true?
  • Be wary of anything that causes a lot of outrage or seems too good to be true.
  • Look for the original source and check the date.
  • Consult fact-checkers.
  • Correct respectfully in group chats, without humiliating anyone.

References

  1. Vosoughi, S., Roy, D. y Aral, S. (2018). The spread of true and false news online. Science, 359(6380), 1146–1151.
  2. Pennycook, G. y Rand, D. G. (2019). Lazy, not biased: susceptibility to partisan fake news is better explained by lack of reasoning than by motivated reasoning. Cognition, 188, 39–50.
  3. Roozenbeek, J. et al. (2022). Psychological inoculation improves resilience against misinformation on social media. Science Advances, 8(34), eabo6254.

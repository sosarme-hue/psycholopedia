Ageing

What changes and what stays the same in the mind over the years, and what helps us age well.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Ageing is not only about loss. Some abilities, such as speed of processing or memory for new information, decline over the years. Others, such as vocabulary, knowledge and emotion regulation, hold steady or even improve. Most older people do not have dementia or depression, and many say they feel more satisfied than in midlife.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

Two kinds of intelligence

Raymond Cattell and John Horn distinguished fluid intelligence (solving new problems, which peaks in youth and then declines slowly) from crystallised intelligence (knowledge and experience, which keeps growing into old age). The Seattle Longitudinal Study, led by K. Warner Schaie, showed that most abilities remain fairly stable into the sixties.

The paradox of ageing

Laura Carstensen proposed socioemotional selectivity theory: when we perceive our remaining time as limited, we prioritise close relationships and experiences that make us feel good. This helps explain why older people tend to remember the positive more and have fewer negative emotions day to day.

What helps us age well

  • Physical activity: one of the best-supported factors for protecting memory and mood.
  • Relationships: unwanted loneliness is associated with worse physical and mental health.
  • Hearing: treating hearing loss is one of the modifiable risk factors for dementia.
  • Attitudes: Becca Levy found that people with a more positive view of old age lived on average 7.5 years longer.

Retirement and purpose

Retirement is a major change. For many it brings relief and greater wellbeing; for others, the loss of routine, contacts and meaning. Keeping up meaningful activities, such as volunteering, caring for grandchildren or learning something new, is associated with better physical and mental health in old age.

Ageism

Age discrimination is common and often invisible: jokes, assumptions of incapacity or patronising treatment. In 2021 the WHO published a global report on ageism linking it to poorer health and shorter life expectancy.

Loneliness and isolation

Loneliness (feeling alone) and social isolation (having few contacts) are not the same, but both affect health. A 2010 meta-analysis of nearly 150 studies found that people with stronger social relationships had about a 50% greater likelihood of survival during follow-up, an effect comparable to quitting smoking. In later life, loss of a partner, retirement, reduced mobility and deafness increase the risk of isolation.

Caring for the carer

Much of the care for dependent older people is provided by relatives, mostly women. Caring can bring meaning and closeness, but when it is intense and prolonged it is associated with more anxiety, depression, sleep problems and poorer physical health. Caregiver burden can be measured, for example with the Zarit scale. Information about the illness, support groups, day centres and respite breaks all help.

Sexuality in later life

Sexuality does not disappear with age. In a 2007 US national survey, more than half of people aged 65 to 74 and a quarter of those aged 75 to 85 were still sexually active. The most common problems were related to health, medication or lack of a partner, and few had discussed them with their doctor. Asking about it naturally is part of good care.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

Dementia: what can be prevented

In 2024 The Lancet commission on dementia estimated that about 45% of cases worldwide could be delayed or prevented by acting on 14 risk factors across the lifespan, including childhood education, hearing loss, hypertension, smoking, depression, social isolation, physical inactivity and air pollution. These are population estimates: they guarantee nothing to any one person, but they show where action is worthwhile.

Cognitive reserve

David Snowdon's Nun Study, which followed hundreds of American nuns and examined their brains after death, found that some had Alzheimer's lesions without having shown symptoms in life. The idea of cognitive reserve, developed by Yaakov Stern, proposes that education and a mentally active life help the brain compensate for damage.

"Brain training"

Commercial brain-training games improve the task being practised, but there is little evidence that they transfer to daily life. In 2016 the US Federal Trade Commission fined a company in the sector for misleading advertising.

Depression in later life

Depression is not a normal part of ageing, but it is sometimes overlooked because it is mistaken for tiredness, physical complaints or grief. The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) was designed to detect it in older people. Treatment works as well as at other ages, and social and physical activity help prevent it.

SuperAgers

A team at Northwestern University studies "SuperAgers": people over 80 with memory similar to people 20 or 30 years younger. Their brains show less cortical thinning and more neurons of a particular type in areas linked to attention and emotion. As for their lives, what stands out most is that they keep rich social relationships. There is no recipe, but these cases show that decline is not inevitable for everyone.

"Blue zones", with caution

"Blue zones" have become popular: places such as Sardinia, Okinawa or Ikaria where many people supposedly live past one hundred, with their longevity attributed to diet and lifestyle. Some researchers have pointed out that part of these records may be due to errors in birth registration. The habits proposed are reasonable, but the evidence that they explain such extreme longevity is weaker than is usually told.

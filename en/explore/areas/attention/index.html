Attention

Why we cannot process everything at once: selection, distraction, sustained concentration and what happens when we do two things at once.

Level 1 · Short introduction

At every moment far more information reaches us than we can process. Attention is the set of processes that select a part of it and leave the rest in the background. It lets us concentrate, sustain effort and switch tasks, but it has clear limits: when we try to do two demanding things at once, at least one suffers.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

Kinds of attention

  • Selective: focusing on something and ignoring the rest, like following a conversation at a noisy party (the "cocktail party effect").
  • Sustained: keeping concentration over time, like a guard watching a screen.
  • Divided: sharing it among several tasks, which only works well when at least one is highly automatic.
  • Executive control: deciding what to attend to, resisting distraction and shifting focus.

What we miss

In a famous 1999 experiment, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris asked participants to count the passes made by one basketball team. About half failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene. This is inattentional blindness: what we do not attend to can go completely unnoticed.

Multitasking

What we call multitasking is usually rapid switching between tasks, and every switch costs time and errors. That is why talking on the phone while driving, even hands-free, impairs driving.

Attention and daily life

Sleep, stress, interest in the task and interruptions strongly influence attention. ADHD is a neurodevelopmental disorder involving persistent difficulties with attention, hyperactivity or impulsivity that interfere with daily life.

Attention and control

Attention does not just select: it also lets us hold on to a goal and suppress automatic responses. Michael Posner proposed three attentional networks in the brain: alerting (being ready), orienting (directing attention to a location) and executive (resolving conflicts, as in the Stroop effect). Each can fail in different ways.

Mind-wandering

We spend much of the day thinking about something other than what we are doing. In a 2010 study, Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert used phones to ask thousands of people at random moments: almost half the time their minds were elsewhere, and at those moments they felt, on average, somewhat worse. Mind-wandering is not always bad: it is also related to planning and creativity.

Attention and fatigue

Sustaining concentration for a long time is hard. In vigilance tasks, such as monitoring a radar, performance begins to drop within minutes. Short breaks, good sleep and fewer interruptions help more than trying to force concentration.

The cocktail party effect

At a party we can follow one conversation and ignore the rest, yet if someone says our name across the room, we hear it. Colin Cherry studied this in 1953 with dichotic listening: a different message reached each ear and people had to repeat one of them. They remembered almost nothing of the ignored message, not even whether it switched language, but they did notice if the voice changed from male to female. These experiments opened the debate about when unattended information is filtered out.

Talking on the phone while driving

Driving feels automatic but it demands attention. In simulator experiments, David Strayer's team found that talking on a mobile phone while driving slows reactions to a degree comparable to driving at the US legal alcohol limit, and that hands-free does not remove the problem: what distracts is not just the hands but the conversation. Talking with a passenger is less distracting, because the person beside you sees the road and goes quiet when traffic gets tricky.

ADHD: when attention takes more effort

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder affects around 5% of children and a smaller proportion of adults. It is not a general lack of attention: many people with ADHD can focus for hours on what interests them. What mainly fails is regulation: keeping attention on unstimulating tasks, holding back impulses and getting organised. Diagnosis requires symptoms that began in childhood, appear in several settings and cause real difficulties.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

William James wrote in 1890 that "everyone knows what attention is". Half a century later, Donald Broadbent (1958) proposed an early filter that would let only one channel of information through. It soon became clear the filter was not so strict: we hear our own name even when not attending to that conversation. Anne Treisman proposed a filter that attenuates, and other authors a later selection.

Treisman's feature integration theory (1980) explained why finding a red object among green ones is immediate, while searching for a combination of features requires checking items one by one. Michael Posner distinguished attentional networks for alerting, orienting and executive control, with different brain bases. The Stroop effect (1935), naming the ink colour of the word "RED" printed in blue, remains one of the most widely used tests of attentional control.

Open debates: whether attention is a single limited resource or several; what real effect phones and social media have on our ability to concentrate; and whether cognitive training or meditation improves attention beyond the trained tasks.

The bottleneck

In 1958 Donald Broadbent proposed an early filter: only part of the information is processed in depth. Anne Treisman refined it: unattended information is attenuated but not lost, which is why we hear our name at a noisy party. Later, Treisman proposed feature integration theory (1980): attention is the "glue" that binds colour, shape and position into a single object.

Can it be trained?

Action video games improve some visual and attentional abilities, according to studies by Daphne Bavelier, although the size of the effect is debated. Mindfulness interventions show modest improvements. What lacks support is the idea that a few minutes a day of a commercial game will change attention in daily life.

Three attention networks

Michael Posner proposed that attention depends on three relatively independent brain networks: alerting, which keeps a state of readiness; orienting, which directs attention to a place or stimulus; and executive control, which resolves conflict between responses, as when you have to name the ink colour of the word "RED" printed in blue (the Stroop effect). The model has been useful for studying how attention changes with age and in different disorders.

Meditation and attention

Many mindfulness meditation programmes promise better concentration. Systematic reviews find moderate effects on anxiety, depression and pain, comparable to other active treatments, and less certain effects on attention measured in the laboratory. It is a useful tool for some people, not a universal solution, and in occasional cases it can stir up distress that is best accompanied.

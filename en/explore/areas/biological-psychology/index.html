Biological psychology

Biological bases of behaviour: nervous system, hormones, genes and environment, evolution.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Everything we think, feel and do has a biological basis: neurons that communicate, hormones circulating in the blood and genes that influence how the brain is built. Biological psychology studies those bases. It does not reduce the mind to biology: it shows how biology, experience and environment constantly influence one another.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

The neuron

The human brain has about 86 billion neurons. At the end of the nineteenth century Santiago Ramón y Cajal showed that they are independent cells communicating across tiny gaps, the synapses. A neuron receives signals, integrates them and, if they pass a threshold, sends an electrical impulse that releases neurotransmitters such as dopamine, serotonin or glutamate.

The nervous system

  • Central nervous system: brain and spinal cord.
  • Peripheral nervous system: the nerves connecting to the rest of the body, including the autonomic system, which regulates the heart, digestion and the alarm response.

Hormones

The endocrine system acts more slowly than the nervous system, but its effects last longer. Cortisol mobilises energy under stress; oxytocin is involved in childbirth, breastfeeding and bonding; melatonin regulates the sleep–wake cycle.

Genes and environment

Almost all psychological traits are influenced both by many genes, each with a small effect, and by the environment. Genes are not destiny: their expression depends on experience. And the brain changes with what we do, which is known as plasticity.

Neurotransmitters

Neurons communicate mainly through chemicals. Glutamate is the main excitatory one and GABA the main inhibitory one. Others, such as dopamine, serotonin and noradrenaline, modulate wide circuits. Many drugs act on them, but the idea that each disorder is due to a lack or excess of a single neurotransmitter is an oversimplification.

The stress axis

Faced with a threat, the hypothalamus activates the pituitary gland, which activates the adrenal glands, which release cortisol. This is the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. In the short term it mobilises energy; sustained over time, it affects sleep, the immune system and metabolism.

Gut and brain

There is constant communication between the digestive system and the brain through the vagus nerve, hormones and the immune system. Animal studies suggest that gut microbiota influence behaviour. In humans the results are still preliminary, and many "mood" products are advertised without sufficient evidence.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

Cajal received the Nobel Prize in 1906, shared with Camillo Golgi, who defended the opposite idea of a continuous network. His drawings of neurons, such as the Purkinje cells of the cerebellum, are still admired for their precision. In 1949 Donald Hebb proposed that "neurons that fire together wire together", an intuition later confirmed by the discovery of long-term potentiation.

Twin and adoption studies indicate that, in the societies studied, between 30% and 60% of differences in traits such as intelligence or personality are associated with genetic differences. Heritability describes a population in a particular environment, not an individual, and can change if the environment changes.

The stress response studied by Hans Selye and later by Robert Sapolsky shows how activation that is useful in the short term can damage health when it persists. The gut–brain axis and the relationship between inflammation and mood are very active research fields.

Open debates: how much can be predicted from polygenic scores, which so far have low predictive power for individuals; whether the popular idea that depression is simply a "lack of serotonin" holds up, which reviews do not support; and how to translate findings in animals to human behaviour.

Behavioural genetics today

For years researchers looked for "genes for" depression, intelligence or aggression. Genome-wide studies with hundreds of thousands of people showed that these traits depend on thousands of variants, each with a tiny effect. Many earlier candidate-gene findings, such as the famous 5-HTTLPR and depression, were not confirmed in large samples (Border and colleagues, 2019).

Epigenetics

Epigenetics studies changes in how genes are expressed without changing their sequence. Michael Meaney's studies in rats showed that maternal care altered the expression of stress-related genes in the pups. In humans the idea is promising but often exaggerated in popular accounts: many effects are small and hard to separate from other factors.

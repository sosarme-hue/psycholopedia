Consciousness and sleep

What it means to be conscious, why we sleep and dream, and what happens under anaesthesia, hypnosis or meditation.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Consciousness is the experience of being aware of the world and of ourselves. It is not a switch that turns on or off: it passes through different states during the day, from alert wakefulness to deep sleep, and changes with anaesthesia, tiredness or some substances. Sleep is not wasted time: the brain works during it to restore the body, sort memories and regulate emotions.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

The stages of sleep

In 1953 Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman discovered REM sleep (for rapid eye movements), in which brain activity resembles wakefulness, the muscles are paralysed and the most vivid dreams occur. The rest is non-REM sleep, ranging from light to deep. Each night we go through four or five cycles of about 90 minutes.

What sleep is for

  • Memory: sleeping after learning helps consolidate what was learnt.
  • Clearance: during sleep the removal of waste substances from the brain increases.
  • Emotions: lack of sleep makes us react more intensely to negative things.

Circadian rhythms

An internal clock of about 24 hours, located in the hypothalamus and synchronised mainly by light, regulates when we feel sleepy. That is why jet lag and night shifts disrupt rest. The 2017 Nobel Prize in Medicine rewarded those who discovered its genetic basis.

Altered states

Hypnosis increases suggestibility; it is not sleep, and it does not allow exact memories to be recovered. Meditation trains attention and has modest effects on anxiety and stress. General anaesthesia is still partly a mystery: we know how to produce unconsciousness better than we can explain it.

Common sleep disorders

Chronic insomnia affects about 10% of adults. Its first-choice treatment is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, which includes restricting time in bed and associating bed only with sleep; it works better in the long term than sleeping pills. Sleep apnoea, with snoring and breathing pauses, causes daytime sleepiness and is associated with cardiovascular problems. Sleep paralysis, waking unable to move for a few seconds, is common and harmless, though frightening.

Teenagers and sleep

In adolescence the internal clock shifts later: teenagers feel sleepy later and find it hard to wake early. Several studies found that delaying school start times improved sleep duration and attendance.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

The hard problem

In 1995 the philosopher David Chalmers distinguished between the "easy problems" of consciousness (explaining how the brain discriminates stimuli or reports on them) and the "hard problem": why all of that is accompanied by subjective experience. Several scientific theories compete. Global workspace theory (Bernard Baars, Stanislas Dehaene) proposes that something is conscious when it is broadcast across wide brain networks. Integrated information theory (Giulio Tononi) tries to measure how much a system integrates information. In 2023 an "adversarial" experiment designed by supporters of both did not fully vindicate either.

Consciousness without response

In 2006 Adrian Owen and his team asked a patient in a vegetative state to imagine playing tennis and to imagine walking through her house. Her brain showed the same patterns on the scanner as healthy people. Later studies suggest that some non-responsive patients have some degree of awareness, with clinical and ethical implications.

Why do we dream?

There is no settled answer. Freud saw dreams as disguised wishes, an idea without empirical support. Hobson and McCarley's activation-synthesis hypothesis (1977) explained them as an attempt to make sense of REM brain activity. Other proposals link dreams to memory consolidation or to rehearsing threats.

Lucid dreams

In a lucid dream the person knows they are dreaming. In the 1980s Stephen LaBerge showed that lucid dreamers could send prearranged signals with eye movements from within REM sleep. In 2021 four laboratories even managed a brief two-way exchange with people in lucid dreams, who answered simple questions.

Culture and mind

How culture shapes the way we perceive, feel, think and understand ourselves.

Level 1 · Short introduction

For a long time psychology took it for granted that what it observed in its laboratories applied to all humanity. Today we know that culture influences how we perceive, remember, feel and describe ourselves. Some things are very similar everywhere, such as the ability to learn language or recognise certain emotions. Others vary considerably. Studying these differences helps us not to mistake our own ways for universal ones.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

Pioneers

In the 1920s and 1930s Lev Vygotsky argued that higher psychological functions, such as voluntary memory or reasoning, are formed through social interaction and cultural tools such as language and writing. His colleague Alexander Luria tested this in Uzbekistan (1931–1932): unschooled peasants reasoned about logic problems from their concrete experience, while those who had been to school more readily accepted abstract premises.

What varies

  • Self-concept: in many Western cultures people describe themselves through personal traits; in others, through roles and relationships.
  • Attention: some studies find that in East Asia more attention is paid to the context of a scene.
  • Emotions: the rules about when and how to express them vary, as do the emotions considered desirable.
  • Mental health: distress is expressed and interpreted in different ways.

What seems universal

The ability to learn any language in childhood, the formation of attachment bonds, some facial expressions, a sense of fairness and the tendency to form groups appear in every society studied, though in different forms.

Culture and desirable emotions

Jeanne Tsai has shown that cultures differ in which emotions they value. In the United States high-arousal positive states such as excitement are preferred; in East Asia, calm positive states such as serenity. These preferences show up in advertisements, children's books and even in leaders' smiles.

Bilingualism and culture

Some bicultural people slightly change how they describe themselves depending on the language they answer in. It is an example of how culture works as a repertoire of frames activated by context, not as a fixed essence.

Individualism and collectivism

One of the most studied cultural differences is how much the individual is prioritised over the group. In more individualist cultures, independence, self-expression and personal achievement are valued; in more collectivist ones, harmony, obligations to family and group, and interdependence. Geert Hofstede measured this in surveys of IBM employees in dozens of countries in the 1970s. These are average tendencies: within each country there is huge variety, and the same person can be more individualist at work and more collectivist at home.

The culture of honour

In the 1990s, Richard Nisbett and Dov Cohen ran an experiment at a US university: an accomplice bumped into a student in a corridor and insulted him. Students raised in the South reacted with more anger and greater rises in cortisol and testosterone than those from the North. They explained it by a culture of honour, typical of herding societies with little state protection, where defending one's reputation was vital. Such norms can persist long after their causes have disappeared.

Cultural ways of expressing distress

Psychological distress is experienced and described differently across cultures. DSM-5 includes cultural concepts of distress, such as "ataque de nervios" described in the Caribbean, with shouting, trembling and a sense of loss of control, or "susto" in some regions of Latin America. It also includes a Cultural Formulation Interview, with questions to understand how the person explains what is happening to them and what help they expect.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

The sampling problem

In 2008 Jeffrey Arnett calculated that about 96% of participants in the leading psychology journals came from industrialised Western countries, which account for only around 12% of the world's population. In 2010 Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan coined the term WEIRD to describe this bias. Some effects considered universal, such as the Müller-Lyer illusion or certain choices in economic games, vary widely between societies.

Culture and the brain

Cultural neuroscience studies how cultural experience is reflected in brain activity. One example is reading: learning to read recruits a brain area, the "visual word form area", which in non-literate people responds more to faces and objects, according to Stanislas Dehaene's work.

Cautions

Cross-cultural comparisons carry risks: translating a questionnaire badly, treating whole countries as homogeneous or turning average differences into stereotypes. Differences within a single country are often larger than those between countries. That is why combining comparative studies with fieldwork and local researchers is recommended.

Global mental health

The global mental health movement seeks to bring treatment to countries with few professionals. One example is Zimbabwe's "Friendship Bench", created by Dixon Chibanda, where trained grandmothers offer problem-solving therapy on park benches. A 2016 trial showed a clear reduction in depressive symptoms.

Rice and wheat

In 2014, Thomas Talhelm and colleagues compared northern China, where wheat has traditionally been grown, with the south, where rice is grown. Rice farming requires a great deal of cooperation between neighbours to manage water and labour. People from rice-growing regions showed more holistic, relational thinking than those from wheat-growing regions, even though all were Chinese and living today. The hypothesis suggests that ways of making a living leave a mark on the mind for generations.

Acculturation

When a person moves to another culture, they must decide how far to keep their own and how far to adopt the new one. John Berry described four strategies: integration (keeping both), assimilation (adopting the new and leaving one's own), separation (keeping only one's own) and marginalisation (losing ties with both). Integration is generally associated with better psychological adjustment, though much depends on how the host society responds.

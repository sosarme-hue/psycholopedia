Area · Person and development

Development

Changes and continuities across the whole lifespan, from birth to old age.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Developmental psychology studies how people change from conception to old age: body, thinking, emotions, relationships and identity. Some changes follow a similar timetable in almost everyone, like starting to walk; others depend heavily on culture and experience. Development does not end in adolescence: it continues throughout life.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

Infancy

Babies are born with more abilities than was once thought: they recognise their mother's voice, prefer to look at faces and learn very quickly. Attachment, the bond with those who care for them, gives them a secure base from which to explore.

Childhood

Jean Piaget described how children actively build their knowledge. For example, until age 6 or 7 many believe there is more water in a tall, thin glass than in a short, wide one, even after watching it poured across. By around 4 most children understand that other people can hold false beliefs, which is called theory of mind.

Adolescence

It is a time of searching for identity and greater autonomy. The brain continues to mature, especially areas involved in impulse control, while sensitivity to rewards and to peers' opinions increases. This helps explain why teenagers take more risks when they are with friends.

Adulthood and old age

Some abilities, such as processing speed, decline with age; others, such as vocabulary and knowledge, are maintained or improve. Many older people report emotional well-being equal to or greater than in youth, because they prioritise relationships and meaningful experiences.

Play

Play is not a waste of time: it is one of the main ways children learn. In symbolic play ("let's pretend this is a phone") they practise imagination, language and understanding others. Outdoor play with moderate risks, such as climbing, helps develop coordination and manage fear.

Executive functions in childhood

Between the ages of three and five, the ability to wait, switch rules and remember instructions improves greatly. A New Zealand study that followed 1,000 children from birth (Moffitt and colleagues, 2011) found that childhood self-control predicted health, income and legal problems in adulthood, even taking intelligence and social class into account.

Screens

Paediatric recommendations advise avoiding screens before age two, except for video calls, and limiting them in preschool years. In adolescents the link between screen time and distress is small on average; what matters more is what is done, when (for example, at night) and what activities it displaces.

Understanding that others think differently

In the Sally–Anne test, a child sees Sally put a marble in a basket and leave; while she is away, Anne moves it to a box. Where will Sally look for the marble? By about four, most children answer the basket, because they understand Sally has a false belief. Before that, they usually say the box, where it actually is. This ability to attribute to others thoughts different from one's own is called theory of mind and is key to social life.

How the moral sense forms

Lawrence Kohlberg proposed that moral reasoning passes through stages: first avoiding punishment, then following group rules and, later, reasoning from principles. Carol Gilligan criticised the model, based on boys, for undervaluing an ethics of care and relationships. More recent research suggests that even babies prefer a helper to a hinderer, though these results have not always replicated.

The weight of early adversity

The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) study, published in 1998, found that abuse, neglect and serious problems at home were associated with more physical and mental illness in adulthood, and that risk grew with the number of experiences. The Bucharest Early Intervention Project compared children who stayed in orphanages with others randomly assigned to foster families: those placed before age two improved much more. Early foster care protects.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

Piaget proposed four stages (sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operations and formal operations). Later research showed that young children know more than his tasks revealed, and that development is less uniform than stages suggest. Lev Vygotsky stressed, against Piaget, the role of social interaction and language. Erik Erikson described eight psychosocial stages across the whole lifespan.

John Bowlby formulated attachment theory after observing children separated from their families. Mary Ainsworth designed the "Strange Situation" (1978) to classify attachment as secure, insecure-avoidant or insecure-ambivalent; disorganised attachment was added later. Studies of children raised in Romanian orphanages showed that early adoption into families greatly improves development, and that timing matters.

Paul Baltes championed lifespan psychology, with the idea that every age brings gains and losses. Laura Carstensen's socioemotional selectivity theory explained why older people prioritise close relationships.

Open debates: how far early experiences determine the future, since there is continuity but also great capacity for change; the real effect of screens on child development; and how to promote active ageing.

Development and inequality

Socioeconomic conditions influence development from very early on, through nutrition, family stress, access to education and the amount of verbal interaction. High-quality early education programmes, such as Perry Preschool and Abecedarian in the United States, showed long-term benefits in education, income and health, according to analyses by the economist James Heckman.

A lifespan view

Paul Baltes argued that development lasts a lifetime, involves both gains and losses, and depends on historical context. Urie Bronfenbrenner proposed an ecological model of concentric circles: family and school, the relationships between them, settings with indirect influence (such as parents' work) and culture and laws.

Parenting styles

In the 1960s, Diana Baumrind described several parenting styles along two dimensions: warmth and demands. The authoritative style combines warmth with clear, explained rules and is fairly consistently associated with better adjustment in children. The authoritarian (high demands, little warmth), permissive (much warmth, few rules) and neglectful styles tend to have poorer outcomes. These are average associations: children also influence how they are raised, and culture modifies the effect of each style.

Emerging adulthood

In 2000, Jeffrey Arnett proposed that in industrialised societies the years from 18 to 29 form a stage of their own: emerging adulthood. It is marked by identity exploration, instability in work and housing, and the feeling of being neither adolescent nor fully adult. In Spain, where young people leave home on average around age 30, this stage is especially long.

Emotion

What emotions are, how they are expressed, what role context plays and how we regulate them.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Emotions are reactions to what matters to us. They involve changes in the body (the heart races), in expression (face, voice), in what we think and in what we feel like doing. They are not the enemy of reason: they help us decide, communicate and respond quickly. Problems arise when they are very intense, last too long or do not fit the situation.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

Components of an emotion

  • Appraisal: how we interpret what is happening (is it a threat? a loss? an injustice?).
  • Physiological changes: heart rate, breathing, muscle tension, sweating.
  • Expression: gestures, posture, tone of voice.
  • Action tendency: to flee, approach, attack or withdraw.
  • Subjective experience: what we feel and how we name it.

What are they for?

Fear prepares us to escape; anger, to defend boundaries; sadness invites us to stop and seek support; disgust protects us from contamination; joy reinforces what is good for us. Emotions also tell others how we are.

Regulating emotions

We do not choose what we feel, but we can influence how we live it. James Gross described several strategies: choosing or changing the situation, directing attention, reappraising what happened or modulating the response. Reappraisal usually works better than suppressing expression, which tends to prolong distress and strain relationships.

Naming what we feel

Putting precise words to an emotion ("I'm disappointed", not just "I feel bad") is associated with better regulation. Some researchers call this emotional granularity.

Emotions and the body

Emotions come with bodily changes: the heart speeds up, breathing changes, muscles tense. A Finnish study in 2014 asked hundreds of people to colour in on a silhouette where they felt each emotion, and obtained "body maps" fairly similar across people from different countries. Anger was felt in the upper body and arms; sadness as a general decrease.

Social emotions

Shame, guilt, pride, envy and gratitude depend on how we see ourselves before others. June Tangney distinguished guilt ("I did something bad"), which usually leads to making amends, from shame ("I am bad"), which tends to lead to hiding or anger and is more associated with psychological problems.

Emotions in childhood

Babies show distress, interest and joy from very early on. More complex emotions such as shame appear around the age of two, when children begin to recognise themselves. Learning to name and regulate emotions depends a great deal on how the adults who care for them respond.

Basic or constructed emotions?

For decades the dominant idea, associated with Paul Ekman, was that there are a few universal basic emotions, each with its facial expression. Lisa Feldman Barrett argues for another view: the brain constructs emotions from bodily sensations, context and learned concepts. A 2019 review found that people do not always make the "typical" face when they feel an emotion, and that context weighs heavily in interpreting an expression. The debate remains open, and probably both views are partly right.

When it is hard to name what you feel

Some people notice discomfort in the body but find it hard to identify it as sadness, anger or fear, and to describe it to others. This trait is called alexithymia, and about 10% of the population has it to a high degree. It is not a disorder in itself, but it is associated with more physical symptoms without a clear cause, more difficulty asking for help and a poorer response to some treatments. Learning emotional vocabulary and linking bodily sensations to situations helps.

Catching emotions

We tend to unconsciously mimic the expressions, posture and tone of the person in front of us, and this brings us closer to what they feel. This is emotional contagion. In 2014, a controversial experiment on Facebook showed that reducing the positive posts users saw made them post slightly less positive content. The effect was tiny, but the study was carried out without explicit consent and sparked a wide debate about the ethics of experimenting on social media users.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

In the 1880s William James and Carl Lange proposed that emotion is the perception of bodily changes: "we do not cry because we are sad; we are sad because we cry". Walter Cannon objected that bodily changes are too slow and too similar across emotions. In 1962 Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer proposed that emotion combines physiological arousal with a cognitive interpretation of that arousal.

Darwin (1872) argued that emotional expressions have an evolutionary origin. In the 1960s and 1970s Paul Ekman found that some basic expressions (happiness, sadness, fear, anger, disgust, surprise) were recognised in very different cultures, including a population in Papua New Guinea with little contact with the West. Lisa Feldman Barrett instead defends a constructionist theory: emotions are not fixed circuits but categories the brain builds from bodily sensations, context and learned concepts.

In the nineteenth century Guillaume Duchenne electrically stimulated facial muscles and described the difference between the spontaneous smile, which also contracts the muscles around the eyes, and the forced smile.

Open debates: whether universal basic emotions exist; what role the amygdala plays, now seen as more than the "fear centre"; and whether the idea that putting on a happy face makes us feel better (facial feedback) has a real effect, which seems to exist but to be small.

The emotional brain

For years the "limbic system" was spoken of as the centre of emotion. We now know that emotions depend on wide networks including the amygdala, insula, prefrontal cortex and other areas, and that there is no region exclusive to each emotion. Joseph LeDoux, who studied fear in the amygdala, has insisted on distinguishing between the circuits that detect threats and the conscious experience of being afraid.

Emotions and decisions

Antonio Damasio proposed the somatic marker hypothesis: bodily signals associated with past experiences guide our decisions. Patients with damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex reasoned well in the abstract but made poor decisions in their lives. The idea that emotion and reason are not opposites but work together is now widely accepted, although the details of his hypothesis are debated.

Emotional intelligence

In 1990, Peter Salovey and John Mayer proposed the concept of emotional intelligence: the ability to perceive, understand, use and regulate emotions. Daniel Goleman popularised it in 1995 with very broad promises. Later research has been more cautious: measured as an ability, with tests that have right answers, it predicts something about job performance and relationship quality, but much of that effect overlaps with IQ and personality. Measured with self-report questionnaires, it closely resembles traits already known.

Emotions and the heart

The link between emotions and physical health is not just a metaphor. Acute emotional stress can trigger takotsubo cardiomyopathy, or "broken heart syndrome", a dysfunction of the left ventricle that mimics a heart attack and usually recovers within weeks. It was described in Japan in 1990 and is more common in women after menopause. It is a reminder that intense emotions have real effects on the body.

Intelligence and creativity

Models of intelligence, how it is assessed, what creativity is and why these topics still spark debate.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Intelligence is the ability to reason, solve problems, understand complex ideas and learn from experience. Creativity is the ability to produce ideas or things that are both new and useful. Both can be studied and, with care, measured. Both depend on biological, educational and social factors, and neither can be reduced to a single number.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

The first tests

In 1905 Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon created a test in France to identify children who needed extra help at school. Binet insisted that the score did not measure something fixed and innate. Later, in the United States, the test was adapted and the intelligence quotient (IQ), with an average of 100, became popular.

One intelligence or several?

  • In 1904 Charles Spearman observed that people who do well on one kind of test tend to do well on others, and proposed a general factor, g.
  • The CHC (Cattell-Horn-Carroll) model, the most widely accepted today, combines g with more specific abilities: fluid reasoning, acquired knowledge, memory, processing speed and others.
  • Howard Gardner proposed multiple intelligences (linguistic, musical, interpersonal...). It is very popular in education but has little empirical support as a theory of intelligence.

What IQ predicts

Intelligence scores predict school performance reasonably well and, to a lesser extent, job performance. But they do not explain everything: motivation, personality, opportunities and health also count.

Creativity

It is usually measured with divergent thinking tasks, such as thinking of as many uses as possible for a brick. Creativity appears in science, art and everyday life, and is fostered by deep knowledge of a field, intrinsic motivation and environments that tolerate mistakes.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

The history of intelligence testing has dark episodes. During the First World War Robert Yerkes tested about 1.75 million American recruits, and the results were used to support restrictive immigration laws in 1924. Eugenic ideas also led to forced sterilisations. That is why the responsible use of tests is stressed today.

James Flynn documented that average IQ scores rose about 3 points per decade for much of the twentieth century in many countries (the Flynn effect). Such a rapid change cannot be genetic, and points to the influence of nutrition, schooling and a more cognitively demanding environment. In some countries the rise has stopped or reversed.

Twin studies show that the heritability of intelligence increases with age, but also that schooling raises IQ: each additional year of school is associated with a gain of 1 to 5 points. In creativity, J. P. Guilford distinguished convergent from divergent thinking in 1950, and Teresa Amabile showed the importance of intrinsic motivation.

Open debates: what explains average differences between groups, where the evidence points to environmental and social causes; the relationship between intelligence and creativity, which seems positive only up to a certain level; and how to assess intelligence fairly in people of different cultures and languages.

Language

How we learn to speak, how we understand and produce sentences, non-verbal communication and bilingualism.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Language lets us combine a limited number of sounds and words to express an unlimited number of ideas. Children learn it astonishingly fast, without formal lessons, through interaction with the people around them. Psychology studies how it is acquired, how we understand and produce sentences and how it relates to thought.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

How children learn to talk

  • First months: babies distinguish the sounds of all languages; around their first birthday they specialise in those of their surroundings.
  • Babbling at around 6–8 months and first words at around one year.
  • Vocabulary spurt in the second year, and two-word sentences ("more juice").
  • Grammar: between ages 2 and 5 revealing errors appear, such as "I goed" instead of "I went", which show that the child is applying rules and not just imitating.

Understanding and producing

When listening, the brain anticipates the next word and resolves ambiguities in fractions of a second. When speaking we make slips, such as swapping sounds around, that reveal how we plan sentences. The "tip of the tongue" phenomenon shows we can know a word's meaning without being able to reach its sound.

Non-verbal communication

Gestures, gaze, tone of voice and distance accompany words and sometimes contradict them. Sign languages are complete languages with their own grammar, and they are acquired through the same stages as spoken languages.

Bilingualism

Growing up with two languages does not confuse children or meaningfully delay their development. At first each vocabulary may be somewhat smaller, but the total is comparable.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

In 1957 B. F. Skinner explained language as behaviour learned through reinforcement. Noam Chomsky responded in 1959 with a highly influential critique: children produce sentences they have never heard and learn from limited data, which suggested an innate capacity for grammar. Since then, usage-based approaches (Michael Tomasello) have shown that statistical learning and shared attention explain far more than was thought: 8-month-old babies already detect regularities in sequences of syllables.

Paul Broca (1861) and Carl Wernicke (1874) linked damage to specific areas of the left hemisphere to difficulties producing or understanding language (aphasias). Today we know language depends on broad networks in both hemispheres. Luria studied in depth the aphasias of soldiers wounded in the Second World War.

The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, that language determines thought, does not hold in its strong form. But there are more modest effects: for example, Russian speakers, who have two basic words for light and dark blue, distinguish those shades slightly faster.

Open debates: how much of the capacity for language is innate; what large language models teach us about how a language is learned; and the effects of bilingualism on executive control, which have turned out smaller and less consistent than first reported.

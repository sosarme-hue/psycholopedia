Learning

How behaviour changes with experience: conditioning, observational learning, deliberate practice and skill acquisition.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Learning is a relatively lasting change brought about by experience. We learn by associating things that happen together, by seeing the consequences of what we do, by watching others and by practising. We learn not only knowledge but also fears, tastes, habits and skills.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

Classical conditioning

Ivan Pavlov noticed that his dogs salivated on hearing the footsteps of the person who brought their food. A neutral stimulus, such as a sound, repeatedly presented before food eventually triggers the response on its own. This explains many emotional reactions, such as the unease we feel at the smell of hospital disinfectant.

Operant conditioning

Edward Thorndike and later B. F. Skinner studied how consequences shape behaviour. What is followed by something pleasant (reinforcement) tends to be repeated; what is followed by something unpleasant, or by the removal of something pleasant, tends to decrease. Intermittent, unpredictable reinforcement, as in slot machines, produces very persistent behaviour.

Observational learning

In the 1960s Albert Bandura showed that children who watched an adult hit a doll tended to imitate them. We do not need to experience something first-hand to learn it: we learn by watching others and seeing what happens to them.

Skills and practice

Learning to drive, play an instrument or type goes through stages: at first it demands a lot of attention and, with practice, it becomes automatic. Deliberate practice, focused on improving weak points and with feedback, is more effective than mere repetition.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner (1972) showed that conditioning depends not just on two things occurring together, but on one predicting the other and on how surprising the outcome is. This idea of "prediction error" reappeared in neuroscience: Wolfram Schultz found that dopamine neurons respond precisely to unexpected rewards, and the same logic is used in reinforcement learning in artificial intelligence.

Martin Seligman described biological preparedness: we easily learn to fear snakes or spiders but hardly ever flowers. John Garcia showed that a taste aversion can be learned in a single trial. Edward Tolman found that rats formed "cognitive maps" of a maze even without reward, an early crack in strict behaviourism.

Applications: graded exposure for phobias is based on the extinction of conditioned fear; applied behaviour analysis is used in education and autism; retrieval practice and spacing improve school learning.

Open debates: how much "talent" and how much practice explain expertise, an open question after studies found that practice explains less than was believed; and how associative learning and reasoning combine.

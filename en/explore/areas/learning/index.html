Learning

How behaviour changes with experience: conditioning, observational learning, deliberate practice and skill acquisition.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Learning is a relatively lasting change brought about by experience. We learn by associating things that happen together, by seeing the consequences of what we do, by watching others and by practising. We learn not only knowledge but also fears, tastes, habits and skills.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

Classical conditioning

Ivan Pavlov noticed that his dogs salivated on hearing the footsteps of the person who brought their food. A neutral stimulus, such as a sound, repeatedly presented before food eventually triggers the response on its own. This explains many emotional reactions, such as the unease we feel at the smell of hospital disinfectant.

Operant conditioning

Edward Thorndike and later B. F. Skinner studied how consequences shape behaviour. What is followed by something pleasant (reinforcement) tends to be repeated; what is followed by something unpleasant, or by the removal of something pleasant, tends to decrease. Intermittent, unpredictable reinforcement, as in slot machines, produces very persistent behaviour.

Observational learning

In the 1960s Albert Bandura showed that children who watched an adult hit a doll tended to imitate them. We do not need to experience something first-hand to learn it: we learn by watching others and seeing what happens to them.

Skills and practice

Learning to drive, play an instrument or type goes through stages: at first it demands a lot of attention and, with practice, it becomes automatic. Deliberate practice, focused on improving weak points and with feedback, is more effective than mere repetition.

Punishment and reinforcement

Reinforcement, positive or negative, increases a behaviour. Punishment reduces it. Research shows that physical punishment of children is associated with more behaviour problems, not fewer, and that reinforcing desired behaviour and explaining rules works better. That is why dozens of countries, including Spain since 2007, have banned physical punishment in the family.

Meaningful learning

In the 1960s David Ausubel championed meaningful learning: we learn better when we connect new material with what we already know. From this come techniques such as explaining with examples, asking oneself "why" questions (elaborative interrogation) or self-explanation.

Mistakes that teach

Making a mistake and then receiving the right answer often improves learning, especially if one was confident in the wrong answer. This is the hypercorrection effect. An environment where mistakes are not punished encourages this kind of learning.

Learned helplessness

In 1967, Martin Seligman and Steven Maier observed that dogs that had received shocks they could not avoid later stopped trying to escape, even when escaping was easy. They called it learned helplessness and proposed it as a model of some aspects of depression. Almost fifty years later the same authors revised the idea in the light of neuroscience: passivity would be the default response to what cannot be controlled, and what is learned is rather control, when experience shows that our actions do change things.

Learning without noticing

Much of what we learn is not conscious. In 1996, Jenny Saffran and colleagues showed that eight-month-old babies, after hearing two minutes of a continuous stream of made-up syllables, could tell "words" that always occurred together from chance combinations. They detected statistical regularities that nobody taught them. Implicit learning explains how we master the grammar of our language or social rules without being able to state them.

Sleeping to learn

Sleep is not time lost for learning. During deep sleep the brain replays what was learned during the day and consolidates it; REM sleep seems to help integrate and link memories. Students who sleep well after studying remember more than those who stay up all night, and sleep deprivation before learning reduces the ability to take in new information. Cramming into the small hours before an exam usually comes at a cost.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner (1972) showed that conditioning depends not just on two things occurring together, but on one predicting the other and on how surprising the outcome is. This idea of "prediction error" reappeared in neuroscience: Wolfram Schultz found that dopamine neurons respond precisely to unexpected rewards, and the same logic is used in reinforcement learning in artificial intelligence.

Martin Seligman described biological preparedness: we easily learn to fear snakes or spiders but hardly ever flowers. John Garcia showed that a taste aversion can be learned in a single trial. Edward Tolman found that rats formed "cognitive maps" of a maze even without reward, an early crack in strict behaviourism.

Applications: graded exposure for phobias is based on the extinction of conditioned fear; applied behaviour analysis is used in education and autism; retrieval practice and spacing improve school learning.

Open debates: how much "talent" and how much practice explain expertise, an open question after studies found that practice explains less than was believed; and how associative learning and reasoning combine.

The brain and learning

In the 1990s Wolfram Schultz recorded dopamine neurons in monkeys and found that they did not respond to the reward itself but to the difference between what was expected and what was received: the reward prediction error. This finding connected the psychology of learning with Rescorla and Wagner's mathematical models (1972) and with artificial intelligence, where reinforcement learning is used to train programs.

Evidence-based study techniques

A review by John Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) assessed ten study techniques. It rated retrieval practice (testing oneself) and distributed practice as of high utility. Highlighting, rereading and summarising, precisely the ones students use most, were rated low utility.

The learning styles myth

It is very common to believe that each person learns best in their own "style" (visual, auditory or kinaesthetic) and should be taught in it. In 2008, Harold Pashler and colleagues reviewed the evidence and found no well-designed studies supporting it. People have preferences, but matching teaching to them does not improve learning. What does work is choosing the format according to the content: a map is learned better by seeing it and a pronunciation by hearing it.

The transfer problem

What is learned in one context is not always applied in another. This is the question of transfer, one of the oldest in educational psychology. Practising a memory game mostly improves that game; learning Latin does not make anyone more logical in general. Transfer is more likely when principles are learned and not just examples, when practice uses varied situations and when the similarities between problems are pointed out explicitly.

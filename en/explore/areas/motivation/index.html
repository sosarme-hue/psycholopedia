Motivation

What moves us to act: goals, incentives, persistence, habits and self-regulation.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Motivation is what starts, directs and sustains behaviour. Sometimes we are driven by a bodily need, such as hunger; sometimes by an external reward, such as pay; and sometimes by our own interest in what we are doing. Understanding motivation helps us set realistic goals, build habits and not rely on willpower alone.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation

We act from intrinsic motivation when we enjoy or are interested in the activity itself, and from extrinsic motivation when we seek something external: a grade, a prize or avoiding a penalty. According to Edward Deci and Richard Ryan's self-determination theory, we feel more motivated when three needs are met: autonomy, competence and relatedness.

Goals that work

  • Specific, somewhat challenging goals, better than "do your best".
  • Breaking a big goal into small, visible steps.
  • "If X happens, I will do Y" plans (implementation intentions): "if it is 7 pm, I go for a walk".
  • Feedback on progress.

Habits

Many of our daily actions depend not on a conscious decision but on habits triggered by cues in the environment. Forming a habit takes weeks or months of repetition in the same context. Changing the environment (leaving fruit in sight, the phone out of the bedroom) is usually more effective than trying to resist every temptation.

When motivation fails

Putting things off, abandoning resolutions or feeling apathetic is very common. Sometimes it means the goal is not really ours, it is too vague or we are afraid of failing. A persistent lack of interest in almost everything can also be a symptom of depression.

Basic psychological needs

Edward Deci and Richard Ryan's self-determination theory proposes three needs: autonomy (feeling that we choose), competence (feeling capable) and relatedness (feeling connected to others). When an environment, such as a classroom or a job, satisfies them, motivation is longer-lasting and of better quality. It is one of the most heavily researched theories of motivation.

Growth mindset

Carol Dweck popularised the idea that believing intelligence can grow with effort improves performance. Large later studies found small effects, appearing mainly in students who struggle more and in schools that support that way of thinking. It is not a magic solution, but it is not baseless either.

Rewards and procrastination

We value an immediate reward more than a future one, even if the future one is bigger: this is temporal discounting. It partly explains why we put off what costs effort today and pays off later. Making tasks smaller and more concrete, and bringing some reward closer in time, helps.

Believing you can

Albert Bandura called the confidence in one's ability to do something specific self-efficacy. It is not general self-esteem: someone may feel very confident cooking and not at all confident speaking in public. Self-efficacy predicts whether a person will try something, how much effort they will put in and how long they will persist in the face of difficulty. It is built mainly through experiences of success, starting with small steps, and also by seeing similar people succeed and receiving credible encouragement.

Curiosity

George Loewenstein described curiosity as the discomfort of noticing a gap in what we know, which drives us to fill it. That is why a good headline leaves a question open. Curiosity not only motivates: it also improves memory. In a 2014 study, people better remembered answers to questions that made them curious, and even unrelated faces they saw while waiting for those answers.

Helping change without pushing

When someone is unsure whether to change a habit, insisting on the reasons to change often leads them to defend the reasons not to. Motivational interviewing, developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick, proposes the opposite: asking, listening and helping the person voice their own reasons. It is widely used in clinics for alcohol, smoking or physical activity, and its effects are modest but consistent.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

Early theories spoke of instincts (William McDougall) and drives that reduce the organism's needs (Clark Hull). In 1943 Abraham Maslow proposed a hierarchy of needs, well known though with little empirical support in its strict form. David McClelland studied the needs for achievement, power and affiliation.

A much-debated finding is the overjustification effect: in a 1973 study, children promised a reward for drawing later drew less when the reward was removed. Meta-analyses indicate that expected, task-contingent rewards can reduce intrinsic motivation, whereas informative praise does not.

Peter Gollwitzer showed in many studies that implementation intentions can, in some cases, double the likelihood of carrying out a resolution. Phillippa Lally and her team (2010) observed that forming a simple habit took about 66 days on average, with wide variation between people.

In neuroscience, the dopamine system is linked more to "wanting" (seeking rewards) than to "liking" (pleasure itself), according to Kent Berridge's work. Open debates: whether willpower is a resource that runs out, an idea that has not replicated well; and how to design incentives that do not crowd out people's own motivation.

The "ego depletion" debate

In 1998 Roy Baumeister proposed that willpower works like a muscle that tires with use. The idea became very popular, but a large replication study coordinated across 23 laboratories (Hagger and colleagues, 2016) found an effect close to zero. Today self-control fatigue is thought to depend more on motivation and on beliefs about one's own willpower.

Wanting and liking

Kent Berridge distinguished in the brain between "wanting" something (driven mainly by dopamine) and "liking" it (which depends on other systems). In addiction, wanting can grow while pleasure declines, which helps explain why someone keeps using something that no longer satisfies them.

The Yerkes–Dodson law

It is often said that a few nerves improve performance and too many worsen it, following an "inverted U". This is attributed to a 1908 experiment by Robert Yerkes and John Dodson in which mice learned to choose between two boxes using shocks of different strength. The original study was small and did not measure anxiety, and the curve has been greatly simplified. What does seem true is that arousal helps with simple, well-learned tasks and gets in the way more in complex or new ones.

Paying people to change

Do financial incentives work for health habits? For smoking, the 2019 Cochrane review found that incentives increase long-term quit rates, even after payments stop, and that they are particularly useful in pregnancy. This qualifies the idea that external rewards always destroy motivation: it depends on the task, how it is framed and whether the behaviour was already enjoyable in itself.

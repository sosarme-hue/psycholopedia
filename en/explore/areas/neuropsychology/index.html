Neuropsychology

What brain injury and brain function teach us about memory, language, attention and behaviour.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Neuropsychology studies the relationship between the brain and behaviour, above all through what happens when the brain is damaged by a stroke, injury, tumour or neurodegenerative disease. It assesses which functions have been affected, such as memory, language, attention or behavioural control, and designs rehabilitation to recover or compensate for what was lost.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

Cases that made history

  • Phineas Gage (1848): an iron bar passed through his frontal lobe. He survived, but his character changed, which suggested the role of this region in social behaviour and planning.
  • "Tan", Paul Broca's patient (1861), could only say that syllable: his lesion in the left frontal lobe pointed to an area key to producing speech.
  • Henry Molaison (1953): after surgery on his temporal lobes he could not form new conscious memories, though he could still learn skills.

What a neuropsychologist assesses

Using standardised tests they measure orientation, attention, memory, language, perception, praxis (the ability to make purposeful movements) and executive functions: planning, inhibiting impulses, switching strategy. They compare the results with those of people of the same age and education.

Rehabilitation

The brain has some capacity to reorganise, especially in the first months. Rehabilitation combines exercises to restore functions, strategies to compensate for them (diaries, alarms, routines) and changes to the environment, with the family's involvement.

Dementias

In diseases such as Alzheimer's, neuropsychological assessment helps detect decline early, distinguish it from normal ageing and plan support.

Executive functions

Planning, switching strategy, stopping an impulse or holding a goal depend mainly on the prefrontal cortex. They are called executive functions. After a head injury they can be impaired even when memory and language are fine, and the person seems "different", as happened with Phineas Gage. They are assessed with tests such as the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test or the Tower of London.

Lesions and language

Aphasias are language disorders caused by brain damage, often after a stroke. In Broca's aphasia the person understands but speaks with great effort. In Wernicke's they speak fluently but with errors and understand poorly. Speech therapy and intensive practice help recover part of language, especially in the first months.

Acquired brain injury

Strokes and head injuries are the most common causes of brain damage in adults. Besides physical problems, fatigue, irritability, lack of awareness of one's own deficits and depression are common, and often weigh more on family life than motor after-effects.

Half the world disappears

After some strokes in the right hemisphere, especially the parietal lobe, the person stops attending to the left side of space. They eat only the right half of the plate, shave half their face or, when asked to draw a clock, put all the numbers on the right. This is unilateral spatial neglect. It is not a problem with vision: the eyes see, but that half does not reach awareness. Many patients are also unaware of their problem, which complicates rehabilitation.

Phantom limbs

Most people who have an arm or leg amputated still feel it, and many suffer pain in it. In the 1990s, Vilayanur Ramachandran devised the mirror box: the patient sees the reflection of their healthy hand in place of the missing one and, by moving it, feels they are moving the phantom hand. In some cases the pain eases. These phenomena show that the brain keeps a map of the body that does not change straight away when the body changes.

Head knocks and sport

A concussion can cause headache, dizziness, slowness, concentration problems and mood changes, even without loss of consciousness or findings on imaging. Current guidelines recommend removing any athlete with suspected concussion from play and returning gradually. Repeated blows have been linked to chronic traumatic encephalopathy, described in boxers and American football players.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

Alexander Luria founded modern neuropsychology by studying Soviet soldiers with brain wounds. He proposed that psychological functions do not reside in a single spot but in distributed functional systems, in which each area contributes a component. His books about a man with a prodigious memory and a soldier rebuilding his world after a head wound launched "romantic science", continued by Oliver Sacks.

The split-brain patients studied by Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga, whose corpus callosum had been cut to treat epilepsy, showed that each hemisphere can process information separately. Sperry received the Nobel Prize in 1981. Antonio Damasio revisited the Gage case to propose that emotional signals from the body guide decision-making (the somatic marker hypothesis).

Double dissociation is a central method: if one lesion impairs A but not B, and another impairs B but not A, the two functions depend at least partly on different systems. Today it is combined with neuroimaging, transcranial magnetic stimulation and connectivity studies.

Open debates: how much an adult brain can recover; whether commercial brain training prevents decline, which has not been shown; and how changes in brain networks relate to behaviour in daily life.

Cases that changed ideas

Oliver Sacks popularised cases such as the man who mistook his wife for a hat, who had visual agnosia. Alexander Luria wrote about Zasetsky, a soldier with a bullet wound to the head who spent decades rebuilding his world in writing. These detailed clinical accounts complement studies with large groups and help us understand what living with brain damage is like.

From lesions to networks

Classical neuropsychology sought the "location" of each function. Today it is combined with neuroimaging and the idea of networks: a small lesion at a connection hub can have wide effects, and a large lesion in a redundant area, smaller ones. Lesion network mapping is one of the recent techniques exploring this idea.

Not knowing you are ill

Some people with hemiplegia after a stroke sincerely deny that they cannot move an arm, or give explanations such as "I don't feel like moving it". This is anosognosia, lack of awareness of one's own deficit, described by Joseph Babinski in 1914. It also appears in many people with Alzheimer's disease and in some psychiatric disorders. It is not psychological denial but an effect of the injury, and it is worth bearing in mind when a patient refuses help they need.

Cognitive reserve

Some people with advanced Alzheimer's changes at post-mortem showed no clear symptoms in life. Yaakov Stern called the brain's ability to compensate for damage cognitive reserve. It is associated with more years of education, mentally demanding jobs, bilingualism and an active social and leisure life. Reserve does not prevent the disease, but it can delay the onset of symptoms.

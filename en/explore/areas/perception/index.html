Perception

How the senses and the brain turn stimuli into a world of objects, distances and meaning. Includes illusions, face recognition and perceptual experience.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Perceiving is not taking a photograph. Our eyes, ears and skin pick up energy (light, vibrations, pressure) and the brain interprets it to give us a world of meaningful objects, faces, distances and sounds. To do so it combines what comes in through the senses with what it already knows and expects. It is almost always right; illusions show us how it works when it gets things wrong.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

From sensation to perception

Sensation is the detection of a stimulus by the receptors; perception is its organisation and interpretation. The retina, for example, receives a flat, upside-down image, yet we see a three-dimensional world the right way up.

How we organise what we see

  • Figure and ground: we separate an object from its surroundings, as in Rubin's famous vase, which can also be seen as two profiles.
  • Grouping: Gestalt psychologists described how we tend to group things that are close together, similar or moving together.
  • Constancies: an opening door projects a trapezoid on the retina, but we still see it as rectangular; a friend walking away does not seem to shrink.
  • Depth: we use cues such as the difference between what each eye sees, relative size or perspective.

What we expect matters

The same smudge can be read as a letter or a number depending on what surrounds it. The brain works both "bottom-up", from the data, and "top-down", from knowledge and context.

Faces, a special case

We recognise faces with astonishing speed and accuracy, and we see faces where there are none, in plug sockets or clouds (pareidolia). Some people have prosopagnosia, a difficulty recognising faces, from birth or after brain injury.

Seeing depth

The retina is flat, yet we see the world in three dimensions. The brain combines several cues: binocular disparity (each eye sees a slightly different image), perspective (parallel lines converge in the distance), relative size, occlusion (what covers something is in front of it) and motion parallax (as we move, near things shift faster than far ones). That is why a person with one eye still perceives depth, though less precisely up close.

Perceptual constancies

A white sheet of paper looks white in sunlight and in shade, even though it reflects very different amounts of light. A half-open door looks rectangular even though its image on the retina is a trapezoid. These constancies of colour, size and shape show that perception does not copy the retinal image: it interprets it to give us stable objects.

The other senses

Vision has been studied most, but the other senses are just as complex. Hearing detects differences of microseconds between the two ears to tell where a sound comes from. The human sense of smell, once thought poor, can distinguish a vast number of odours. And proprioception, the sense of where one's own body is, lets us touch our nose with our eyes closed.

Colour

We see colour thanks to three kinds of cones in the retina, sensitive to short, medium and long wavelengths. This is the trichromatic theory of Young and Helmholtz. But further along, in the retina and the brain, signals are organised into opposing pairs: red versus green, blue versus yellow, light versus dark. This is Ewald Hering's opponent-process theory, and it explains why there is no "reddish green" and why, after staring at a red dot for a while, we see a green patch. The two theories were rivals; today we know both are true, each at a different stage. About 8% of men and 0.5% of women of European origin have some colour vision deficiency, almost always in telling reds from greens.

The senses blend

The senses do not work separately. In the McGurk effect, described in 1976, a person hears the syllable "ba" while watching lips say "ga", and perceives "da": sight changes what is heard. That is why we understand someone better in a noisy place if we can see their face, and why face masks made communication so hard for people with hearing loss. Flavour is also a blend: much of what we call taste is smell, as anyone who has eaten with a blocked nose knows.

Illusions that depend on culture

In the Müller-Lyer illusion, two equal lines look different in length depending on the arrows at their ends. In the 1960s, Segall, Campbell and Herskovits found the illusion was much stronger in people who lived among buildings with right angles than in some groups living in environments with few corners. The proposed explanation is that we learn to read certain angles as depth cues. The finding shows that even something as basic as seeing a line partly depends on experience.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

The psychophysics of Weber and Fechner in the nineteenth century was the first attempt to measure the relationship between a physical stimulus and sensation. Hermann von Helmholtz proposed that perception involves "unconscious inferences". The idea reappears today in predictive processing models, in which the brain constantly generates predictions and corrects only the errors.

James J. Gibson argued instead for direct perception: the environment offers rich enough information, and we perceive above all "possibilities for action" (affordances), such as a chair being for sitting on. David Marr (1982) described vision as a series of levels of computation, a key proposal for cognitive science and computer vision.

Hubel and Wiesel (Nobel Prize 1981) discovered neurons in the visual cortex that respond to lines of a particular orientation. Later, two major visual pathways were described: one related to "what" an object is and another to "where" it is and how to act on it.

Open debates: how much of perception is innate and how much learned; how information from different senses is integrated; and why illusions such as the dress that some saw as blue and black and others as white and gold (2015) are perceived so differently.

Perception as prediction

An increasingly influential idea, going back to Hermann von Helmholtz and his "unconscious inference" (1867), holds that the brain does not passively wait for stimuli: it continuously generates predictions about what it will perceive and corrects them with errors. Predictive processing models help explain illusions, why we see what we expect and some hallucinations. They remain the subject of debate and active research.

Seeing without awareness

Some people with damage to the primary visual cortex say they see nothing in part of their visual field, yet when made to guess they point accurately to where an object is. Lawrence Weiskrantz called this blindsight in the 1970s. It shows that part of visual processing happens without our being aware of it.

When recognition fails

Some brain injuries cause agnosias: the person sees well but does not recognise what they see. They can describe an object, its colours and shape, and not know it is a key ring until they touch it. The neurologist Oliver Sacks made such cases famous in The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat (1985). Agnosias showed that "seeing" and "recognising" depend on different brain systems: a more ventral pathway, towards the temporal lobe, deals with what something is, and a more dorsal one, towards the parietal lobe, with where it is and how to act on it.

Hearing well also protects the mind

Hearing loss in midlife is one of the modifiable risk factors for dementia identified by the Lancet commission in 2020. It is not fully known why: the effort of hearing may use up cognitive resources, or the social isolation that deafness brings may have its own effect. In practice, having hearing checked and using hearing aids when needed is a simple step worth taking, and one many people put off for years.

Area · Person and development

Personality

Traits, temperament, identity and self-concept: what is relatively stable in each person and what changes.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Personality is the relatively stable pattern of thinking, feeling and behaving that distinguishes each person. It is not fixed: it changes slowly over life and is expressed differently depending on the situation. Psychology describes it with measurable traits and studies where differences come from and what they are useful for.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

The Big Five

The most widely used model describes personality with five dimensions, on each of which every person sits somewhere between two extremes:

  • Openness to experience: curiosity, imagination, interest in new things.
  • Conscientiousness: organisation, persistence, dependability.
  • Extraversion: sociability, energy, seeking stimulation.
  • Agreeableness: trust, cooperation, consideration for others.
  • Neuroticism or emotional instability: a tendency to experience anxiety, sadness or irritability.

Temperament

From birth babies differ in activity, reactivity and how easily they settle. That temperament, partly inherited, is the foundation on which experience builds personality.

Does personality change?

Yes, though slowly. On average, as people age they become more conscientious, more agreeable and more emotionally stable. Major life changes, such as a job, a partner or parenthood, and psychotherapy can also bring change.

Identity and self-concept

Beyond traits, each person builds an idea of who they are and a story about their own life. That personal narrative gives coherence and meaning to experience.

How personality is measured

The personality questionnaires most used in research, such as the NEO-PI-R or the BFI, ask people to rate statements like "I see myself as someone who worries a lot". They work reasonably well: what people say about themselves largely matches what those who know them say. Self-report still has biases, and combining it with the views of family or friends gives a fuller picture.

Personality and health

Conscientiousness is associated with living longer, partly because conscientious people smoke less, follow treatments better and drive more carefully. High neuroticism is associated with a greater risk of anxiety and depression. These associations are moderate: they describe group tendencies, not individual destinies.

Traits and disorders

The dimensional approach has reached the clinic. In 2022 the WHO's ICD-11 replaced the classic types of personality disorder with an assessment of severity and a few traits, such as negative affectivity or detachment, very close to the Big Five.

The person or the situation?

In 1968, Walter Mischel shook personality psychology: traits measured with questionnaires were poor predictors of what a person did in a specific situation. For years people argued whether behaviour depends more on who we are or where we are. The current answer is both. Each person has stable patterns of the type "if this happens, I react like this": someone can be very patient with children and impatient with bureaucracy. And traits predict behaviour well when many situations are averaged, not a single moment.

Introversion is not shyness

An introvert prefers quiet settings and small groups and is tired out by a lot of social stimulation. A shy person, by contrast, feels fear or discomfort at others' judgement. They are different things: there are shy extraverts and very confident introverts. Hans Eysenck proposed that introverts have a higher baseline level of brain arousal and so seek less stimulation. Extraversion is somewhat associated with wellbeing, but neither end is better in itself.

The "dark triad"

In 2002, Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams grouped three socially aversive traits: narcissism (grandiosity and need for admiration), Machiavellianism (cold, calculating manipulation) and subclinical psychopathy (impulsivity and low empathy). All three share low agreeableness and a tendency to use others. They are measured as continuous traits in the general population and should not be confused with clinical diagnoses.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

In 1936 Gordon Allport and Henry Odbert compiled about 18,000 English words describing people. The lexical approach assumes that important differences end up encoded in language. Successive statistical analyses of those words, by Raymond Cattell and later Lewis Goldberg, Paul Costa and Robert McCrae, led to the five big factors. An alternative model, HEXACO, adds a sixth factor of honesty–humility.

In 1968 Walter Mischel sparked the "person–situation debate" by pointing out that traits poorly predict behaviour in a particular situation. The answer was that traits predict well behaviour averaged across many situations, and that each person has "if this happens, I react like that" patterns.

Traits predict important outcomes: conscientiousness is associated with better academic and job performance and a longer life; neuroticism, with more mental health problems. There are other approaches too: psychodynamic theory, Rogers's humanism, Bandura's social-cognitive approach and Dan McAdams's life narratives.

Open debates: whether tests such as the Myers-Briggs, very popular in companies, are valid, which research questions because they sort into types what are really continua; and how much personality can be changed intentionally.

Beyond the Big Five

Kibeom Lee and Michael Ashton proposed the HEXACO model, which adds a sixth factor, honesty-humility, useful for predicting behaviours such as deception or exploiting others. Whether there is a "general factor" of personality above all others is also debated; most researchers consider it more an effect of social desirability than a real dimension.

Can it be changed on purpose?

A review by Brent Roberts and colleagues (2017) found that psychological interventions, especially therapy, produce changes in traits such as neuroticism within a few weeks, and that the changes last. Other studies, such as Nathan Hudson's, show that people who set out to change a trait and carry out concrete actions achieve small but real changes.

The Barnum effect

In 1948, Bertram Forer gave his students a supposedly individual personality report and asked them to rate its accuracy. The average was 4.3 out of 5. All had received the same text, partly taken from an astrology book: statements like "at times you have serious doubts as to whether you have made the right decision". We tend to accept vague, flattering descriptions as our own. That is why horoscopes and many online tests seem accurate.

What about the Myers-Briggs?

The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI), which sorts people into 16 types, is very popular in companies. However, it has serious problems: it splits traits that are really continuous into two categories, so two almost identical people can receive opposite types, and a substantial proportion of people who retake the test weeks later get a different type. It can be useful for talking about differences, but not for selecting staff or making important decisions.

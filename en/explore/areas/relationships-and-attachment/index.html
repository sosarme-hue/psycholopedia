Relationships and attachment

Why we need others, how we choose partners and what makes a relationship work.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Human beings need bonds. Attachment theory, begun by John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth, explains how our first ties with those who care for us influence our later relationships, without determining them. Research on couples has identified which behaviours predict that a relationship will last and be satisfying. And studies of loneliness show that a lack of bonds affects health as much as other well-known risk factors.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

From infant to adult attachment

In 1987 Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver proposed that romantic relationships are also attachment bonds. Today adult attachment is described along two dimensions: anxiety (fear of abandonment) and avoidance (discomfort with closeness). Most people have a more or less secure attachment, and the style can change with new experiences.

How we choose partners

  • Proximity: we are more likely to pair up with people we see often.
  • Similarity: we tend to choose people similar in values, age, education and interests. Opposites attract less than the saying claims.
  • Reciprocity: we like those who show they like us.

What predicts a relationship working

John Gottman observed couples in his laboratory for decades. He found that what did the most harm was not arguing but arguing with criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling. Stable couples kept many more positive than negative interactions during conflict and knew how to repair after an argument.

Long-term love

Passion tends to decline over the years, while intimacy and commitment can grow. In 1986 Robert Sternberg proposed his triangular theory of love with those three components.

Friendship

Friendships protect health as much as a partner does. According to Jeffrey Hall's studies, it takes many hours together to go from acquaintance to close friend, around 200. Keeping friendships in adulthood takes deliberate effort, because work, parenting and moving house reduce the chances to meet.

Break-ups and grief

A break-up activates systems similar to physical pain and withdrawal. Most people recover within a few months. Keeping routines, leaning on friends and limiting constant exposure to the ex-partner on social media help.

Meeting online

In a few decades, the way people meet partners has changed completely. According to a US survey analysed by Michael Rosenfeld, in 2017 about 39% of heterosexual couples had met online, more than through friends, at work or in a bar. Among same-sex couples the proportion was even higher. Relationships that start online do not seem to be less stable than others.

Siblings

The relationship between siblings is usually the longest in life: it starts in childhood and often outlasts the relationship with parents or partner. In childhood, siblings are playmates, rivals and a first school of negotiation. In adulthood the relationship becomes more voluntary, and in old age many siblings find each other again as a source of support. Birth order, on the other hand, influences personality much less than people think.

Jealousy

Jealousy is a reaction to the threat of losing a valued relationship to someone else. In small doses it is common and can signal that the relationship matters. It becomes a problem when it leads to monitoring, controlling or isolating a partner, behaviours found in many situations of abuse. David Buss proposed differences between men and women in which kind of infidelity triggers more jealousy, though those results depend heavily on the method.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

Loneliness and health

A meta-analysis by Julianne Holt-Lunstad and colleagues (2010), with over 300,000 participants, found that strong social relationships were associated with a 50% greater likelihood of survival during follow-up. In 2023 the US Surgeon General declared loneliness an epidemic. Loneliness is not the same as being alone: it is the painful gap between the relationships one has and those one would like.

Attachment in adulthood: nuances

Attachment types have become very popular on social media. Research shows that adult attachment is better measured as continuous dimensions than as "types", and that the link between attachment in childhood and in adulthood is moderate. Having an anxious or avoidant style does not condemn anyone: secure relationships and therapy can change it.

Couples therapy

Integrative behavioural couple therapy (Christensen and Jacobson) and emotionally focused therapy (Sue Johnson) have favourable trials. In general about two thirds of couples improve with therapy, though part of the improvement fades over time.

Intimate partner violence

Relationships can also cause harm. Intimate partner violence includes physical assault but also control, isolation, humiliation and economic or sexual violence. It usually begins gradually. In Spain, 016 supports victims of gender-based violence 24 hours a day and leaves no trace on the phone bill.

How many friends can we have?

The anthropologist Robin Dunbar proposed that brain size limits the number of stable relationships we can maintain, and put it at around 150, the famous "Dunbar's number". Within that circle there would be smaller layers: about five intimate people and about fifteen close ones. A 2021 study reanalysed the data and concluded that the margin of error is so large that no specific figure can be set. The idea of layers of closeness, however, remains useful.

Loneliness as a public health problem

In 2018 the United Kingdom created a ministerial post for loneliness, and in 2023 the US Surgeon General published an advisory calling it an epidemic. Data show that loneliness does not affect only older people: young adults also report it often. The measures that work best combine opportunities for contact, such as community activities, with help to change the thoughts that keep a person isolated.

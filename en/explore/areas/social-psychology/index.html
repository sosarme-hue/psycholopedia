Social psychology

How others influence us: identity, attitudes, prejudice, cooperation, conformity and groups.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Social psychology studies how people's thoughts, feelings and behaviour are influenced by the real or imagined presence of others. It explains why we conform to the group, how prejudices form, what leads us to help or to obey and why belonging to a group matters so much to our identity.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

Conformity

In Solomon Asch's experiments (1951), participants had to say which of three lines matched another. When the rest of the group, in league with the researcher, gave a clearly wrong answer, about a third of responses went along with the group.

Obedience

Stanley Milgram (1963) asked participants to give electric shocks, which were in fact fake, to another person each time they made a mistake. 65% went up to the maximum of 450 volts when the experimenter insisted. The study, ethically very controversial, showed the power of the situation and of authority.

Attitudes and persuasion

When our behaviour contradicts what we think, we feel cognitive dissonance and tend to change our attitude to restore consistency. Persuasion works differently depending on whether we carefully analyse the arguments or are swayed by superficial cues, such as how attractive the speaker is.

Groups and prejudice

Simply dividing people into arbitrary groups is enough for them to favour their own, as Henri Tajfel showed. Contact between groups on an equal footing and with shared goals is one of the best-documented ways to reduce prejudice.

Helping

Paradoxically, the more people witness an emergency, the less likely any one of them is to help, because responsibility is diffused (the bystander effect).

Attributions

We constantly try to explain why people do what they do. We tend to credit our successes to ourselves and our failures to circumstances (self-serving bias), and to explain others' behaviour by their character rather than the situation (fundamental attribution error). The latter seems stronger in Western cultures.

Attraction

We are attracted to people who are close by, similar and interested in us. Physical attractiveness weighs heavily in first impressions, and we unjustifiably attribute other qualities to it, such as intelligence or kindness (the halo effect). Over time, similarity in values and reciprocity count for more.

Cooperation

In economic games such as the ultimatum game, many people reject unfair offers even though they lose money, which shows that we care about fairness. Social norms, reputation and the possibility of punishing those who do not cooperate help maintain cooperation in large groups.

Cognitive dissonance

In 1959, Leon Festinger and James Carlsmith asked students to do a very boring task and then tell the next participant it was interesting. Some were paid one dollar and others twenty. Afterwards, those paid one dollar rated the task as more enjoyable. With twenty dollars, lying was justified; with one, the way to reduce the discomfort of having lied was to change their opinion. This cognitive dissonance explains why we often adjust our beliefs to fit what we have already done.

Contact reduces prejudice

Gordon Allport proposed in 1954 that contact between groups reduces prejudice if it happens on equal terms, with common goals, cooperation and institutional support. A meta-analysis of more than 500 studies, published in 2006, confirmed that contact consistently reduces prejudice, even when not all conditions are met, though the effect is larger when they are.

How we are persuaded

According to Richard Petty and John Cacioppo's model, there are two routes to persuasion. Through the central route we think about the arguments, and the resulting attitude change is more lasting. Through the peripheral route we are swayed by cues such as the speaker's attractiveness, the number of arguments or what others do. We follow one or the other depending on how motivated and able we are to think about the message at that moment.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

Kurt Lewin, considered one of the founders of social psychology, proposed that behaviour is a function of the person and the environment, and founded action research. Leon Festinger formulated cognitive dissonance theory in 1957. Henri Tajfel and John Turner developed social identity theory (1979): part of our self-esteem comes from the groups we belong to.

The murder of Kitty Genovese in New York (1964) inspired John Darley and Bibb Latané's experiments on the bystander effect. Later research showed that the press account of 38 passive witnesses was inaccurate, and a 2019 analysis of real CCTV footage from three countries found that someone intervened in 9 out of 10 public conflicts.

The much-cited Stanford prison experiment (1971) has been criticised because the researchers themselves shaped the "guards'" behaviour. This and other re-examinations led to the replication crisis: in 2015 a large collaborative project reproduced similar results in only about 36–39% of 100 psychology studies, many of them in social psychology. The response has been a more open and transparent science.

Open debates: how to measure implicit prejudice and what it actually predicts; how social media influences polarisation; and which classic effects survive replication with large samples.

The classics, revisited

Some classic experiments have been re-examined. The Stanford prison experiment archives show that the "guards" were given guidance on how to behave. Gina Perry reviewed Milgram's recordings and found variations in the procedure. The Kitty Genovese case, which launched research on the bystander effect, was told with exaggerations in the press. The phenomena studied exist, but their reach and causes are more complex than textbooks used to say.

Replication in social psychology

The 2015 reproducibility project, which repeated 100 published studies, obtained significant results in only 36% of cases, and social psychology fared worse than cognitive psychology. Since then the field has adopted preregistration, larger samples and studies coordinated across many laboratories.

Groupthink

Irving Janis studied disastrous government decisions, such as the Bay of Pigs invasion in 1961, and proposed that highly cohesive, isolated groups with a strong leader tend to seek agreement over realistic appraisal. He called it groupthink. Among his remedies: the leader should not give an opinion at the start, someone should be appointed devil's advocate and outside experts should be consulted.

Social norms and behaviour

Telling people what others do can change their behaviour. In a 2007 study, households were told their electricity use compared with that of their neighbours. Those who used more cut their consumption, but those who used less increased it: the "boomerang" effect. When the message also included a smiley face for low users, that increase disappeared. These findings are now used in health and energy-saving campaigns.

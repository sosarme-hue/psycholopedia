Wellbeing and happiness

What we know about happiness, how it is measured and what factors support it.

Level 1 · Short introduction

Psychology distinguishes between hedonic wellbeing (feeling good and being satisfied with life) and eudaimonic wellbeing (feeling that life has meaning and that one is growing). It can be measured with simple questionnaires that work reasonably well. The factors that matter most are close relationships, health, having enough to live on and doing meaningful things. Money helps, especially when it is lacking.

Level 2 · Fuller explanation

How it is measured

The most common way is to ask directly. Ed Diener's Satisfaction with Life Scale has five statements. Other tools record emotions throughout the day. The measures have limitations, but they correlate with what friends and family say, with health and even with longevity.

What influences it

  • Relationships: the Harvard Study of Adult Development, following its participants since 1938, concludes that the quality of relationships best predicts a healthy, happy old age.
  • Money: a study by Kahneman and Deaton (2010) suggested that above a certain income level emotional wellbeing stopped rising. In 2023 Killingsworth, Kahneman and Mellers revisited the question: for most people it keeps rising, though ever more slowly, and the plateau mainly affects a group of unhappy people.
  • Genes and personality: they explain a substantial part of the differences, but do not decide everything.
  • Circumstances: unemployment and the loss of a partner have lasting effects.

Hedonic adaptation

We get used to many changes, good and bad. A classic study by Brickman (1978) compared lottery winners with people who had been paralysed and found smaller differences than expected, though later studies add that adaptation is not complete.

Spending on experiences and others

Several studies suggest that money yields a little more wellbeing when spent on experiences rather than objects, and on other people rather than oneself. In an experiment by Elizabeth Dunn (2008), those given money to spend on others reported feeling happier at the end of the day than those who spent it on themselves. The effects are modest and do not always replicate in the same way.

Social comparison

We constantly compare ourselves, and this influences how we value what we have. Earning more than the neighbours can matter almost as much as earning more in absolute terms. Social media, which mostly show the best of others, make unfavourable comparisons easier.

Feeling good and living well

Since antiquity, two ideas of happiness have been distinguished: hedonic, centred on pleasure and satisfaction, and eudaimonic, centred on living according to what one values and developing one's potential. In 1989, Carol Ryff proposed six dimensions of psychological wellbeing: self-acceptance, positive relationships, autonomy, environmental mastery, purpose in life and personal growth. Both forms of wellbeing are related but do not always go together: raising a young child can be exhausting and, at the same time, deeply meaningful.

Small interventions

Writing down three things you are grateful for each week, doing acts of kindness or imagining your best possible future are positive psychology exercises. Meta-analyses find small but real effects on wellbeing and somewhat smaller ones on depression. They work better when the person chooses them, varies them and practises regularly, and they do not replace treatment when there is a disorder.

A U across the lifespan?

Several studies using large surveys have found that life satisfaction follows a U-shaped curve: high in youth, lower in midlife, around 45–50, and rising again afterwards. Other researchers dispute the shape of the curve, which depends on what is controlled for in the analysis. What seems clear is that old age is not, in general, an unhappier stage.

Level 3 · Going further, with references

Happiness across countries

The World Happiness Report, published annually since 2012 using Gallup survey data, consistently ranks the Nordic countries at the top. The variables that best explain differences between countries are income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make choices, generosity and perceived corruption.

Pursuing happiness

Some studies by Iris Mauss suggest that valuing happiness too highly and constantly monitoring it can backfire and increase disappointment. What usually works better is spending time on activities and people that matter, rather than chasing feeling good as a goal.

How much can it change?

For years a chart was popularised according to which 50% of happiness was genetic, 10% depended on circumstances and 40% on our activities. Several authors have criticised these figures as too precise for the data behind them. The cautious conclusion is that there is room for change, but less than many self-help books promise.

Wellbeing and health

People more satisfied with their lives tend to live longer and have less cardiovascular disease, according to longitudinal studies. The relationship runs both ways: health influences wellbeing and wellbeing influences healthy behaviours. Cause and effect are hard to separate, but the association is consistent.

The World Happiness Report

Each year, the World Happiness Report ranks countries according to how their inhabitants rate their lives on a scale from 0 to 10, the "Cantril ladder". Finland has been in first place since 2018, followed by other Nordic countries. Differences between countries are explained mainly by income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make choices, generosity and trust in institutions.

Nature and wellbeing

Spending time in natural settings is associated with better health and wellbeing. In a 2019 study of almost 20,000 people in England, those who spent at least 120 minutes a week in nature, in one or several visits, reported better health and wellbeing than those who spent none. Below that figure no clear benefits were seen. These are survey data, which do not prove causation, but they support the importance of parks and green spaces.

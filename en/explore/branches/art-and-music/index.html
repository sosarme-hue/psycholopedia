Psychology: art and music

Aesthetic perception, creativity, musical experience and emotions.

What it does

The psychology of art and music studies how we perceive, create and enjoy works of art: why a melody moves us, what makes an image seem beautiful, how a creative idea emerges or how a musician learns. It also investigates how art and music can be used to improve health and well-being.

Where it works

  • Research in experimental aesthetics, creativity and music cognition.
  • Music therapy and art therapy in hospitals, care homes and schools.
  • Conservatoires and art schools: learning, performance anxiety and musicians' health.
  • Museums, design and communication.

What research has contributed

  • Musical chills: the moments in a piece that give us goosebumps activate the brain's reward system and release dopamine, like other pleasures.
  • Expectations: much musical emotion comes from anticipating what will be heard next, and from the music confirming or surprising that expectation.
  • Familiarity: we tend to prefer what we have already heard or seen, up to a point (the mere exposure effect).
  • Music and health: music reduces anxiety before surgery, helps speech rehabilitation after stroke and can evoke memories in people with dementia.
  • Performance anxiety: very common among professional musicians; it responds well to cognitive behavioural techniques and gradual exposure.

An example

In 1876 Gustav Fechner, the founder of psychophysics, published his Introduction to Aesthetics. He proposed studying beauty "from below", by asking many people which rectangles they preferred, instead of starting from grand philosophical theories. It was the beginning of experimental aesthetics. Today neuroaesthetics uses brain imaging to study what happens in the brain when a work of art moves us.

A myth

The so-called "Mozart effect", the claim that listening to Mozart makes children more intelligent, arose from a small 1993 study with university students and short-lived effects. Later reviews have not confirmed it. What does seem beneficial is learning to play an instrument, because of the practice, attention and enjoyment it involves.

  3. Huron, D. (2006). Sweet Anticipation: Music and the Psychology of Expectation. Cambridge (MA): MIT Press.

Psychology: consumer and economic

Purchasing decisions, advertising, risk and financial habits.

What it does

Consumer and economic psychology studies how we make decisions about money: what we buy, how we save, how much risk we take and why we sometimes act against our own interests. Together with behavioural economics, it has shown that people do not always decide as the model of the "rational human" predicts.

Where it works

  • Market research, advertising and product design.
  • Consumer protection bodies and financial regulators.
  • Financial education and prevention of over-indebtedness.
  • Prevention and treatment of problem gambling and compulsive buying.

What research has contributed

  • Loss aversion: losing 100 euros hurts more than winning them pleases (Kahneman and Tversky).
  • Framing effect: "90% survival" and "10% mortality" lead to different decisions, even though they are the same.
  • Anchoring: the first price we see shapes what seems reasonable to pay.
  • Defaults: automatically enrolling workers in a pension plan, with the option to leave, multiplies participation.
  • Scarcity: lack of money occupies the mind and makes planning harder, which can perpetuate poverty.

An example

In the jam experiment, published in 2000, a supermarket stall offering 24 flavours attracted more people than one offering 6, but sold far less. The finding became famous as the "paradox of choice". Later reviews, however, have shown that the effect is highly variable and depends on the situation: more options are not always worse.

Ethics and manipulation

The same knowledge can be used to help people decide better or to exploit weaknesses: subscriptions that are hard to cancel, online betting with bonuses or "dark patterns" on websites. The European Union has begun to regulate some of these practices in the Digital Services Act.

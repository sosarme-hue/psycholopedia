Psychology: environmental

Our relationship with spaces, nature, cities and environmental behaviour.

What it does

Environmental psychology studies the two-way relationship between people and their physical surroundings: how homes, streets, noise or nature affect us, and how our behaviour affects the environment. It is a highly interdisciplinary branch that works with architecture, urban planning, public health and climate science.

Where it works

  • Design of hospitals, schools, offices and public spaces.
  • Urban planning, mobility and green spaces.
  • Campaigns to save energy and water, recycle and use sustainable transport.
  • Risk communication for wildfires, floods or heatwaves.

What research has contributed

  • Nature aids recovery: contact with green spaces is associated with less stress and better mood.
  • Chronic noise, for example near airports, is associated with poorer sleep and, in children, reading difficulties.
  • Social norms drive behaviour: learning that one's neighbours save energy leads people to save more.
  • Information is not enough: making the desired behaviour easy, for example with nearby bins or a green default option, usually works better than explanation alone.

An example

In 1984 Roger Ulrich compared patients recovering from gallbladder surgery in a Pennsylvania hospital. Those with a window overlooking trees needed fewer strong painkillers and went home, on average, almost a day earlier than those facing a brick wall. It was a small study, but it inspired a line of research on the design of healthcare settings.

Climate change

Environmental psychology studies why it is hard to act on a problem that is distant, gradual and collective, and how to communicate it without causing paralysis. It also addresses climate anxiety, especially among young people, which is an understandable reaction to a real threat and not a disorder in itself.

References

  2. Schultz, P. W., Nolan, J. M., Cialdini, R. B., Goldstein, N. J. y Griskevicius, V. (2007). The constructive, destructive, and reconstructive power of social norms. Psychological Science, 18(5), 429–434.
  3. Steg, L. y de Groot, J. I. M. (eds.) (2019). Environmental Psychology: An Introduction (2.ª ed.). Hoboken: Wiley-Blackwell.

Psychology: legal and forensic

Testimony, memory, expert assessment and the justice system.

What it does

Legal psychology applies psychological knowledge to the world of law. It studies how witnesses remember, how judges and juries decide, how victims and defendants are assessed and how reoffending can be prevented. Forensic psychology is the part that prepares expert reports for the courts.

Where it works

  • Family and juvenile courts, and court psychosocial teams.
  • Institutes of Legal Medicine and private expert practice.
  • Prisons and rehabilitation programmes.
  • Police, victim support, mediation and crime victim assistance offices.

What research has contributed

  • Eyewitness memory is reconstructive: leading questions can change what is remembered (Elizabeth Loftus).
  • Line-ups: running them blind, with neutral instructions and recording the witness's confidence, reduces mistaken identifications.
  • The cognitive interview elicits more correct information from witnesses without increasing errors.
  • Detecting lies from gestures or gaze is barely better than chance, even for professionals.
  • Interviewing children: protocols such as the NICHD protocol, with open questions, improve the quality of child testimony.

An example

The Innocence Project in the United States has documented hundreds of convictions overturned through DNA evidence. In the great majority of the early exonerations there was at least one mistaken identification by a witness who was sincerely convinced. That evidence drove changes in line-up procedures in many countries.

Limits and ethics

A forensic psychologist is not the therapist of the person being assessed and must be impartial. The tests used must be validated for that purpose. Research on "parental alienation" or criminal profiling has shown the risks of bringing concepts with little scientific support into court.

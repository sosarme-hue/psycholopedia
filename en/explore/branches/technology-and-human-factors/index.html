Psychology: technology and human factors

Usability, attention, social media, interaction with AI and design.

What it does

This branch studies how people interact with technology, from an aircraft cockpit to a mobile app or an artificial intelligence assistant. Its goal is to design systems that fit how human attention, memory and perception actually work, instead of requiring people to adapt to machines.

Where it works

  • User experience (UX) design and usability research.
  • Aviation, driving, energy and other sectors where an error can be serious.
  • Patient safety in hospitals.
  • Digital accessibility and the study of social media and AI use.

What research has contributed

  • Human error is often a design problem: similar buttons, alarms that sound constantly or confusing screens make predictable failures more likely.
  • Mental workload: there is a limit to what we can attend to at once. Using a phone while driving, even hands-free, impairs driving.
  • Automation: when a system does almost everything on its own, people find it hard to monitor it and take back control in time.
  • Checklists: simple tools reduce errors in complex tasks.

An example

During the Second World War several B-17 bombers crashed on landing because pilots raised the landing gear instead of the flaps: the two controls were almost identical and sat side by side. The psychologist Alphonse Chapanis proposed giving each control a different shape, a small wheel and a wing-shaped piece. It was one of the founding moments of ergonomics. In 2009 the World Health Organization published a surgical safety checklist which, in a study across eight countries, was associated with a substantial reduction in complications and deaths.

Current challenges

Today this branch studies how to calibrate trust in artificial intelligence, neither blind nor excessively suspicious; how constant notifications affect attention; and how to design technology that is accessible to older people and people with disabilities.

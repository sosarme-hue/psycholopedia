Key figures

Biographies

1832–1920

Wilhelm Wundt

German physician and philosopher. He organised one of the first psychology laboratories in Leipzig and trained a generation of researchers from around the world.

1842–1910

William James

American physician, psychologist and philosopher. His Principles of Psychology (1890) described consciousness as a stream and shaped generations of readers.

1863–1930

Mary Whiton Calkins

American psychologist and philosopher. She completed every requirement for a Harvard doctorate, which was refused because she was a woman, and was the first woman to preside over the American Psychological Association.

1871–1939

Margaret Floy Washburn

American psychologist, the first woman to earn a doctorate in psychology (Cornell, 1894). Her book The Animal Mind was a standard text in comparative psychology.

1856–1939

Sigmund Freud

Austrian neurologist and founder of psychoanalysis. He proposed that much of mental life is unconscious and developed a talking treatment that influenced all later psychotherapy.

1849–1936

Iván Pávlov

Russian physiologist, winner of the 1904 Nobel Prize in Medicine for his work on digestion. While studying salivation in dogs he described the conditioned reflex, the basis of classical conditioning.

1878–1958

John B. Watson

American psychologist and founder of behaviourism. He argued that psychology should study only observable behaviour and applied conditioning to human emotions in the controversial Little Albert case.

1904–1990

B. F. Skinner

American psychologist and the central figure of radical behaviourism. He studied how the consequences of a behaviour make it more or less likely to recur, which he called operant conditioning.

1880–1943

Max Wertheimer

Prague-born psychologist and founder of Gestalt psychology. His study of apparent motion showed that we perceive organised wholes, not sums of separate sensations.

1887–1967

Wolfgang Köhler

German Gestalt psychologist. In Tenerife he studied how chimpanzees solved problems and described learning by sudden understanding, or insight.

1896–1980

Jean Piaget

Swiss biologist and epistemologist. He studied how children construct knowledge and proposed one of the most influential theories of development of the twentieth century.

1896–1934

Lev Vygotski

Soviet psychologist who argued for the social and cultural origin of the mind. He proposed that we learn with the help of others and of tools such as language, and formulated the zone of proximal development.

1886–1969

Frederic Bartlett

British psychologist who showed that remembering is reconstructing. With the story 'The War of the Ghosts' he found that memory adapts experience to our cultural schemas.

1913–1999

Mary Ainsworth

American-Canadian developmental psychologist. She observed families in Uganda and Baltimore, created the Strange Situation and described children's attachment styles.

JB1907–1990

John Bowlby

British psychiatrist and psychoanalyst who created attachment theory. He argued that a baby's bond with its caregivers is a basic need, not just a by-product of being fed.

1902–1987

Carl Rogers

American psychologist who championed humanistic psychology and person-centred therapy. He argued that the therapist's empathy, acceptance and genuineness foster change.

1908–1970

Abraham Maslow

American psychologist and one of the founders of humanistic psychology. He proposed a hierarchy of needs culminating in self-actualisation, though he never drew it as a pyramid.

1925–2021

Albert Bandura

Canadian psychologist who showed that we learn by watching others. His Bobo doll experiment and his concept of self-efficacy made him one of the most cited psychologists in the world.

1902–1977

Alexander Luria

Soviet neuropsychologist. A colleague of Vygotsky, he studied how culture shapes thinking and founded modern neuropsychology through his work with soldiers wounded in the Second World War.

n. 1918

Brenda Milner

British-Canadian neuropsychologist. Her study of the patient H. M. showed that memory depends on specific brain regions and that there are several distinct memory systems.

n. 1944

Elizabeth Loftus

American psychologist. Her experiments showed that memories can be altered by later information, with direct consequences for trials and eyewitness testimony.

1928–2012

Ulric Neisser

German-born American psychologist. His book Cognitive Psychology (1967) gave the field its name, and he later argued for studying memory and perception in real life.

1934–2024

Daniel Kahneman

Israeli-American psychologist and winner of the 2002 Nobel Prize in Economics. With Amos Tversky he showed that we judge and decide using shortcuts that produce systematic errors, founding behavioural economics.

1937–1996

Amos Tversky

Israeli cognitive psychologist. With Daniel Kahneman he showed that people judge and decide using mental shortcuts that produce systematic biases, and together they formulated prospect theory.

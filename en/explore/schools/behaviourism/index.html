Schools and movements

Behaviourism

Studying observable behaviour and how it is learned.

What does it propose?Psychology should study observable behaviour and the environmental conditions that change it, without appealing to mental states that cannot be directly observed.
What does it study?Learning: how stimuli become associated (classical conditioning) and how the consequences of a behaviour make it more or less likely to recur (operant conditioning).
How does it study it?Controlled experiments, often with animals (dogs, rats, pigeons), with precise recording of responses and manipulation of stimuli and reinforcers.
Who took part?Ivan Pavlov (conditioned reflexes), Edward Thorndike (law of effect), John B. Watson (behaviourist manifesto, 1913), B. F. Skinner (operant conditioning), Clark Hull, Edward Tolman (cognitive maps).
What remains?Behaviour modification techniques, applied behaviour analysis, part of cognitive-behavioural therapies, educational programme design and the demand for methodological rigour.
What is debated?Leaving out thought, language and emotion proved insufficient, as Chomsky's critique (1959) and the cognitive revolution showed. Some experiments of the period, such as Watson's "Little Albert", are also questioned on ethical and methodological grounds.

Context

In the early twentieth century psychology mainly studied consciousness through introspection, and the results varied from one laboratory to another. In 1913 John B. Watson published "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It", proposing to abandon consciousness as a subject of study and focus on what can be observed and measured: behaviour.

Watson drew on two earlier lines of work. In Russia, Ivan Pavlov had described conditioned reflexes by studying salivation in dogs. In the United States, Edward Thorndike had formulated the law of effect with cats that learnt to escape from puzzle boxes.

From Watson's behaviourism to Skinner's

Watson was strongly environmentalist: he claimed that, given a dozen healthy infants, he could turn any of them into a doctor or a thief. His "Little Albert" experiment (1920), in which he conditioned fear in a baby, is now a classic example of research without ethical safeguards.

From the 1930s B. F. Skinner developed operant conditioning: behaviour changes according to its consequences. He designed the "Skinner box", studied schedules of reinforcement and defended a radical behaviourism that also treated thoughts as private behaviours.

Other authors, such as Clark Hull and Edward Tolman, spoke of intervening variables between stimulus and response. Tolman showed that rats formed "cognitive maps" of a maze, a bridge towards cognitive psychology.

What remains today

Behaviourism as the dominant school ended with the cognitive revolution of the 1960s. But its learning principles still hold and underpin applied behaviour analysis, exposure therapy, many teaching techniques and the "behavioural" part of cognitive behavioural therapy. It also left a demand psychology keeps: to define clearly what is being measured.

Utopias and controversies

In 1948 Skinner wrote a novel, Walden Two, about a community organised on the principles of reinforcement. In 1971 he published Beyond Freedom and Dignity, arguing that freedom is an illusion and that behaviour is determined by the environment. The book was a bestseller and drew strong criticism.

Evidence-based applications

  • Token economies: exchangeable point systems used in hospitals and classrooms since the 1960s.
  • Contingency management: giving incentives for negative drug tests is one of the most effective treatments for dependence on stimulants such as cocaine.
  • Exposure: the treatment of phobias builds on the extinction of conditioned fear.

Applied behaviour analysis for autistic children has support for teaching specific skills, although some autistic people criticise intensive versions aimed mainly at making the child "look normal".

Little Albert

In 1920, John Watson and Rosalie Rayner set out to show that fears are learned. They showed an eleven-month-old baby, known as "Albert B.", a white rat that did not frighten him at first, pairing it with a loud noise behind his head. After several repetitions, the baby cried at the sight of the rat and also at other furry objects. The experiment is cited in every textbook, but it had a single participant, the fear was not removed at the end and it would be ethically unacceptable today. The child's real identity is still debated.

Tolman and mental maps

Not all behaviourists were so strict. In the 1930s and 1940s, Edward Tolman showed that rats exploring a maze without reward learned its layout, and that when food was later placed at the end, they ran it almost as well as rats rewarded from the start. They had learned without reinforcement: latent learning. Tolman spoke of "cognitive maps" in 1948, a concept that anticipated cognitive psychology and that neuroscience would confirm with place cells.

Chomsky's critique

In 1957, Skinner published Verbal Behavior, explaining language as behaviour shaped by reinforcement. Two years later, the linguist Noam Chomsky published a devastating review: children produce sentences they have never heard, learn grammar from scarce and imperfect data, and parents rarely correct grammatical errors. For Chomsky, this could only be explained by mental structures specific to the species. The review is considered one of the texts that drove the cognitive revolution, though the debate about how much of language is learned is still alive.

A school's historical importance and the current scientific support for its ideas are different things: we explain them separately.

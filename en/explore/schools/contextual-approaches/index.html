Schools and movements

Contextual approaches

Behaviour understood in its context and function.

What does it propose?Behaviour, including thoughts and emotions, is understood by its function in a context: what precedes it, what follows it and what it does for the person, rather than by its form.
What does it study?Language and the relations between words, experiential avoidance, acceptance, personal values and psychological flexibility.
How does it study it?Functional analysis, laboratory experiments on verbal relations and clinical trials of therapies such as ACT, dialectical behaviour therapy or behavioural activation.
Who took part?Steven C. Hayes, Kirk Strosahl and Kelly Wilson (ACT and relational frame theory); Marsha Linehan (dialectical behaviour therapy); Robert Kohlenberg; Neil Jacobson; in Spain, Marino Pérez Álvarez and Carmen Luciano.
What remains?The "third-wave" therapies, the emphasis on acceptance and acting according to values, and the idea that a thought does not have to be eliminated in order to stop obeying it.
What is debated?Some critics note that their efficacy is similar to that of classic cognitive behavioural therapy and that their theoretical basis is complex. The quality of trials has improved but is still uneven.

Context

Contextual approaches emerged in the late twentieth century within the behavioural tradition, but with a different outlook from Watson's. They rest on functional contextualism: a behaviour is neither good nor bad in itself, but helpful or harmful depending on the context and the person's goals. That is why they pay so much attention to language, which lets us live inside our thoughts.

Key ideas

  • Experiential avoidance: trying not to feel what we feel (anxiety, sadness, memories) often makes the problem bigger.
  • Defusion: learning to see thoughts as thoughts, not as commands or truths.
  • Values: choosing life directions that matter and taking steps towards them even when there is discomfort.
  • Dialectics (Linehan): combining acceptance and change, validation and new skills.

An example

A person afraid of speaking in public has stopped going to family gatherings. A contextual therapy would not focus on convincing them that nothing bad will happen, but on helping them notice the fear without fighting it and return to those gatherings because the people there matter to them, even if they feel anxious.

Historical importance and current support

Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) has accumulated hundreds of clinical trials in chronic pain, anxiety, depression and other problems, and the World Health Organization has used it in self-help programmes for refugees. Dialectical behaviour therapy is a reference treatment for borderline personality disorder and self-harm. Their efficacy is, in general, comparable to other well-established psychological treatments.

Third-wave therapies

Besides acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), the group includes Marsha Linehan's dialectical behaviour therapy, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy to prevent depressive relapse, functional analytic psychotherapy and behavioural activation. They share an interest in the function of behaviour in its context rather than its form.

Relational frame theory

The theoretical basis of ACT is relational frame theory, by Steven Hayes, Dermot Barnes-Holmes and Bryan Roche (2001). It proposes that human language consists of learning to relate things arbitrarily ("more than", "same as", "before"), and that this ability explains both our intelligence and much of our suffering.

Evidence

Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy reduces relapse in people with three or more episodes of depression, according to a meta-analysis by Kuyken (2016). ACT has hundreds of trials, with effects similar to classic cognitive behavioural therapy for many problems.

Don't think of a white bear

In 1987, Daniel Wegner asked students not to think of a white bear for five minutes and to ring a bell whenever they did. They thought of it often, and when they were later allowed to think of it, they did so even more than those who had not tried to suppress it. This rebound effect helps explain why fighting an obsessive thought, a worry or a painful memory tends to make it more present, and it is one of the arguments for acceptance strategies.

Marsha Linehan and dialectical behaviour therapy

In the 1980s, Marsha Linehan developed dialectical behaviour therapy for people with repeated suicide attempts and borderline personality disorder, a group with whom existing therapies often failed. It combines change techniques with validation and skills training in emotion regulation, distress tolerance, relationships and mindfulness. In a 2006 trial it halved suicide attempts compared with treatment by expert community therapists. In 2011, Linehan revealed publicly that she herself had been hospitalised as a young woman for severe self-harm.

Behavioural activation

In 1996, Neil Jacobson compared full cognitive therapy with just one of its parts: helping the depressed person get back to valued and enjoyable activities, and analysing what keeps them inactive. The behavioural component worked just as well. This is how behavioural activation was born as a therapy in its own right. In the British COBRA trial, published in 2016, behavioural activation delivered by less highly trained staff was as effective as cognitive behavioural therapy and cheaper, which makes it very useful in primary care.

A school's historical importance and the current scientific support for its ideas are different things: we explain them separately.

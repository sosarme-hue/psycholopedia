Evolutionary perspectives

What functions psychological processes may have had in evolution.

What does it propose?The human mind, like the body, is a product of evolution by natural selection. Many psychological processes can be understood by asking what problems they helped our ancestors solve.
What does it study?Emotions, mate choice, parenting, cooperation and altruism, fear of certain stimuli, cheater detection and differences and similarities between cultures.
How does it study it?Comparison between species, cross-cultural studies, laboratory experiments, mathematical models of evolution and behavioural genetics.
Who took part?Charles Darwin; William James and the functionalists; the ethologists Konrad Lorenz and Niko Tinbergen; Leda Cosmides, John Tooby, David Buss, Sarah Blaffer Hrdy and Robin Dunbar, among others.
What remains?The idea that emotions have functions, biological preparedness to learn certain fears, the importance of cooperation and shared childcare in our species.
What is debated?It is easy to invent plausible evolutionary stories that cannot be tested. It is sometimes criticised for underestimating culture and learning, and some of its findings have been used to justify inequality.

Context

As early as 1859 Darwin predicted that psychology would rest on a new foundation, evolution. In 1872 he published The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals. The idea inspired functionalism and ethology, which studied animal behaviour in its natural setting and won the Nobel Prize in 1973. From the late 1980s, evolutionary psychology proposed applying this logic systematically to the human mind.

Key ideas

  • Adaptation: some psychological traits persisted because they helped individuals survive or reproduce.
  • Mismatch: our minds were shaped in environments very different from today's, which may explain, for example, our taste for sugar or our disproportionate fear of snakes compared with cars.
  • Biological preparedness: we learn some fears and aversions much more easily than others.
  • Cooperative breeding: Sarah Blaffer Hrdy argued that humans evolved raising children among many carers, which favoured empathy and understanding of others.

An example

In the 1960s John Garcia found that rats learned to avoid a taste after a single bout of sickness, even if it came hours later, but did not associate the sickness with a light or a sound. For an animal that eats new things, that preparedness makes a lot of sense. Many people remember a food they never tried again after it once made them ill.

Historical importance and current support

The evolutionary perspective is now part of the general framework of psychology and neuroscience: almost nobody disputes that the brain is a product of evolution. What is debated is the level of detail. The strongest hypotheses are those that make testable predictions and are confirmed across very different cultures. The more speculative ones, especially those about sex differences, remain the subject of intense debate.

A school's historical importance and the current scientific support for its ideas are different things: we explain them separately.

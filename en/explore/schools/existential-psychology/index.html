Schools and movements

Existential psychology

Meaning, freedom, isolation and death as psychological questions.

What does it propose?The great questions of existence, such as death, freedom, isolation and the search for meaning, influence suffering and wellbeing.
What does it study?How people face finitude, the responsibility of choosing and the emptiness of meaning, and how this relates to anxiety and depression.
How does it study it?Mainly clinical and philosophical reflection; later, social psychology experiments (such as terror management theory) and meaning-in-life scales.
Who took part?Ludwig Binswanger and Medard Boss in Switzerland, Viktor Frankl (logotherapy), Rollo May, Irvin Yalom; in research, Jeff Greenberg, Sheldon Solomon and Tom Pyszczynski.
What remains?Attention to meaning in life in psychotherapy and palliative care, and the idea that not all suffering is a symptom to be removed.
What is debated?As a therapy it has fewer controlled trials than other approaches. Terror management theory has been criticised for results that do not always replicate.

Context

Existential psychology was born from the meeting of psychiatry and the existentialist philosophy of Kierkegaard, Heidegger and Sartre. In the 1930s and 1940s Swiss psychiatrists such as Ludwig Binswanger and Medard Boss tried to understand their patients through their way of being in the world, not only through their symptoms.

Viktor Frankl, a Viennese psychiatrist, survived several Nazi concentration camps, including Auschwitz. In Man's Search for Meaning (1946) he argued that the search for meaning is a basic human motivation and created logotherapy.

In the United States, Rollo May spread these ideas in the 1950s and 1960s, in dialogue with humanism. Later, Irvin Yalom organised them into four "ultimate concerns": death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness.

From philosophy to the laboratory

Drawing on the anthropologist Ernest Becker, terror management theory emerged in the 1980s. Its experiments suggested that being reminded of one's own death leads people to defend their group's beliefs more strongly. At the same time, scales were created to measure meaning in life, which is associated with better health and less depression.

Assessment

Its influence is visible in palliative care, such as William Breitbart's meaning-centred psychotherapy for people with advanced cancer, which has favourable trials. As a general school it has little controlled evidence, and a large replication study in 2022 did not find the classic terror management effect.

Viktor Frankl and logotherapy

Frankl had developed his ideas before the war, working in Vienna with young people at risk of suicide and with depressed patients. He proposed techniques such as paradoxical intention, which involves humorously wishing for what one fears, for example asking someone afraid of sweating in public to try to sweat as much as possible. The technique was later incorporated into behavioural therapy for insomnia and some phobias.

Meaning and health

Meaning-in-life scales, such as Michael Steger's Meaning in Life Questionnaire (2006), show that having a purpose is associated with better physical health, lower mortality and lower risk of cognitive decline, according to longitudinal studies. Meaning need not come from grand goals: many people find it in relationships and everyday life.

Rollo May and anxiety

In The Meaning of Anxiety (1950), Rollo May distinguished two kinds. Normal anxiety is proportionate to the threat and accompanies every important decision: choosing one path means giving up others. Neurotic anxiety is disproportionate and leads to avoidance, shrinking back and giving up possibilities. For May, the aim was not to remove all anxiety but to face it with courage. The idea connects with current therapies that do not try to suppress distress but to stop it ruling the person's life.

Dignity therapy

The Canadian psychiatrist Harvey Chochinov developed a brief intervention for people at the end of life. Over a few sessions, the patient answers questions about what has mattered most in their life, what they want their loved ones to know and what they would like to pass on. The conversation is transcribed and given back as a document to share. Studies show that patients and families value it highly, though its effects on depression or anxiety are modest.

Psychedelics and meaning

In 2016, two clinical trials published together, at Johns Hopkins University and New York University, gave a dose of psilocybin, with psychological preparation and support, to patients with cancer and significant distress. Many experienced a rapid and lasting reduction in anxiety and depression, and described the experience as one of the most meaningful of their lives. These are small studies, and these substances carry risks and should not be taken without medical supervision, but they have revived interest in existential questions in the clinic.

A school's historical importance and the current scientific support for its ideas are different things: we explain them separately.

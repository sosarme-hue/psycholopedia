Feminist psychology

Reviewing what was studied, in whom, and with what assumptions about gender.

What does it propose?Psychology is not neutral: its questions, samples and conclusions have been influenced by assumptions about men and women that need examining.
What does it study?Gender differences and similarities, research biases, gender-based violence, women's health and the history of overlooked women psychologists.
How does it study it?Critical reviews of studies, meta-analyses of gender differences, qualitative methods and studies of social context.
Who took part?Helen Thompson Woolley and Leta Hollingworth, pioneers in the early twentieth century; Naomi Weisstein, Sandra Bem, Carol Gilligan, Janet Shibley Hyde.
What remains?Correcting baseless claims about women's intellectual inferiority, the conclusion that men and women are alike on most traits, and more attention to who takes part in studies.
What is debated?How to combine social commitment with scientific neutrality is debated, and some theories, such as Gilligan's ethics of care, have received limited empirical support.

Context

In the late nineteenth century many scientists took it for granted that women were less intellectually capable. The first women psychologists put this to the test. In 1903 Helen Thompson Woolley compared students of both sexes and found more similarities than differences. In 1914 Leta Hollingworth refuted the belief that menstruation reduced performance and challenged the idea that men were more variable.

In 1968 Naomi Weisstein published a provocative essay, "Psychology Constructs the Female", criticising theories of "female nature" that had no data to support them. In the 1970s the APA founded its division of the Psychology of Women.

Contributions

  • In 1974 Sandra Bem proposed that masculinity and femininity are not opposite poles and that a person can have traits of both.
  • In 1982 Carol Gilligan criticised Kohlberg's moral theory for being based on boys and proposed an ethics of care.
  • In 2005 Janet Shibley Hyde reviewed dozens of meta-analyses and formulated the "gender similarities hypothesis": on most psychological variables, differences between men and women are small or close to zero.

What remains

Its clearest legacy is methodological: asking who is in the sample, examining the assumptions behind theories and not generalising from a single group. It has also recovered the history of pioneers such as Calkins, Washburn and Mamie Phipps Clark. The average differences that do exist in some traits are still being researched, together with the role of biology and social context in them.

Mamie Phipps Clark and the dolls

In the 1940s Mamie Phipps Clark and Kenneth Clark studied African American children's preferences between white and black dolls. Their work was cited in the US Supreme Court ruling that declared school segregation unconstitutional in 1954 (Brown v. Board of Education). The idea for the study was Mamie's, although for years it was credited mainly to her husband.

More inclusive research

Until the 1990s many clinical trials excluded women, and some drug effects were discovered late. In psychology it is now recommended to analyse results by sex and gender where relevant and not to treat men as the norm. The SAGER guidelines (2016) set out these recommendations for scientific journals.

Male and female brains?

In 2015, Daphna Joel and colleagues analysed scans of more than 1,400 brains. They found average differences between men and women in some regions, but almost no brain was "typically male" or "typically female" across all its features: most were a mosaic of traits more common in one sex or the other. Other researchers have replied that, by combining many measures, an algorithm can guess sex quite accurately. Both things can be true at once, and the debate continues. The writer and psychologist Cordelia Fine has criticised studies that exaggerate these differences to justify stereotypes.

Stereotypes learned early

In a study published in Science in 2017, Lin Bian, Sarah-Jane Leslie and Andrei Cimpian told boys and girls a story about a "really, really smart" person and asked whether it was a man or a woman. At five, boys and girls tended to choose their own sex. At six, girls chose women less often and began to avoid games presented as "for children who are really, really smart". Stereotypes about brilliance are acquired early and may influence the interests and careers chosen later.

Trauma and violence

In 1992, the psychiatrist Judith Herman published Trauma and Recovery, linking the experience of victims of domestic violence and sexual abuse with that of war veterans and political prisoners. She proposed that prolonged, repeated trauma produces a more complex picture than classic post-traumatic stress, with problems in emotion regulation, self-image and relationships. In 2019, the WHO's ICD-11 recognised complex post-traumatic stress disorder. The feminist perspective helped make violence against women a subject of health research.

A school's historical importance and the current scientific support for its ideas are different things: we explain them separately.

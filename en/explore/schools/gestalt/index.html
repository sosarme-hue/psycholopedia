Schools and movements

Gestalt

The whole is different from the sum of its parts.

What does it propose?Experience is organised into wholes with properties of their own; it is not a sum of separate sensations.
What does it study?Perception (apparent motion, figure and ground, grouping), problem solving through sudden understanding (insight) and, later, motivation and groups.
How does it study it?Perceptual demonstrations with simple stimuli, phenomenological description and observation of problem solving in people and chimpanzees.
Who took part?Max Wertheimer, Wolfgang Köhler, Kurt Koffka, Edgar Rubin and Kurt Lewin, among others.
What remains?The laws of perceptual organisation, widely used in design and interfaces; current research on visual perception; Lewin's social psychology.
What is debated?It described better than it explained: its physiological hypotheses were not confirmed. Current research has quantified and qualified many of its laws.

Context

In 1910, on a train, Max Wertheimer thought about apparent motion: two lights that switch on one after the other look like a single moving light. He studied it in Frankfurt with Wolfgang Köhler and Kurt Koffka and published his results in 1912. This "phi phenomenon" could not be explained by adding up sensations, as structuralism claimed: the perceived movement was not in any of the elements.

Hence the central idea of Gestalt (a German word meaning "form" or "configuration"): perception organises the world into wholes, and the whole is different from the sum of its parts.

Laws of perceptual organisation

Gestalt psychologists described principles by which we group what we see: proximity, similarity, closure, continuity, common fate and the distinction between figure and ground, which the Dane Edgar Rubin illustrated with his famous vase that is also two faces. All tend towards the simplest, most stable form, the so-called law of good form or Prägnanz.

Beyond perception

Between 1913 and 1920 Köhler studied chimpanzees in Tenerife that solved problems through sudden insight, such as joining two sticks to reach a banana. Wertheimer wrote about productive thinking, and Kurt Lewin carried Gestalt ideas into social psychology with his field theory.

With the rise of Nazism, Wertheimer, Koffka, Köhler and Lewin emigrated to the United States, where behaviourism dominated and the school lost strength as a movement.

What remains today

The principles of perceptual organisation are still in textbooks and are applied every day in graphic design, interfaces and signage. Its critique of atomism influenced cognitive psychology. What is disputed is its physiological explanation: the idea of "electrical fields" in the brain was not confirmed. It should not be confused with Fritz Perls's "Gestalt therapy", which has a different origin.

Kurt Lewin and social psychology

Kurt Lewin, trained in the Gestalt circle in Berlin, proposed that behaviour depends on both the person and their environment, summed up in the formula B = f(P, E). He studied authoritarian, democratic and laissez-faire leadership styles and promoted action research. His student Bluma Zeigarnik described in 1927 that we remember interrupted tasks better than finished ones.

Gestalt and design

Gestalt principles underpin much of visual design: grouping related buttons (proximity), using the same colour for elements with the same function (similarity) or leaving white space to separate blocks. Logos that play with figure and ground, such as the arrow hidden between two letters of a well-known courier logo, apply its ideas directly.

Köhler and the chimpanzees of Tenerife

Between 1913 and 1920, Wolfgang Köhler ran a chimpanzee research station in Puerto de la Cruz, Tenerife, funded by the Prussian Academy of Sciences. There he set the animals problems such as reaching a banana hanging from the ceiling. He observed that, after a period without success, some chimpanzees such as Sultan suddenly stacked boxes or fitted two sticks together, as if they had "seen" the solution. He called it insight and contrasted it with the behaviourists' trial-and-error learning. His book The Mentality of Apes (1917) is a classic, and the house where he worked, known as the Casa Amarilla, still stands in Puerto de la Cruz.

The Zeigarnik effect

It is said that Kurt Lewin noticed a Berlin waiter who remembered unpaid orders perfectly and forgot them as soon as they were settled. His student Bluma Zeigarnik studied this in 1927 and found that people remembered interrupted tasks better than finished ones, as if the tension of the unfinished kept them active. The "Zeigarnik effect" is widely cited in productivity books, but modern replications have produced inconsistent results.

Gestalt and Nazism

Hitler's rise to power in 1933 scattered the school. Max Wertheimer, of Jewish origin, emigrated to New York that same year. Wolfgang Köhler, who was not Jewish, published in a Berlin newspaper one of the few protest articles by a German academic against the dismissal of Jewish professors, and in 1935 left for the United States. Kurt Koffka was already teaching there. In exile, the school lost its centre and gradually dissolved into other currents, though its ideas lived on.

A school's historical importance and the current scientific support for its ideas are different things: we explain them separately.

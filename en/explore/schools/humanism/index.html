Schools and movements

Humanism

Personal experience, growth and freedom.

What does it propose?Every person has a natural tendency to grow and develop their potential. Psychology should study subjective experience, freedom and meaning, not only conflict or observable behaviour.
What does it study?Motivation, self-actualisation, self-concept, the therapeutic relationship, creativity and meaning in life.
How does it study it?Case studies and life histories, phenomenological description and, in Carl Rogers's case, recording and analysing therapy sessions with outcome scales.
Who took part?Abraham Maslow, Carl Rogers, Charlotte Bühler, Rollo May, Viktor Frankl, Fritz and Laura Perls (Gestalt therapy) and Virginia Satir, among others.
What remains?The importance of empathy, acceptance and genuineness in any helping relationship; active listening; positive psychology and research on well-being.
What is debated?Its empirical base was thin and some concepts, such as self-actualisation, are hard to measure. It has also been criticised for a very individualistic outlook typical of mid-century American society.

Context

Around 1960 a group of American psychologists presented themselves as the "third force", against psychoanalysis and behaviourism. The Journal of Humanistic Psychology was founded in 1961 and the Association for Humanistic Psychology in 1962–1963, with Charlotte Bühler as its first president. The movement connected with the cultural climate of the 1960s and with European existentialist philosophy.

Key ideas

  • Hierarchy of needs (Maslow, 1943): physiological, safety, belonging, esteem and self-actualisation needs.
  • Person-centred therapy (Rogers): the therapist does not direct; they offer unconditional acceptance, empathic understanding and genuineness.
  • Logotherapy (Frankl): the search for meaning as the fundamental motivation, even in suffering.
  • Freedom and responsibility: people are not determined solely by their past or their environment.

An example

In the 1940s Carl Rogers recorded hundreds of therapy sessions with his patients' permission. It was one of the first times anyone opened the "black box" of the consulting room in order to study it. That decision launched scientific research on the therapeutic process.

Historical importance and current support

Maslow's hierarchy has not been confirmed in the form usually presented, and he did not draw the famous pyramid. But Rogers's central insight has held up well: the therapeutic alliance and the therapist's empathy are among the best predictors of outcome in any psychotherapy. From 1998 positive psychology took up humanistic themes with experimental methods, with mixed results.

Conditions of the therapeutic relationship

In 1957 Carl Rogers proposed that therapeutic change depends on the therapist showing empathy, unconditional positive regard and genuineness. He was one of the first to record therapy sessions for research. Today meta-analyses confirm that empathy and the therapeutic alliance are associated with better outcomes across therapies, although they are not enough on their own.

Maslow beyond the pyramid

Maslow never drew his theory as a pyramid: the image was popularised later in management textbooks. He himself noted that needs are not satisfied in strict order. Late in life he added self-transcendence, the search for something beyond oneself. Research has found support for universal needs, but not for a rigid hierarchy.

Humanistic therapies today

Person-centred therapy, Leslie Greenberg's emotion-focused therapy and experiential therapies have favourable trials, especially in depression, with results similar to other approaches in some studies.

Rogers opened the consulting-room door

Carl Rogers was one of the first to record psychotherapy sessions, in the 1940s, and to study them systematically. Until then, what happened in therapy was a secret between therapist and patient, known only through the therapist's own accounts. Rogers argued that therapy should be subject to research like any other treatment. In 1965 he took part in a famous film, Three Approaches to Psychotherapy, in which the same patient, Gloria, had sessions with Rogers, Fritz Perls and Albert Ellis.

The therapeutic alliance

Humanism's most solid legacy is the importance of the relationship. In 1979, Edward Bordin defined the therapeutic alliance as agreement on goals, agreement on tasks and the bond between patient and therapist. Meta-analyses of hundreds of studies find that the quality of the alliance moderately but consistently predicts the outcome of therapy, whatever the approach. Therapist empathy is also associated with better outcomes.

From humanism to person-centred medicine

Humanistic ideas did not stay in psychotherapy. In 1977, the psychiatrist George Engel proposed the biopsychosocial model, which asked for attention to the whole person and not only the disease. Since then, patient-centred medicine has stressed exploring the experience of illness and each person's concerns and expectations, and making shared decisions. In family medicine these ideas are part of the usual way of understanding the consultation.

A school's historical importance and the current scientific support for its ideas are different things: we explain them separately.

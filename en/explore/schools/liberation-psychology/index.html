Schools and movements

Liberation psychology

A Latin American psychology serving the majority.

What does it propose?Psychology should start from the reality of the poor and oppressed majority and help transform it, instead of importing theories designed for other contexts.
What does it study?The psychological effects of poverty, political violence and war; fatalism; historical memory and community organisation.
How does it study it?Public opinion surveys, participatory action research, community work and analysis of the social and historical context.
Who took part?Ignacio Martín-Baró, a Spanish psychologist and Jesuit killed in El Salvador in 1989; Maritza Montero in Venezuela; influences from Paulo Freire and Orlando Fals Borda.
What remains?Latin American community psychology, psychosocial work with victims of violence and the idea that social context is part of the assessment.
What is debated?How to evaluate its results with methods comparable to those of other approaches, and the balance between research and political commitment, are debated.

Context

Ignacio Martín-Baró was born in Valladolid, Spain, in 1942, joined the Jesuits and moved to El Salvador. He earned a doctorate in social psychology at the University of Chicago and became vice-rector of the Central American University (UCA). In the middle of the Salvadoran civil war he founded a public opinion institute to give the population a voice.

In 1986 he published "Towards a Liberation Psychology", criticising Latin American psychology for copying theories from the United States without asking whether they fitted its reality. On 16 November 1989 he was killed by soldiers together with five other Jesuits, a housekeeper and her daughter.

Main ideas

  • Recovering historical memory to understand how the present situation came about.
  • De-ideologising everyday experience: questioning explanations that present inequality as natural.
  • Studying fatalism as a learnt response to conditions that seem impossible to change.
  • Understanding psychosocial trauma: war harms not only individuals but also breaks social relationships.

What remains

In Venezuela, Maritza Montero developed Latin American community psychology, focused on strengthening communities. Its ideas now influence psychosocial work with victims of violence, migrants and disasters. Outside Latin America it is less well known, and controlled-trial evaluation is scarce, partly because it works with collective processes that are hard to measure.

Public opinion as a tool

In 1986 Martín-Baró founded the UCA's University Institute of Public Opinion (IUDOP). Its surveys showed what the population thought about the war and negotiations, often contradicting the official line. For him, giving society back an accurate picture of itself was a form of psychological intervention.

Influence on psychosocial work

The ideas of this approach influenced support for victims of political violence in Central America, truth commissions and work with relatives of the disappeared in Latin America. Also in Spain and other countries, in community programmes with migrants and post-disaster interventions, with an approach that combines individual support with rebuilding social ties.

Guatemala: Never Again

In 1998, the Human Rights Office of the Archdiocese of Guatemala presented the report Guatemala: Never Again, from the Recovery of Historical Memory project (REMHI). It gathered thousands of testimonies about the violence of the armed conflict, most of it against Maya communities. The project had a strong psychosocial component: training people from the communities themselves to listen to testimonies and to understand fear, silence and grief as collective effects of repression. Two days after presenting it, Bishop Juan Gerardi was murdered.

Elizabeth Lira and reparation

The Chilean psychologist Elizabeth Lira worked from the 1970s with torture survivors and relatives of people detained and disappeared under the dictatorship. She showed that the damage was not only individual: fear and mistrust spread through the whole of society. She also argued that reparation cannot be only therapeutic, but needs truth, justice and public recognition. She took part in Chile's National Commission on Political Imprisonment and Torture, whose report was published in 2004.

Debates and bridges

Liberation psychology has been criticised for mixing research with political commitment and for relying more on case studies than on quantitative methods. Its defenders reply that all psychology starts from assumptions about society and that it is better to make them explicit. Today it finds bridges with public health, which studies how poverty, housing or discrimination affect mental health, the so-called social determinants. What was once a political idea is now measured in large epidemiological studies.

A school's historical importance and the current scientific support for its ideas are different things: we explain them separately.

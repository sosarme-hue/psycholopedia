Schools and movements

Positive psychology

Also studying what makes a life go well.

What does it propose?Psychology should deal not only with problems but also with strengths, wellbeing and what allows people and communities to flourish.
What does it study?Subjective wellbeing, positive emotions, meaning in life, character strengths, gratitude, hope and relationships.
How does it study it?Wellbeing questionnaires, longitudinal studies, experiments with brief interventions (such as writing about things one is grateful for) and meta-analyses.
Who took part?Martin Seligman, who launched it in 1998 as APA president; Mihály Csíkszentmihályi (flow), Ed Diener (subjective wellbeing), Christopher Peterson, Barbara Fredrickson, Sonja Lyubomirsky.
What remains?Measuring wellbeing as something different from the absence of illness, now used by bodies such as the OECD, and some interventions with small but real effects.
What is debated?It is criticised for selling exaggerated results, overlooking the value of negative emotions and feeding 'toxic positivity'. Some of its findings, such as the positivity ratio, were retracted.

Context

In his 1998 address as president of the American Psychological Association, Martin Seligman, known for his studies of learned helplessness, pointed out that psychology had learnt a great deal about depression and anxiety but very little about what makes a life worth living. In 2000 he and Mihály Csíkszentmihályi published a special issue of American Psychologist that launched the movement.

The idea was not entirely new: humanists such as Maslow and Rogers had already spoken of personal growth. The difference was its commitment to quantitative methods.

Key concepts

  • Subjective wellbeing (Ed Diener): life satisfaction and the balance between pleasant and unpleasant emotions.
  • Flow (Csíkszentmihályi): the experience of being absorbed in an activity whose challenge matches our skills.
  • Character strengths: Peterson and Seligman classified 24 in 2004.
  • The PERMA model: positive emotions, engagement, relationships, meaning and accomplishment.

Assessment

Meta-analyses find that interventions such as gratitude journals or acts of kindness have small effects on wellbeing, and smaller ones in better-quality studies. A much-cited paper on Fredrickson and Losada's "positivity ratio" was corrected after a mathematical critique in 2013. Many authors now defend a "second wave" that also integrates the value of difficult emotions and social context.

In school and at work

Positive psychology has inspired "positive education" programmes in schools, such as Geelong Grammar School in Australia, and workplace wellbeing schemes. Results in schools are small and variable: a large British trial of mindfulness in schools (MYRIAD, 2022) found no benefit over usual teaching. Programmes tend to work better when combined with changes in conditions, not just workshops.

Flow

Csíkszentmihályi studied painters, surgeons, climbers and workers using random prompts throughout the day. He described flow as a state of total concentration in which one loses track of time, arising when the challenge matches one's skill and goals are clear. Curiously, people experienced it more often at work than in their free time.

Resilience: ordinary magic

For a long time it was assumed that after a loss or trauma nearly everyone went through a long period of distress. George Bonanno showed that the most common response after the death of a loved one or a serious event is resilience: transient distress with functioning that stays fairly stable. Ann Masten, studying children growing up in adversity, called it "ordinary magic": it does not depend on exceptional qualities but on common resources such as a trusted adult, good self-regulation and opportunities at school and in the community.

Optimism and health

A 2019 meta-analysis of more than 200,000 people found that the most optimistic had about a 35% lower risk of cardiovascular events and lower all-cause mortality. These are observational studies, so optimism could partly be a consequence of better health or better living conditions. Moreover, useful optimism comes with action: expecting things to go well and doing what is needed to make them go well, not denying risks.

Growth after trauma

In 1996, Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun described post-traumatic growth: many people say that after a very hard experience they value life more, their relationships are deeper or they discover strengths they did not know they had. Studies that follow people before and after the event find that some of this perceived growth is not reflected in measurable changes, and that it may be a way of coping. No one should be expected to "grow" from their suffering.

A school's historical importance and the current scientific support for its ideas are different things: we explain them separately.

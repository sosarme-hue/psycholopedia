Schools and movements

Psychoanalysis

The unconscious, conflict and personal history.

What does it propose?Much of mental life is unconscious. Desires, conflicts and memories we are not aware of influence what we feel, think and do, and can produce symptoms.
What does it study?Neurotic symptoms, dreams, slips of the tongue, sexuality, child development, defence mechanisms and, later, early relationships.
How does it study it?Case studies in the consulting room: free association, dream interpretation and analysis of transference, that is, of the feelings the patient transfers onto the therapist.
Who took part?Sigmund Freud and Josef Breuer; later Carl Jung, Alfred Adler, Anna Freud, Melanie Klein, Karen Horney, Donald Winnicott, Erik Erikson and John Bowlby, among many others.
What remains?The "talking cure" as the basis of psychotherapy, the importance of childhood and relationships, the notion of defence mechanisms and brief psychodynamic therapies with empirical evaluation.
What is debated?Many of Freud's specific hypotheses have not been confirmed. Critics point to how hard his theories are to test, the use of poorly controlled cases and a view of women typical of his time.

Context

In late nineteenth-century Vienna the neurologist Sigmund Freud (1856–1939) was treating patients with paralysis, blindness or anxiety that had no apparent physical cause. With his colleague Josef Breuer he observed that symptoms improved when patients talked about painful experiences. Between 1895 and 1900 this gave rise to a method of treatment and a general theory of the mind.

Key ideas

  • The unconscious: what we do not know about ourselves influences our behaviour.
  • Id, ego and superego (1923): the drives, the part that adapts to reality and the internalised moral conscience.
  • Defence mechanisms: repression, denial, projection or rationalisation protect against anxiety, sometimes at a high price. Anna Freud systematised them in 1936.
  • Development and relationships: childhood leaves its mark. Later authors such as Klein, Winnicott and Bowlby emphasised relationships with caregivers more than drives.

The branches that emerged

The movement soon split. Jung founded analytical psychology, with the collective unconscious and archetypes. Adler founded individual psychology, centred on feelings of inferiority and social interest. Horney and Erich Fromm stressed culture. In France, Jacques Lacan proposed a rereading based on language, highly influential in Latin America and Spain.

Historical importance and current support

Few theories have influenced twentieth-century culture so much: literature, film, art and everyday language. As science, the record is mixed. Hypotheses such as a universal Oedipus complex have no empirical support. On the other hand, the existence of non-conscious mental processes is well established, though understood differently, and brief psychodynamic therapies have shown in clinical trials an efficacy comparable to other psychotherapies for some problems, such as depression.

A school's historical importance and the current scientific support for its ideas are different things: we explain them separately.

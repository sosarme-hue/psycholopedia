Sociocultural approaches

The mind is formed through interaction and culture.

What does it propose?Higher mental functions, such as thinking, voluntary memory or directed attention, are formed first through interaction with other people and through cultural tools, above all language.
What does it study?Child development, school learning, the relation between language and thought and the influence of culture and schooling on reasoning.
How does it study it?Observation of development, the "genetic method" that studies processes as they change, cross-cultural studies and teaching experiments with graduated help.
Who took part?Lev Vygotsky, Alexander Luria and Alexei Leontiev in the USSR; later Jerome Bruner, Michael Cole, Barbara Rogoff, James Wertsch and Jean Lave, among others.
What remains?The zone of proximal development, scaffolding, cooperative learning and attention to culture and context in education and assessment.
What is debated?Some concepts are broad and hard to turn into testable hypotheses. Much of Vygotsky's work became known late, incomplete and sometimes poorly translated.

Context

In the Soviet Union of the 1920s a young literature teacher, Lev Vygotsky (1896–1934), proposed a psychology that took seriously the fact that human beings live in societies with history, language and tools. In barely ten years, before dying of tuberculosis aged 37, he wrote a body of work that would be read mainly after 1960, when it was translated into English.

Key ideas

  • The law of double formation: every function appears twice, first between people and then within the child.
  • Mediation: we use tools (a knot in a handkerchief, a list, a word) to control our own minds.
  • Zone of proximal development: the gap between what a child can do alone and what they can achieve with help. Effective teaching works within that zone.
  • Private speech: when children talk aloud to themselves while solving something, it is not egocentrism but a way of guiding themselves that is later internalised.

An example

In 1976 David Wood, Jerome Bruner and Gail Ross coined the term scaffolding to describe how an adult helps a child build a pyramid of blocks. The adult simplifies the task, keeps the goal in view and withdraws help as the child becomes able to do it alone.

Historical importance and current support

Its influence on education is enormous: peer tutoring, cooperative learning and dynamic assessment, which measures what students learn with help and not only what they already know. Current research on social learning, language and self-regulation supports many of its insights, though with more precise formulations than the originals.

Scaffolding and guided participation

In 1976 Jerome Bruner, David Wood and Gail Ross introduced the term scaffolding: the adult offers help tailored to what the child cannot yet do alone and withdraws it as the child gains independence. Barbara Rogoff studied "guided participation" in Mayan communities in Guatemala, where children learn by watching and joining in adults' tasks.

Vygotsky in the classroom

Sociocultural ideas inspire cooperative learning, reciprocal teaching and classroom dialogue. Meta-analyses find that well-structured cooperative learning improves achievement and relationships between pupils, provided there are shared goals and individual accountability.

A troubled history

Vygotsky died of tuberculosis in 1934, aged 37, and part of his work was censored in the USSR for two decades. It spread in the West from the 1960s, often in translations that simplified his ideas, something specialists are still revising.

Learning in a community of practice

The anthropologist Jean Lave studied apprentice tailors in Liberia and saw that they learned without formal lessons: they started with peripheral but real tasks, such as sewing buttons, and gradually took on more complex ones, such as cutting cloth. With Etienne Wenger, in 1991 she proposed the idea of legitimate peripheral participation: learning is gradually becoming part of a community of practice. The idea has influenced professional training, including in medicine, where residents learn by taking part alongside more experienced doctors.

Bruner and narrative

Jerome Bruner was one of the drivers of the cognitive revolution, but in 1990, in Acts of Meaning, he regretted that the discipline had swapped the study of meaning for information processing. He proposed that we think in two ways: the paradigmatic, logical and scientific, and the narrative, with which we make sense of experience by telling stories with characters, intentions and time. Cultures provide the stories through which we understand our lives.

Learning by watching and pitching in

Barbara Rogoff spent decades studying how children learn in Maya communities in Guatemala. She found that rather than receiving instructions, they learned by watching adults very closely and taking part in family and community activities from an early age. She called it learning by observing and pitching in and compared it with the Western school model, centred on the adult's explanation. Neither is better in the abstract, but knowing both helps in understanding children from different backgrounds.

A school's historical importance and the current scientific support for its ideas are different things: we explain them separately.

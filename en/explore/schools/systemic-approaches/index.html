Schools and movements

Systemic approaches

People within systems: families, groups, communities.

What does it propose?People are part of systems (families, couples, groups, organisations), and their problems are better understood by looking at relationships and communication patterns rather than at the individual alone.
What does it study?Communication, families' implicit rules, the cycles that maintain a problem, life transitions and relationships between generations.
How does it study it?Observation of families interacting, often through a one-way mirror with a team behind it, joint interviews, genograms and, more recently, clinical trials.
Who took part?Gregory Bateson, Don Jackson, Paul Watzlawick, Virginia Satir and Jay Haley at Palo Alto; Salvador Minuchin, Murray Bowen, Mara Selvini Palazzoli; Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg.
What remains?Family and couple therapy, the idea of circularity (A influences B and B influences A), work with the patient's network and brief solution-focused therapies.
What is debated?Some early theories unfairly blamed families, for example for schizophrenia. Feminist critics pointed out that systemic neutrality could ignore power imbalances and violence.

Context

After the Second World War, cybernetics and general systems theory, from Norbert Wiener and Ludwig von Bertalanffy, offered a language for describing self-regulating wholes. The anthropologist Gregory Bateson applied it to human communication, and in the 1950s the Palo Alto group in California began observing whole families rather than isolated patients.

Key ideas

  • One cannot not communicate: even silence conveys something (Watzlawick, Beavin and Jackson, 1967).
  • Circularity: instead of looking for who started it, one observes how each behaviour maintains the others.
  • Family structure (Minuchin): boundaries, hierarchies and alliances that may be too rigid or too diffuse.
  • Solutions before problems: brief therapies ask what already works and what would be different if the problem disappeared.

An example

A teenage girl stops eating. An individual approach would focus on her. A systemic approach would also ask what happens in the family around the symptom: who worries, who withdraws, which conflicts stay in the background. Family-based treatment derived from the Maudsley model, with systemic roots, is now a first-choice treatment for anorexia in adolescents.

Historical importance and current support

Systemic family therapy has empirical support for conduct problems and substance use in adolescents, eating disorders and some couple problems. The idea of looking at the relational context has passed into family medicine, social work and education. Current versions pay more attention to violence, gender and culture.

A school's historical importance and the current scientific support for its ideas are different things: we explain them separately.

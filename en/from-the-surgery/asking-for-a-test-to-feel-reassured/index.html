Asking for a test to feel reassured

When worry about health leads to asking for test after test: the reassurance cycle and how to break it.

By Alejandro Rosàs, family doctor

It is understandable to want a test when something worries us. But in the surgery a particular pattern is often seen: the person asks for a blood test or a scan, the result is normal, they feel calm for a few days… and the doubt returns, sometimes with a different symptom. This is what psychology describes as the reassurance cycle.

It works like this: worry causes anxiety; seeking safety (a test, a visit, an internet search, asking family) reduces that anxiety; and that relief reinforces safety-seeking. In the medium term, the worry does not go away but becomes easier to trigger. It is the same mechanism that keeps other fears going.

When worry takes up a lot of space

If worry about having a serious illness is intense, lasts months and takes up much of the day, it may be what current classifications call health anxiety (formerly hypochondria). It is not exaggerating or "seeking attention": it is an anxiety problem with treatment.

What helps

It helps when the doctor and the person agree a plan: which tests make sense and which do not, scheduled reviews instead of visits every time a doubt arises, and a clear explanation of how the cycle works. Cognitive-behavioural therapy has good results in health anxiety: it works on catastrophic interpretations of bodily sensations and gradually reduces checking behaviours.

A practical tip: searching for symptoms online late at night rarely reassures. It is usually better to write the doubt down and bring it to the next appointment.

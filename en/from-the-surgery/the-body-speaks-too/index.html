The body speaks too

Pain, tiredness or dizziness without a clear organic cause: why they are so common in primary care and how they can be approached.

By Alejandro Rosàs, family doctor

A significant share of primary care consultations involves real physical symptoms (headache, stomach upset, tiredness, palpitations, dizziness) for which, after an examination and reasonable tests, no disease fully explains them. Medical literature has given them many names: medically unexplained symptoms, functional symptoms, somatisation. None of these names is entirely satisfying, and rightly so.

The first thing to say is that these symptoms are not imaginary. They are felt, they hurt and they limit people. What happens is that body and mind do not work as separate compartments: ongoing stress, anxiety, grief or poor rest change muscle tension, breathing, bowel rhythm and the way the nervous system processes bodily signals. Psychology has studied this for decades.

What helps and what does not

It helps to listen to the whole symptom: when it began, what was happening in the person's life, what they fear it means. Often the underlying question is not "what do I have?" but "is it serious?". It helps to explain clearly how stress and attention to the body can amplify a symptom, without implying that "it's all nerves".

Repeating tests that were already normal does not usually help: they reassure for a few days and then the doubt returns. Nor does the message "there's nothing wrong with you", which the person hears as "I don't believe you".

What does have support is a shared plan: scheduled reviews instead of urgent visits, gradual physical activity, looking after sleep and, when needed, a psychological intervention. Cognitive-behavioural therapy has shown benefits in several of these conditions.

What psychology brings

Psychology offers a language to talk about this without blame: the symptom is real, body and mind influence each other, and there are things that can be done. That message, delivered with time and respect, is often the first step of treatment.

From the surgery

The carer is a patient too

People caring for a sick relative usually come to the surgery for the other person, not for themselves. Why the carer deserves attention too.

By Alejandro Rosàs, family doctor

In primary care we see many people who care for others: daughters looking after a mother with dementia, husbands supporting a long illness, parents of children with disabilities. They almost always come to the surgery for the relative: a prescription, a report, a question about medication. They rarely come for themselves.

What goes unseen

Caring for a long time wears people down. Studies show that carers have more symptoms of anxiety and depression, sleep worse, skip their own check-ups and have less time for social life. On top of this comes guilt: for getting angry, for wanting a break, for considering a care home. Many carers do not complain because they feel they have no right to, since "the other person is the one who is ill".

Warning signs

  • Tiredness that does not improve with rest.
  • Irritability, crying easily or feeling unable to cope.
  • Having stopped seeing friends or doing anything for yourself.
  • Neglected health problems of your own.
  • Thoughts that "I wish it would all end".

What the surgery can do

Asking the carer "and how are you?" is sometimes the most useful intervention of the day. The surgery can assess their health, inform them about support and respite services, put them in touch with social workers and associations and, when needed, offer treatment. Caring for the carer is not a luxury: it is also caring for the sick person, who depends on them.

If you care for someone, your health counts too. Making an appointment for yourself is not selfish.

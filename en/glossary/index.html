Glossary

Psychology glossary

178 psychology terms explained briefly and clearly, from addiction to the zone of proximal development.

ABCDEFGHILMNOPRSTUVWZ

A

Addiction
Loss of control over using a substance or engaging in a behaviour, which continues despite negative consequences and takes an increasingly central place in life.
ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder)
A neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by a persistent pattern of inattention, hyperactivity or impulsivity, beginning in childhood and affecting daily life in several areas.
Agoraphobia
Intense fear of situations where escape or help might be difficult if panic symptoms appeared: public transport, crowds, open spaces or leaving home alone.
Alexithymia
Difficulty identifying and describing one's own emotions and telling them apart from bodily sensations.
Amnesia
Memory loss due to brain injury, illness, trauma or a substance.
Amygdala
A small almond-shaped structure deep in each temporal lobe.
Anchoring
A bias whereby an initial number or piece of information, even an irrelevant one, pulls later estimates towards it.
Anhedonia
A reduced or lost capacity to feel pleasure or interest in activities that used to be enjoyable.
Antidepressant
A drug used to treat depression and also some anxiety disorders, OCD or chronic pain.
Anxiety
An anticipatory response to a future or uncertain threat, with tension, worry and physical arousal (palpitations, fast breathing, restlessness).
Anxiolytic
A drug that reduces anxiety.
Aphasia
A language disorder caused by brain damage, often a stroke in the left hemisphere.
Assertiveness
The ability to express one's own opinions, needs and limits clearly and respectfully, without attacking or giving in.
Attachment
The emotional bond a child forms with the people who care for them, which serves as a secure base for exploring the world.
Attention
The ability to select part of the available information and keep focus on it, leaving the rest in the background.
Attribution
The explanation we give for the causes of our own or others' behaviour: the person (their character, effort) or the situation (the circumstances).
Autism (autism spectrum disorder)
A neurodevelopmental condition characterised by persistent differences in communication and social interaction, together with intense interests, repetitive behaviours and sensory differences.
Avoidance
Behaviour that steers away from situations, places, thoughts or emotions that cause distress.

B

Behaviourism
A school of psychology that, from John B.
Big Five
The personality model best supported by research, describing five broad traits: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism (or emotional stability).
Bipolar disorder
A mood disorder in which episodes of depression alternate with episodes of mania or hypomania (very elevated or irritable mood, less need for sleep, overactivity, impulsivity).
Brain lateralisation
The specialisation of each brain hemisphere for certain functions: in most people, language depends more on the left.
Burnout
A syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, with three dimensions: exhaustion, detachment or cynicism towards work, and a sense of ineffectiveness.
Bystander effect
The tendency for any given person to be less likely to help in an emergency when more people are present, due to diffusion of responsibility.

C

Catastrophising
A cognitive distortion that involves anticipating the worst possible outcome and treating it as likely, or magnifying the consequences of something negative.
Catharsis
An idea, Greek in origin and taken up by early psychoanalysis, that intensely expressing an emotion releases and reduces it.
Circadian rhythm
A biological cycle of about 24 hours that regulates sleep and wakefulness, body temperature and many hormones.
Classical conditioning
Learning in which a neutral stimulus, repeatedly paired with one that triggers an automatic response, comes to trigger that response on its own.
Cognition
The set of mental processes by which we acquire, transform and use information: perception, attention, memory, language, reasoning and decision-making.
Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)
A structured, research-based form of psychotherapy that works on the relationship between thoughts, emotions and behaviours.
Cognitive bias
A systematic, predictable error in the way we think, judge or decide, usually arising from mental shortcuts or heuristics.
Cognitive development
Changes in the way of thinking, reasoning and understanding the world from infancy to adulthood.
Cognitive dissonance
The discomfort that arises when we hold two ideas, or an idea and a behaviour, that contradict each other.
Cognitive distortion
A habitual, biased way of interpreting reality that feeds negative emotions: all-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralising from one case, mind-reading, catastrophising.
Cognitive psychology
An approach that studies mental processes (perception, attention, memory, language, reasoning) by viewing the mind as an information-processing system.
Compulsion
A repetitive behaviour or mental act a person feels driven to perform, usually to reduce the anxiety caused by an obsession or to prevent something feared: washing, checking, ordering, counting.
Confirmation bias
The tendency to seek, remember and interpret information in ways that confirm what we already believe, and to overlook what contradicts it.
Conformity
Changing opinions or behaviour to match the group, because we think others know more or because we want to be accepted.
Consciousness
The subjective experience of oneself and one's surroundings: the fact that there is "something it is like" to see a colour or have a thought.
Coping
The thinking and behavioural efforts used to manage situations perceived as taxing or threatening.
Correlation
A statistical relationship between two variables that tend to change together.
Cortisol
A hormone released by the adrenal glands, especially under stress and following a daily rhythm (highest on waking).
Creativity
The ability to produce ideas or products that are both original and useful or appropriate.

D

Defence mechanisms
According to psychoanalysis, unconscious strategies to protect oneself from anxiety or unacceptable ideas: denial, projection, rationalisation, repression, sublimation.
Delusion
A firm belief held despite clear contrary evidence and not shared by the person's cultural setting, such as feeling persecuted or believing the television sends personal messages.
Dementia
An acquired decline in memory and other mental abilities serious enough to affect independence in daily life.
Depersonalisation
A feeling of being detached from oneself, as if watching from outside or acting on autopilot.
Depression
A mood disorder characterised by persistent sadness or loss of interest for at least two weeks, together with other symptoms such as tiredness, changes in sleep and appetite, difficulty concentrating, feelings of guilt or thoughts of death.
Dissociation
A disconnection between functions that are normally integrated, such as memory, identity, perception or awareness of one's own body.
Dopamine
A neurotransmitter involved in movement, motivation, reward learning and anticipating what is worth pursuing.

E

Eating disorder
A group of disorders with serious disturbances in eating and in the relationship with body and weight, such as anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa or binge-eating disorder.
Effect size
A measure of how large a difference or relationship is in a study, independent of sample size.
Emotion
A brief, organised response to something relevant to us, including bodily changes, a subjective experience, an expression and a tendency to act.
Emotional intelligence
The ability to perceive, understand, use and regulate one's own and others' emotions.
Empathy
The ability to understand what another person thinks or feels (cognitive empathy) and to resonate with their emotion (affective empathy).
Episodic memory
Memory for personal experiences located in a time and place, such as remembering your last birthday dinner.
Executive functions
The set of abilities that let us steer behaviour towards a goal: planning, keeping information in mind, inhibiting impulses, switching strategy and monitoring performance.
Exposure therapy
A psychological technique that involves facing what is feared in a gradual, planned and repeated way, without escaping or using safety behaviours, until fear decreases.
Extinction
In learning, the weakening of a learned response when the association or consequence that maintained it no longer occurs.
Extraversion
A personality trait reflecting a tendency to seek social stimulation, activity and positive emotions.

F

False memory
A memory of something that did not happen, or happened differently, that the person experiences as genuine.
Fight-or-flight response
An automatic reaction to threat, described by Walter Cannon, in which the sympathetic nervous system and adrenaline speed up the heart and breathing, tense the muscles and mobilise energy.
Flow
A state of total absorption in an activity, in which one loses track of time and action seems to flow by itself.
Flynn effect
The rise in intelligence test scores seen during the 20th century in many countries, of several points per decade.
Framing effect
A bias whereby the same information, presented as a gain or as a loss, leads to different decisions.
Frontal lobe
The front part of the cerebral cortex, the most developed in humans.
Fundamental attribution error
The tendency to explain other people's behaviour by their character, underestimating the role of the situation.

G

g factor
The general intelligence factor proposed by Charles Spearman, which explains why people who score well on one type of test tend to score well on others.
Gestalt
A school of psychology that emerged in Germany in the early 20th century, arguing that we perceive organised wholes rather than sums of elements: "the whole is different from the sum of its parts".
Grief
The natural response to losing someone or something important, including emotions, thoughts, physical sensations and changes in behaviour.
Groupthink
A phenomenon described by Irving Janis in which a cohesive group, to maintain consensus, stops considering alternatives and criticism and makes poor decisions.

H

Habituation
A decrease in response to a stimulus that is repeated and irrelevant, such as no longer noticing a clock's ticking.
Hallucination
A perception experienced as real without the corresponding stimulus: hearing voices, seeing figures or feeling touches that are not there.
Halo effect
A bias whereby a positive impression in one area (physical attractiveness, for example) spreads to unrelated ones, such as intelligence or honesty.
Happiness
In psychology it is usually studied as subjective wellbeing: life satisfaction and a predominance of pleasant emotions.
Harm reduction
A public health approach aiming to lessen the negative consequences of substance use even if the person does not stop, such as methadone programmes, needle exchange or information on safer use.
Health anxiety (hypochondria)
Intense, persistent worry about having or developing a serious illness, which is not relieved by normal test results or medical reassurance.
Health psychology
The branch of psychology that studies how psychological, social and behavioural factors influence physical health and illness, and how to promote healthy habits, improve adherence and help people live with chronic illness.
Hedonic adaptation
The tendency for the joy or distress caused by a life change to return over time to levels close to before.
Heritability
The proportion of differences between people in a trait that is attributed to genetic differences, in a particular population and environment.
Heuristic
A mental shortcut that allows quick, low-effort judgements or decisions.
Hierarchy of needs
Abraham Maslow's theory ordering human needs from physiological and safety needs up to belonging, esteem and self-actualisation.
Hippocampus
A structure in the inner part of the temporal lobe, essential for forming new memories of facts and experiences and for spatial navigation.
Humanistic psychology
A movement that emerged in the mid-20th century, with Carl Rogers and Abraham Maslow, focusing on subjective experience, freedom, personal growth and the therapeutic relationship, in contrast to behaviourism and psychoanalysis.

I

Imprinting
Rapid learning that occurs in a very early period of some animals' lives, such as ducklings following the first moving object they see after hatching.
Insight
A sudden understanding of a problem's solution, the "aha!" experience.
Insomnia
Persistent difficulty falling or staying asleep, or waking too early, with consequences during the day.
Intelligence
The ability to reason, plan, solve problems, think abstractly, understand complex ideas and learn from experience.
Intrinsic motivation
Motivation to do something because the activity itself is interesting or satisfying, as opposed to extrinsic motivation, which seeks an external reward or to avoid punishment.
Introspection
Observing one's own mental states.
IQ (intelligence quotient)
A score on an intelligence test indicating a person's position relative to others of the same age.

L

Learned helplessness
A state in which, after repeated experiences of being unable to control events, a person or animal stops trying to change the situation even when it becomes possible.
Limbic system
A classic name for a group of brain structures, such as the amygdala and hippocampus, involved in emotion, motivation and memory.
Locus of control
The extent to which a person believes that what happens to them depends on their own actions (internal locus) or on external factors such as luck or other people (external locus).
Loneliness
The painful experience that our relationships are fewer or less satisfying than we would like.
Loss aversion
The tendency for a loss to affect us more than a gain of the same size.

M

Memory consolidation
The process by which a newly formed memory stabilises and becomes lasting.
Mental disorder
A clinically significant disturbance in thinking, emotional regulation or behaviour that causes distress or interferes with daily life.
Mental health
According to WHO, a state of wellbeing that enables people to cope with life's stresses, realise their abilities, learn, work and contribute to their community.
Meta-analysis
A statistical method that combines the results of many studies on the same question to obtain a more precise estimate of the effect.
Metacognition
Knowledge and control of one's own mental processes: knowing what we know and do not know, noticing we are not understanding a text, or choosing how to study.
Mindfulness
The practice of deliberately directing attention to the present moment, observing thoughts, emotions and sensations without judging them.
Mood
A background emotional tone, longer-lasting and more diffuse than an emotion, that colours how we experience what happens.
Motivational interviewing
A style of clinical conversation, developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick, that aims to strengthen a person's motivation to change based on their own reasons, without lecturing.

N

Neurodevelopmental disorders
A group of conditions that appear in the early stages of development and affect personal, social, academic or occupational functioning: autism, ADHD, intellectual disability or learning and language disorders, among others.
Neuron
A specialised nervous system cell that receives, integrates and transmits information through electrical and chemical signals.
Neuroplasticity
The nervous system's ability to change its structure and function with experience, learning or after an injury.
Neuropsychology
The discipline that studies the relationship between the brain and mental functions and behaviour, often through people with brain damage.
Neuroticism
A personality trait reflecting a tendency to experience negative emotions such as anxiety, sadness or irritability more often and more intensely.
Neurotransmitter
A chemical released by neurons at the synapse to pass a signal to another cell.
Nocebo effect
The appearance of symptoms or adverse effects due to negative expectations, without the treatment causing them.

O

Obedience
Compliance with the orders of an authority figure.
Object permanence
The understanding that objects continue to exist even when they cannot be seen, heard or touched.
Observational learning
Learning that occurs by watching other people's behaviour and its consequences, without having to practise it.
Obsession
An intrusive, repetitive thought, image or urge that the person experiences as unwanted and that causes anxiety or distress, such as fear of contamination or of causing harm by accident.
Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)
A disorder characterised by obsessions (intrusive thoughts causing anxiety) and compulsions (repetitive acts to neutralise it), which take up time and cause distress.
Operant conditioning
Learning in which the likelihood of a behaviour changes according to its consequences: it rises when followed by reinforcement and falls when followed by punishment.
Optimism bias
The tendency to believe we are less likely than others to suffer problems (illness, accidents, divorce) and more likely to experience good things.

P

Panic attack
A sudden episode of intense fear that peaks within minutes, with symptoms such as palpitations, breathlessness, dizziness, trembling, a sense of unreality or fear of dying or losing control.
Pareidolia
The tendency to perceive meaningful shapes, especially faces, in ambiguous stimuli such as clouds, sockets or building fronts.
Perception
The process by which the brain organises and interprets sensory information to make sense of it.
Personality
A relatively stable pattern of thoughts, emotions and behaviours that characterises a person and distinguishes them from others.
Personality disorder
A persistent, inflexible pattern of experiencing, thinking and relating that departs from what is expected, causes distress or significant problems and appears from adolescence or early adulthood.
Personality trait
A relatively stable tendency to think, feel and behave in a certain way, shown across different situations.
Phobia
Intense, disproportionate fear of a specific object or situation (animals, heights, blood, flying), leading to avoidance and causing distress or limitation.
Placebo effect
Improvement after a treatment with no active ingredient, due to expectations, context and the ritual of care.
Positive psychology
A movement launched by Martin Seligman in the late 1990s to study wellbeing, strengths and what makes life worth living scientifically.
Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)
A disorder that can follow a traumatic event, with intrusive memories or nightmares in which it is relived, avoidance of reminders and a persistent sense of threat (startle, hypervigilance).
Prejudice
An attitude, usually negative, towards a person because they belong to a group.
Procedural memory
Memory for how to do things, such as riding a bike or tying shoelaces.
Procrastination
Voluntarily delaying a task one intends to do, despite knowing it is harmful.
Prosopagnosia
Difficulty recognising faces, even of familiar people, with the rest of vision intact.
Psychiatric medication
A medicine that acts on the nervous system to treat mental symptoms or disorders: antidepressants, anxiolytics, antipsychotics, mood stabilisers or hypnotics.
Psychoanalysis
A theory of mind and therapeutic method created by Sigmund Freud in the late 19th century, focused on the unconscious, inner conflicts and the influence of childhood.
Psychological trauma
The psychological impact of an event that threatens one's own or others' life or physical or sexual integrity, such as an accident, an assault, war or disaster.
Psychometrics
The discipline concerned with building and evaluating psychological tests to measure abilities, traits or symptoms.
Psychosis
A state in which contact with reality is partly lost, with delusions, hallucinations or very disorganised thinking.
Psychotherapy
Treatment of psychological problems through the relationship and conversation with a trained professional, using research-based techniques.
Punishment
In operant conditioning, a consequence that reduces the likelihood of a behaviour being repeated.
Pygmalion effect
A phenomenon whereby another person's expectations, such as a teacher's or a boss's, influence the performance of the person receiving them, in the expected direction.

R

Randomised controlled trial
A study in which participants are randomly assigned to groups that do or do not receive an intervention, to compare outcomes.
Reinforcement
In operant conditioning, a consequence that increases the likelihood of a behaviour being repeated.
Reliability
In psychometrics, the degree to which a test measures consistently: it gives similar results when repeated or across its parts.
REM sleep
A sleep stage characterised by rapid eye movements, high brain activity and near-total muscle relaxation.
Replication (crisis)
Replication means repeating a study to see whether the same result is obtained.
Resilience
The capacity to adapt well and recover in the face of adversity, trauma or major stress.
Rumination
A tendency to go over problems, their causes and consequences repetitively and passively, without reaching solutions.

S

Schema
A mental structure that organises knowledge about a topic and guides how we perceive, remember and interpret information.
Schizophrenia
A serious mental disorder that usually begins in late adolescence or early adulthood, with psychotic symptoms (delusions, hallucinations), disorganised thinking and symptoms such as lack of motivation or withdrawal.
Self-actualisation
For Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers, the tendency to develop one's potential and become fully oneself.
Self-compassion
Treating oneself with the kindness we would show a friend in a hard moment, recognising that suffering and mistakes are part of human experience.
Self-concept
The set of ideas and beliefs a person holds about themselves: what they are like, what they can do, what roles they have.
Self-efficacy
A person's confidence in their ability to successfully perform a specific task.
Self-esteem
A person's overall positive or negative evaluation of themselves.
Sensitive period
A stage of development in which certain experiences have a particularly strong effect, such as exposure to the sounds of a language or vision with both eyes in the early years.
Serotonin
A neurotransmitter involved in regulating mood, sleep, appetite, pain and digestion.
Social anxiety (social phobia)
Intense fear of social situations where one may be observed or evaluated, such as speaking in public, eating in front of others or meeting people, for fear of embarrassment or judgement.
Social identity
The part of one's self-concept that comes from belonging to groups (nationality, profession, team, religion).
Social skills
Learned behaviours that allow us to relate to others effectively and satisfyingly: starting a conversation, listening, expressing opinions, asking, saying no or resolving conflicts.
Social support
Emotional, practical or informational help we receive from others, and the sense that we can count on them.
Somatisation
The expression of psychological distress through physical symptoms, such as pain, tiredness or digestive complaints, without a physical illness fully explaining them.
Stereotype
A generalised belief about the characteristics of a group's members.
Stigma
The set of prejudices, stereotypes and discrimination towards a group, such as people with mental health problems.
Stress
The body's response to a demand perceived as taxing or threatening, with physical and psychological arousal.
Subjective wellbeing
A person's evaluation of their own life, including overall satisfaction and the balance between pleasant and unpleasant emotions.
Synaesthesia
A phenomenon in which stimulating one sense automatically produces an experience in another: seeing colours when hearing music or linking each letter with a colour.
Synapse
The point of communication between two neurons, or between a neuron and another cell, where the electrical signal is converted into a chemical one through neurotransmitters.
System 1 and System 2
A metaphor popularised by Daniel Kahneman to describe two modes of thinking: one fast, automatic and intuitive (System 1) and another slow, deliberate and effortful (System 2).

T

Temperament
Individual differences in emotional reactivity, activity and self-regulation that appear from the first months of life and have a biological basis.
Theory of mind
The ability to attribute to others mental states (beliefs, desires, intentions) different from one's own and to use them to understand and predict their behaviour.
Tolerance
The need for increasing doses of a substance to get the same effect, or a reduced effect from the same dose.
Treatment adherence
The extent to which a person follows the treatment agreed with professionals: taking medication, doing exercises or attending check-ups.

U

Unconscious
Broadly, the set of mental processes that influence behaviour without our being aware of them.

V

Validity
The extent to which a test actually measures what it claims to, or a study supports the conclusions it states.
Variable
In research, a characteristic that can take different values and is observed or measured, such as age, anxiety level or reaction time.

W

Withdrawal
The set of physical and psychological symptoms that appear when cutting down or stopping a substance the body has become dependent on.
Working memory
The system that holds and manipulates for a few seconds the information we are using, such as a phone number while dialling it.

Z

Zone of proximal development
Lev Vygotsky's concept: the gap between what a child can do alone and what they can do with help from an adult or a more capable peer.

Popular definitions: they do not replace assessment by a professional.

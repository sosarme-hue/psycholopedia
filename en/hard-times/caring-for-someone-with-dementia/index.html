Caring for someone with dementia

Watching a loved one change: carer strain, how to communicate better and what support exists.

According to the World Health Organization, in 2021 there were about 57 million people with dementia worldwide, and Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause. Most live at home, cared for by relatives, usually women: daughters and wives. Caring for someone with dementia is one of the most demanding forms of care, because it lasts years, progresses and changes the relationship with the loved one.

Grieving before the loss

Many carers feel they are losing their relative little by little: they stop recognising them, remembering their shared history or being who they were. This is called anticipatory grief, a pain that begins long before death and often goes unrecognised. Feeling sadness, anger, guilt or even wishing it would all end is common and understandable.

What wears carers down most

It is usually not the physical help that is most tiring but the changes in behaviour: agitation, repeated questions, suspicion, wandering or night-time sleeplessness. Carers of people with dementia have more depression, anxiety and health problems than the general population. The REACH II trial, led by Steven Belle, showed that a programme combining information, training in managing behaviours, self-care for carers and social support improved carers' quality of life and reduced depression.

Communicating better

  • Short sentences, one idea at a time. Speak slowly, face to face and calmly.
  • Do not argue or constantly correct. If they think it is another year or that their mother is alive, it is often better to respond to the emotion ("you miss her") than to the facts.
  • Look for the cause of agitation: pain, hunger, needing the toilet, noise or tiredness.
  • Stable routines and a simple environment, with enough light and few distractions.

What tends to help carers

  • Asking for and accepting respite. Day centres, home help and short stays in care homes allow carers to rest. In many countries they are arranged through social services.
  • Family associations, such as Alzheimer's societies, offer training, support groups and guidance.
  • Looking after your own health: keeping medical appointments, exercise and some time of your own.
  • Planning ahead legal and financial decisions, while the person can still take part.

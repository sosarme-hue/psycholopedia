Feeling that you don't stand out

Comparing yourself with the best is a recipe for feeling small. Why it happens and how to measure life by a different yardstick.

Many people feel they are not special: that at work, in their hobbies or in their family there is always someone brighter, better-looking or more successful. That feeling can weigh heavily, especially at a time when social media shows the best of other people's lives every day.

Why it happens

Leon Festinger proposed in 1954 that we evaluate ourselves by comparing ourselves with others, especially when there is no objective measure. The problem is whom we compare ourselves with. Social media puts in front of us the few who stand out, not the majority, and shows their best moments. We also overestimate how much others notice us: in Thomas Gilovich's experiments on the spotlight effect, participants thought twice as many people had noticed their embarrassing T-shirt as actually had.

Standing out is, by definition, rare

In almost any ability, most people are close to the average. That is a matter of statistics, not a personal failure. The setting also changes the yardstick: Herbert Marsh showed, with the "big fish, little pond" effect, that the same student rates themselves lower in a class of high achievers than in an ordinary class, even with the same abilities.

What tends to help

  • Choosing the comparison. Comparing yourself with yourself a year ago is usually more useful and fairer than comparing yourself with the best in the world.
  • Limiting exposure to what hurts. Noticing which accounts or situations leave you feeling worse and cutting them back.
  • Thinking in terms of values, not rank. Being a good friend, parent or neighbour does not depend on beating anyone.
  • Practising self-compassion. Kristin Neff has shown that treating yourself with the kindness you would show a friend is associated with more motivation and less anxiety than harsh self-criticism.
  • Seeking mastery, not shine. Getting better at something you enjoy brings satisfaction even if you are never the best.
If the feeling of being worthless is constant, comes with sadness or isolation, or leads you to avoid people, it may be more than an unfavourable comparison and is worth discussing with a professional.

References

  1. Festinger, L. (1954). A theory of social comparison processes. Human Relations, 7(2), 117–140.
  2. Gilovich, T., Medvec, V. H. y Savitsky, K. (2000). The spotlight effect in social judgment. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78(2), 211–222.
  3. Marsh, H. W. y Parker, J. W. (1984). Determinants of student self-concept: is it better to be a relatively large fish in a small pond even if you don't learn to swim as well? Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 47(1), 213–231.
  4. Neff, K. D. (2003). Self-compassion: an alternative conceptualization of a healthy attitude toward oneself. Self and Identity, 2(2), 85–101.

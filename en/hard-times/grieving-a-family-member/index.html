Grieving the death of a family member

Losing a parent, partner or sibling changes your life. What is normal in grief, what helps and when to seek support.

The death of a spouse ranked first on Holmes and Rahe's scale of stressful events, and that of a close family member was among the top five. Grief is the natural response to such a loss, and it has no fixed timetable.

What is normal

In the first weeks and months, intense sadness, crying, disbelief, anger, guilt about what was or was not done, insomnia, loss of appetite and the sense of hearing or seeing the person who died are common. There are no "stages" to go through in order: grief comes and goes, sometimes in waves triggered by a smell, a song or a date. Significant dates such as birthdays, Christmas or the anniversary tend to be especially hard.

Moving between loss and life

Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut proposed the dual process model: in healthy grief, the person alternates between facing the loss (remembering, crying, missing) and facing the life that goes on (handling paperwork, resuming activities, learning tasks the other person used to do). Both are necessary, and taking a break from pain is not a betrayal of the person who died. It is also now known that keeping a continuing bond with them, through memories, objects or rituals, is compatible with adapting.

When to seek help

Most people gradually adapt with the support of those around them. About one in ten develops prolonged grief: a year after the loss, yearning and pain remain so intense that they prevent a normal life. There is specific psychological treatment that works. It is also worth seeking help if depression appears, if you drink to cope or if you have thoughts of not wanting to live.

What tends to help

  • Allowing yourself to feel and talking about the person who died, including with children, using clear words suited to their age.
  • Accepting practical help with meals, paperwork or care in the first weeks.
  • Keeping basic routines of sleep, food and movement.
  • Using rituals, religious or not, that give memory a place.
  • Avoiding big irreversible decisions in the first months, if possible.

Hard times

Intimate partner violence

It does not always leave visible marks: how to recognise control and abuse, why it is hard to leave and how to help someone.

If you are in danger now, call your local emergency number. In Spain, 016 is free, open 24 hours, available in more than 50 languages and does not show on the phone bill. In the UK, the National Domestic Abuse Helpline is 0808 2000 247; in the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233. See also Where to get help.

The World Health Organization estimates that around one in four women who have had a partner have experienced physical or sexual violence from that partner during their lifetime. Intimate partner violence mainly affects women, but it can also be directed at men and occur in same-sex couples. It is a major health problem, with physical and psychological consequences and effects on children who witness it.

Beyond physical violence

Violence is not only physical. It includes psychological violence (insults, humiliation, threats, making the other person feel mad or guilty), sexual violence, economic abuse (controlling money, preventing work) and control: checking the phone, deciding whom the person talks to or how they dress, cutting them off from friends and family. The sociologist Evan Stark called this pattern coercive control, in which the aim is to dominate the other person's life. It can exist without a single blow and be just as destructive.

Why it is so hard to leave

From outside, people often ask "why don't they leave?". There are many reasons: fear of the abuser's reaction, financial dependence, children, isolation, shame, hope that the person will change and the erosion of self-esteem after years of contempt. Moreover, the time of separation can be the most dangerous: a study by Jacquelyn Campbell on femicides found that leaving a controlling abuser was one of the factors associated with the risk of homicide. That is why it is best to plan leaving with specialist help.

Warning signs

  • You are afraid of their reaction and change what you do so as not to make them angry.
  • They control your phone, your money, your schedule or whom you see.
  • They insult you, humiliate you or make you feel everything is your fault.
  • They pressure or force you into sex.
  • They have broken things, pushed, hit or threatened you, even if they apologise afterwards.

How to help someone

  • Believe them and do not judge. Tell them it is not their fault and they are not alone.
  • Do not pressure them to leave: the decision is theirs and forcing it can push them away.
  • Stay in touch, because isolation is part of the abuse.
  • Share information about resources: specialist helplines also support family and friends.
  • In immediate danger, call the emergency number.

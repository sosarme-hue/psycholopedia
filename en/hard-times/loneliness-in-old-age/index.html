Loneliness in old age

When friends are gone and days grow long: what the data say, why it matters for health and what helps.

In Spain, according to the National Statistics Institute, more than two million people aged over 65 live alone, most of them women. Living alone is not the same as feeling lonely: many people live alone and are well connected. Unwanted loneliness is the painful gap between the relationships one has and those one would like to have.

Does it increase with age?

Not in a simple way. Maike Luhmann and Louise Hawkley analysed data from more than 16,000 Germans and found that loneliness is high in young adults, low in midlife and rises again especially from around 75 to 80. What explains it is not age itself but what tends to come with it: widowhood, loss of friends, problems with mobility, sight or hearing, and low income.

Why it matters for health

Persistent loneliness is associated with more depression, poorer sleep, greater cognitive decline and higher mortality. An often overlooked factor is hearing: hearing loss makes conversation harder, leads people to avoid gatherings and isolates them. Checking hearing and using hearing aids when needed is a simple step with important social effects.

What works best

Reviews of interventions against loneliness show that those that work best take into account what the person wants, are based on shared activities with a purpose, such as workshops, volunteering or community gardens, and address the thoughts that keep loneliness going, such as "I'm a bother" or "nobody will want to see me". Telephone befriending programmes or volunteers who visit at home also help those who cannot go out.

What tends to help

  • Taking the first step: calling an old friend, joining an activity at a senior centre or local group, saying hello to neighbours.
  • Looking after the senses and mobility: glasses, hearing aids and exercise to keep getting out.
  • Learning to use a phone or video calls with help from relatives or local courses.
  • Asking about local resources at the town hall, council or health centre: many have befriending schemes.
  • If you are a relative or neighbour: a regular call, a short visit or an invitation for coffee can change someone's week.

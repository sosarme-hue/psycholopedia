Losing your job

Unemployment takes away more than income: it also removes structure, contacts and purpose. What we know about its impact and what helps.

Losing a job is one of the events that most affect wellbeing. A meta-analysis by Karsten Paul and Klaus Moser, covering more than 300 studies, found that unemployed people have more symptoms of depression and anxiety, lower self-esteem and more distress than people in work. About a third of unemployed people have significant psychological problems, twice the rate among the employed. Importantly, mental health improves when people return to work, which shows that unemployment is not just the result of poorer health beforehand.

What is lost besides the salary

In the 1930s, the psychologist Marie Jahoda studied an Austrian village, Marienthal, where the only factory had closed. She observed that work provides, besides money, other things that are less visible: a time structure, contacts outside the family, a sense of contributing to something, a place in society and regular activity. When these disappear, many people feel lost even if they have an income. That is why the loss often hurts even those who receive good redundancy pay.

The first weeks

It is normal to feel anger, shame, fear or even relief. Many people hide it, which adds isolation. It helps to tell those close to you early on and to separate the loss from your personal worth: redundancies often depend on economic decisions that have nothing to do with how well you worked.

What tends to help

  • Keeping a routine. Getting up at a set time, getting dressed and planning the day protects sleep and mood.
  • Treating the search as a task with limits. Setting aside specific hours for it and leaving time for other things stops the whole day revolving around it.
  • Looking after relationships and exercise. They are two of the factors that most protect against the distress of unemployment.
  • Seeking practical support. Employment services, careers guidance and training programmes reduce uncertainty. Job-search programmes that also work on confidence and coping have been shown to improve re-employment and mental health.
  • Reviewing finances early. Financial uncertainty is one of the biggest sources of anxiety; knowing the figures, even bad ones, usually brings relief.
If weeks go by and persistent sadness, insomnia, drinking to cope or thoughts that life is not worth living appear, it is worth talking to your GP.

References

  1. Paul, K. I. y Moser, K. (2009). Unemployment impairs mental health: Meta-analyses. Journal of Vocational Behavior, 74(3), 264–282.
  2. Jahoda, M. (1982). Employment and Unemployment: A Social-Psychological Analysis. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.
  3. Vinokur, A. D., Schul, Y., Vuori, J. y Price, R. H. (2000). Two years after a job loss: long-term impact of the JOBS program on reemployment and mental health. Journal of Occupational Health Psychology, 5(1), 32–47.

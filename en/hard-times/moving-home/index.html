Hard times

Moving home

Moving house or city seems a practical matter, but it breaks routines and ties. Why it is hard and how to make it easier.

Moving home is often seen as a matter of boxes and paperwork, but psychologically it is much more. The surroundings, daily routes, neighbours and familiar shops all change at once and, if the move is far away, so do one's circle of friends and nearby family. Even when it is wanted, such as for a better job or a bigger home, it demands an effort of adjustment.

What is lost without noticing

Much of our wellbeing rests on automatic routines: the way to work, the bakery, greeting a neighbour. When we move, all of that has to be rebuilt, and for a while daily life takes more energy. We also lose "place attachment", the emotional bond with a neighbourhood or a home where important things happened. It is normal to feel some sadness or nostalgia even when the change is for the better.

Children and older people

For children, a single move is not usually a problem, but very frequent changes of home and school are associated, in large cohort studies such as a Danish one with more than a million people, with more mental health problems in adulthood. Probably each change breaks friendships and support. For older people, moving into a care home or a child's home can bring disorientation, sadness and loss of independence, especially if they have not taken part in the decision.

What tends to help

  • Saying goodbye. Small rituals, such as a last dinner or a walk around the neighbourhood, help close a chapter.
  • Rebuilding routines quickly. Meal and sleep times, exercise, coffee in the same place: they give a sense of control.
  • Exploring the new area on foot and looking for activities with regular contact, such as a club, volunteering or classes.
  • Keeping earlier ties with planned calls or visits, without letting them stop you forming new ones.
  • With children, preparing them in advance, letting them choose something for their new room and helping them stay in touch with friends.
  • With older people, involving them in the decision and bringing cherished objects and furniture.

Hard times

Not fitting in

Feeling out of place in a country, a job or a group really hurts. Why belonging matters so much and how to build it.

Arriving in a new country, starting a job where everyone knows each other, changing school or feeling you do not fit into your own family or town: the feeling of being on the outside is one of the most painful there is.

The need to belong

In 1995, Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary reviewed hundreds of studies and concluded that the need to belong is a basic human motivation: we need stable, close relationships almost as much as food or rest. When it is lacking, anxiety, sadness and health problems appear.

Exclusion hurts

Kipling Williams created a computer game, Cyberball, in which participants tossed a virtual ball with two other players. When the others stopped passing it to them, people felt bad, even though they knew the others were programs and it did not matter at all. Even a few minutes of exclusion lower mood, self-esteem and sense of control. Prolonged exclusion, such as that experienced by some migrants, minorities or victims of bullying, has much larger effects. Discrimination is consistently associated with poorer mental and physical health.

Fitting in without ceasing to be yourself

For people who move country, the psychologist John Berry described several ways of adapting. The one that usually works best is integration: taking part in the new society while keeping the language, customs and ties of origin. Giving up one's identity entirely or isolating oneself completely tend to be associated with more distress. Integration, however, does not depend only on the person: it requires a welcoming society.

What tends to help

  • Seeking out both "similar" and "different" people. Groups of people in the same situation provide support; mixing with locals opens doors.
  • Learning the local language and codes gradually, being patient with yourself.
  • Having a role. Helping with something, however small, gives a sense of belonging faster than waiting to be invited.
  • Remembering that it takes time. Making real friends requires many shared hours; taking a while to fit in is not a personal failure.
  • Asking for help if there is bullying or discrimination. No one has to put up with it in silence.

References

  1. Baumeister, R. F. y Leary, M. R. (1995). The need to belong: desire for interpersonal attachments as a fundamental human motivation. Psychological Bulletin, 117(3), 497–529.
  2. Williams, K. D., Cheung, C. K. T. y Choi, W. (2000). Cyberostracism: effects of being ignored over the Internet. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 79(5), 748–762.
  3. Pascoe, E. A. y Smart Richman, L. (2009). Perceived discrimination and health: a meta-analytic review. Psychological Bulletin, 135(4), 531–554.
  4. Berry, J. W. (1997). Immigration, acculturation, and adaptation. Applied Psychology, 46(1), 5–34.

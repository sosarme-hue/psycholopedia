Hard times

Receiving a serious diagnosis

Cancer, heart disease or a neurological illness: what tends to happen inside after the news and what helps you cope.

Few sentences change a life as much as "we have the results". A diagnosis of cancer, serious heart disease or a neurological illness suddenly brings uncertainty, fear of suffering and one's own mortality to the fore. It is normal to feel numb, disbelieving, angry or deeply sad, sometimes all on the same day.

What tends to happen in the mind

Many people describe the first hours as if they were watching from outside. Little of what is said in the consultation is retained: intense emotion reduces the ability to pay attention and remember. Then come weeks of ups and downs, with days of hope and days of distress. A meta-analysis by Alex Mitchell of almost a hundred studies of people with cancer found that around one in six met criteria for depression and one in five for adjustment disorder. In other words, most people suffer but do not become psychologically ill, and those who do can be treated.

Getting information at your own pace

Not everyone wants to know everything at once. Some people need figures and details; others prefer to go step by step. It helps to write down your questions, take someone with you, ask for anything you do not understand to be explained and ask what will happen in the coming weeks. Searching the internet alone in the middle of the night usually increases distress: it is better to use reliable sources and discuss them with the medical team.

Growing in spite of everything

Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun described post-traumatic growth: many people who go through a serious illness say, over time, that they value relationships more, have changed their priorities or feel stronger. It is not compulsory and does not mean the illness is "good", but it shows that suffering and meaning can coexist.

What tends to help

  • Letting others support you. Tell those close to you what kind of help is useful: company at appointments, practical tasks or simply listening.
  • Keeping some normality. Holding on to routines and small enjoyable activities gives a sense of control.
  • Specialist psychological care. Psycho-oncology and health psychology offer support to patients and families. Many cancer charities offer free emotional support; in Spain, the Spanish Association Against Cancer runs Infocáncer (900 100 036).
  • Looking after sleep and the body as far as the illness allows: walking, eating what you can, resting.
If sadness or anxiety stops you sleeping, eating or following treatment for weeks, or thoughts of not wanting to live appear, tell your doctor: it can be treated.

References

  1. Mitchell, A. J. et al. (2011). Prevalence of depression, anxiety, and adjustment disorder in oncological, haematological, and palliative-care settings: a meta-analysis of 94 interview-based studies. The Lancet Oncology, 12(2), 160–174.
  2. Tedeschi, R. G. y Calhoun, L. G. (2004). Posttraumatic growth: conceptual foundations and empirical evidence. Psychological Inquiry, 15(1), 1–18.
  3. Kessels, R. P. C. (2003). Patients' memory for medical information. Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine, 96(5), 219–222.

Social isolation

Having few contacts or feeling lonely affects health as much as other well-known risk factors. How people get there and how to get out.

Social isolation is having few contacts with other people; loneliness is feeling that our relationships are not enough. One can be surrounded by people and feel lonely, or live alone and feel connected. Both, however, are associated with poorer physical and mental health.

A health risk

A meta-analysis by Julianne Holt-Lunstad, covering nearly 150 studies, found that people with stronger social relationships had about a 50% greater likelihood of still being alive during follow-up. Isolation is associated with more cardiovascular disease, depression, cognitive decline and mortality. In 2023 the World Health Organization set up a commission on social connection to treat it as a public health problem.

How people become isolated

It is rarely a decision. It usually comes gradually: a move, retirement, a partner's death, an illness that makes going out hard, deafness or caring for a relative. Add to that a vicious circle described by John Cacioppo: loneliness puts the mind on guard against rejection, the person reads social signals more negatively, withdraws, and isolation grows.

What tends to help

  • Starting small. A message to someone you lost touch with, greeting a neighbour or having a coffee in the same café each week.
  • Looking for activities with repeated contact. Relationships grow from seeing people often: classes, volunteering, walking groups, choirs, associations.
  • Reviewing the thoughts that hold you back. Christopher Masi's meta-analysis found that the most effective interventions against loneliness were those that worked on the tendency to expect rejection, rather than those that only offered more opportunities for contact.
  • Dealing with what makes going out difficult, such as hearing loss, pain or lack of transport.
  • Asking at your health centre. Many practices work with community resources and can point you towards local activities.

References

  1. Holt-Lunstad, J., Smith, T. B. y Layton, J. B. (2010). Social relationships and mortality risk: a meta-analytic review. PLoS Medicine, 7(7), e1000316.
  2. Cacioppo, J. T. y Hawkley, L. C. (2009). Perceived social isolation and cognition. Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 13(10), 447–454.
  3. Masi, C. M., Chen, H.-Y., Hawkley, L. C. y Cacioppo, J. T. (2011). A meta-analysis of interventions to reduce loneliness. Personality and Social Psychology Review, 15(3), 219–266.

Hard times

Suicidal thoughts

Thinking about dying is more common than people think, and it can be talked about. What we know, which signs matter and how to ask for or offer help.

Many people have at some point thought they would not want to go on living, especially at times of great pain. Having such thoughts does not mean being mad or weak: it usually means that suffering has, for a while, outstripped one's resources for coping. Suicidal thoughts can change and pass, and help works.

Some figures

Around 700,000 people die by suicide worldwide each year, according to the World Health Organization. In Spain it is the leading cause of non-natural death, ahead of road accidents, with around 4,000 deaths a year. For each death there are many more attempts, and many more people who have considered it. Most people who die by suicide were going through a mental disorder, a severe life crisis or both.

Talking about it does not cause it

A common fear is that asking someone whether they are thinking of suicide will put the idea in their head. Studies show the opposite: asking directly and calmly does not increase risk and often brings relief, because the person feels they can talk about what is happening. Questions such as "Have you thought about ending your life?" are appropriate.

Signs to watch for

  • Talking about wanting to die, being a burden or having no way out.
  • Saying goodbye, giving away treasured possessions or unexpectedly putting affairs in order.
  • Withdrawing, drinking more or using other drugs, or a sudden change in behaviour.
  • Sudden calm after a period of great distress, with no apparent reason.

What helps

  • Telling someone. A trusted person, your GP or a helpline. You do not need to be sure in order to ask for help.
  • Making a safety plan. This is a short list, ideally prepared with a professional, of your own warning signs, what helps you calm down, whom to call and how to make your surroundings safer. It has been shown to reduce suicidal behaviour.
  • Putting means out of reach. Removing or storing outside the home stockpiled medicines or other means reduces risk, because many crises are brief.
  • Treating what lies underneath. Depression, alcohol use, chronic pain or a desperate life situation all have treatment or support.

If someone tells you

Listen without judging, without minimising and without promising to keep it secret if there is danger. Ask directly, stay with the person if the risk is immediate and help them contact a professional or emergency services.

If you are thinking of ending your life or are worried about someone, ask for help now. In the UK and Ireland you can call Samaritans on 116 123; in the United States and Canada, call or text 988; in Spain, call 024. In an emergency, call your local emergency number or go to the nearest emergency department. Talking to someone you trust is a first step. More helplines.

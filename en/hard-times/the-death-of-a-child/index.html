Facing the death of a child

It is one of the most devastating losses there is. What parents and siblings go through, what not to say and what can help.

The death of a child, at any age, breaks the natural order of life. Many parents say there are no words to describe it; there is not even a word, like "widow" or "orphan", for those who suffer it. This page does not aim to explain such pain, but to offer some ideas that research and the experience of many families have shown to be helpful.

An especially intense grief

Studies show that grief for a child tends to be more intense and longer-lasting than other grief, and is associated with a higher risk of depression, anxiety and physical health problems for years. A large Danish study found increased mortality among mothers in the following years. That does not mean it cannot be lived with: many parents say that, over time, the pain does not disappear but changes shape and leaves room for other things. Death during pregnancy or shortly after birth, perinatal loss, is also a real grief that deserves recognition.

The couple and the siblings

Each partner may grieve differently: one needs to talk and the other to act; one cries and the other seems composed. These differences can be read as a lack of pain, and it is worth knowing they are normal. Contrary to what is often said, it has not been shown that most couples end up separating. Siblings, meanwhile, suffer both the loss and overwhelmed parents; sometimes they feel guilty or that they must be "perfect". They need honest information, room for their grief and continued attention.

What can help

  • Saying the child's name. Many parents are grateful when people talk about their child, remember their name and important dates.
  • Bereaved parents' groups. Sharing with people who have been through the same is, for many families, one of the most valuable forms of help.
  • Professional support when pain stops you functioning, when thoughts of not wanting to live appear, or when a parent or sibling withdraws.
  • Rituals and continuing bonds: a place for remembrance, an object, an activity in their name.
  • Returning to work and routine at your own pace, with flexibility if possible.

For those offering support

Phrases such as "they're in a better place", "time heals everything" or "you have other children" usually hurt, even when said with love. It is better to say "I'm so sorry", "I don't know what to say, but I'm here" or simply to listen. Practical help, such as bringing food, handling paperwork or looking after the other children, and staying present when the months pass and others drift away, is what people remember most.

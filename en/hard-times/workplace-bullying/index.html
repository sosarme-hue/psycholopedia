Workplace bullying

When work becomes a hostile place: how to tell bullying from conflict, what effects it has and how to protect yourself.

The German psychologist Heinz Leymann, who worked in Sweden, popularised the term mobbing in the 1980s to describe a situation in which a person is systematically subjected to hostile behaviour at work: isolation, humiliation, constant criticism, rumours, removal of tasks or impossible assignments. His rule of thumb was that it occurred at least once a week for six months. A meta-analysis by Morten Birkeland Nielsen estimated that around 15% of workers experience it to some degree.

What bullying is not

Not every workplace conflict is bullying. A disagreement, a negative appraisal, a demanding manager or a one-off argument can be unpleasant without being bullying. What defines it is repetition, duration, difficulty defending oneself and that the aim, or the effect, is to push the person out or bring them down. Telling the difference helps you choose the right response.

Its effects

Follow-up studies show that workplace bullying precedes mental health problems, not just the other way round: it increases the risk of depression, anxiety, insomnia and symptoms similar to post-traumatic stress, as well as sick leave. One of its most damaging consequences is that the person starts to doubt themselves and wonder whether they deserve it.

What tends to help

  • Keeping a record. Write down dates, events, exact words and witnesses. Save emails and messages.
  • Putting things in writing. Asking for instructions and changes in duties by email creates a record.
  • Seeking support inside and outside work. Talk to trusted colleagues, worker representatives or occupational health. Many employers must have a procedure for bullying complaints, and labour inspectorates or equivalent bodies can be contacted.
  • Legal advice before making major decisions, such as leaving the job.
  • Looking after your health. Your family doctor can assess the impact on your health and, if necessary, sick leave. Talking to a psychologist helps rebuild confidence.

References

  1. Leymann, H. (1996). The content and development of mobbing at work. European Journal of Work and Organizational Psychology, 5(2), 165–184.
  2. Nielsen, M. B., Matthiesen, S. B. y Einarsen, S. (2010). The impact of methodological moderators on prevalence rates of workplace bullying: a meta-analysis. Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology, 83(4), 955–979.
  3. Nielsen, M. B. y Einarsen, S. (2012). Outcomes of exposure to workplace bullying: a meta-analytic review. Work & Stress, 26(4), 309–332.

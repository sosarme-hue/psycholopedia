Chapter 19 · Expansion · 1950–1980

The cognitive revolution

When the mind came back to the centre: memory, attention and language understood as information processing.

For much of the first half of the twentieth century, especially in the United States, behaviourism argued that psychology should deal with observable behaviour and leave mental processes aside. From the 1950s onwards, research from several different fields put the mind back at the centre. This change is called the cognitive revolution, although many historians prefer to speak of a gradual transformation.

What were people trying to understand?

How people receive, select, transform, store and retrieve information. Attention, memory, language and problem solving came to be studied as internal processes that could be inferred from behaviour.

With what methods?

Tightly controlled laboratory experiments (reaction times, recall tasks, listening with a different message in each ear), formal models and, as a novelty, computer programs that simulated how a problem is solved. The computer analogy supplied a vocabulary: input, store, capacity, processing.

Who took part?

  • George A. Miller published "The magical number seven, plus or minus two" in 1956, on the limits of immediate memory.
  • Noam Chomsky criticised the behaviourist account of language in 1959, arguing that language has a structure not learned through reinforcement alone.
  • Donald Broadbent proposed a filter model of attention in 1958.
  • Herbert Simon and Allen Newell built programs that solved logic problems.
  • Jerome Bruner and Miller founded the Harvard Center for Cognitive Studies in 1960.
  • Ulric Neisser named the field with Cognitive Psychology (1967).

Many of these ideas had European precedents: Frederic Bartlett had studied in Cambridge in the 1930s how we reconstruct memories, and Piaget and Vygotsky had been researching the development of thinking for decades.

What did it contribute?

  • Memory models such as Atkinson and Shiffrin's (1968) and Baddeley and Hitch's working memory (1974).
  • The study of heuristics and biases in judgement, with Kahneman and Tversky in the 1970s.
  • The foundations of cognitive science, joining psychology, linguistics, computing, philosophy and neuroscience.
  • Applications in education, ergonomics, interface design and cognitive therapy.

What was revised later?

The computer metaphor helped, but it also simplified. Critics said it left out emotions, the body and social and cultural context. Since the 1980s, connectionist models, embodied cognition, cognitive neuroscience and cultural psychology have broadened and corrected the approach. The "number seven" has also been qualified: working-memory capacity is now usually estimated at around four meaningful items.

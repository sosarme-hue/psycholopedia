Chapter 22 · Diversification and today · 1900–hoy

Cultural psychology and international perspectives

Most psychology studies have been carried out with Western students. What changes when the rest of the world is taken into account.

For much of the twentieth century, psychology was done in Western Europe and the United States, with participants from those countries, and presented as a science of the human mind in general. Little by little it became clear that many conclusions do not hold in the same way in other cultures, and that other traditions have a great deal to contribute.

What were people trying to understand?

Which psychological processes are common to all human beings and which depend on culture, how language, family or social organisation shape ways of thinking and feeling, and how to build a psychology that is useful for very different societies.

With what methods?

Comparison between cultures using the same instruments, ethnographic fieldwork and studies that adapt tasks to the local context. The interest was old: Wilhelm Wundt devoted the last twenty years of his life to a ten-volume Völkerpsychologie on language, myth and custom, because he thought the higher processes could not be studied in the laboratory. Lev Vygotsky and Alexander Luria argued that the mind is formed with cultural tools.

Who took part?

In the 1970s Michael Cole studied in Liberia how the Kpelle solved classification and memory problems, and showed that their performance improved when the tasks made sense in their daily lives. Geert Hofstede compared the values of IBM employees in more than fifty countries and popularised the individualism–collectivism dimension. In 1991 Hazel Markus and Shinobu Kitayama described two ways of understanding the self: independent, more common in North America, and interdependent, more common in East Asia.

In Latin America the Spanish Jesuit and psychologist Ignacio Martín-Baró, murdered in El Salvador in 1989, proposed a liberation psychology committed to the poor majority. In India, the Philippines, China and other countries "indigenous psychologies" emerged to study their own concepts, such as Japanese amae or Filipino kapwa. In 2010 Joseph Henrich and colleagues pointed out that most participants in psychology studies came from WEIRD societies (Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic), which are a minority of humanity and in many respects unrepresentative.

What did it contribute?

  • Evidence that phenomena such as some visual illusions, reasoning and fairness vary across cultures.
  • Assessment instruments adapted and validated in different languages and countries.
  • Community and health psychology more attentive to social context.
  • More diversity in samples and research teams.

What was revised later?

Cultural dimensions such as individualism and collectivism are useful, but they have also been criticised for simplifying whole countries and hiding internal variety. Some famous cultural differences have replicated less well than expected in large samples. The current challenge is to avoid two mistakes: assuming that everything observed in American students is universal and, at the other extreme, turning culture into a stereotype. Today large collaborations run the same experiments in dozens of countries at once.

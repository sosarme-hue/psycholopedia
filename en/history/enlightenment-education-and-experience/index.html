Chapter 7 · Towards a science of the mind · 1730–1810

Enlightenment, education and experience

Condillac's statue, the wild boy of Aveyron, Pinel and Pussin's moral treatment and the first blind experiment, against Mesmer's magnetism.

The Enlightenment trusted that reason and experience could improve society. That trust reached education, the treatment of the mentally ill and the way extraordinary claims were tested. One of the first controlled experiments in history, for example, dates from this period.

What were people trying to understand?

How human abilities are formed from experience, whether anyone can be educated, how to cure madness and how to tell a real effect from an imagined one.

With what methods?

The German philosopher Christian Wolff published an Empirical Psychology (1732) and a Rational Psychology (1734) that made the term common in European universities. Étienne Bonnot de Condillac imagined in 1754 a statue given the senses one at a time, starting with smell, to show that the whole of mental life could be built up from sensations.

In 1784 the King of France asked a commission that included Benjamin Franklin and the chemist Lavoisier to examine the "animal magnetism" of Franz Anton Mesmer, who claimed to cure with an invisible fluid. The commissioners blindfolded participants and led them to believe they were receiving the fluid when they were not, and vice versa. The effects appeared when people believed they were being magnetised, not when they actually were. The commission concluded that the cause was imagination. It is one of the first blind experiments and one of the first demonstrations of what we now call the placebo effect.

Who took part?

Jean-Jacques Rousseau argued in Emile (1762) that childhood has its own stages and that education should respect them, an idea that influenced educators and, much later, developmental psychology. In Paris, the physician Philippe Pinel and the hospital superintendent Jean-Baptiste Pussin, a former patient, replaced chains with care based on observation, routine and conversation: moral treatment. In England, the Quaker merchant William Tuke opened the York Retreat in 1796 on the same principles. Around 1800 the young physician Jean Itard tried to educate Victor, a boy found in the woods of Aveyron, and left detailed reports of his progress and its limits.

What did it contribute?

  • The word "psychology" became established as the name of a discipline.
  • The experiment with a deceived group and blind conditions, the basis of today's trials.
  • A change in the treatment of people with mental disorders, from restraint to observation and care.
  • The first experiences of special education: Itard's methods inspired Édouard Séguin and, later, Maria Montessori.

What was revised later?

The scene of Pinel freeing patients from their chains, painted many times, is partly a myth built afterwards; the role of Pussin and his wife, Marguerite Jubline, was decisive and long forgotten. Moral treatment lost ground in the nineteenth century as asylums became overcrowded. And Victor's case remains open: it is now thought he may have had a developmental disorder before he was abandoned, which makes it hard to draw conclusions about the effects of isolation.

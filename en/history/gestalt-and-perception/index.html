Chapter 15 · The great schools · 1912–1940

Gestalt and perception

Why we see whole forms rather than scattered patches: the school of Wertheimer, Köhler and Koffka.

Watch the lights on a sign switching on one after another: you do not see bulbs going on and off, you see one light moving. In 1912 Max Wertheimer published a study of this effect in Frankfurt, the phi phenomenon. His two main observers were Wolfgang Köhler and Kurt Koffka. The three became the core of Gestalt psychology ("form" or "configuration" in German).

What were people trying to understand?

Gestalt psychology challenged the idea that experience is a sum of elementary sensations. If apparent motion is not in any of the stimuli separately, the whole has properties that cannot be found in its parts. The question became: how does perception organise what reaches us?

With what methods?

Perception experiments with simple stimuli and careful phenomenological description: what an untrained person sees when shown a figure. The aim was clear demonstrations, often visible to anyone, rather than averages over many trials.

Who took part?

Besides the founding trio, the Dane Edgar Rubin studied the relation between figure and ground in 1915 (his famous vase that is also two faces in profile). During his stay in Tenerife between 1913 and 1920, Köhler watched chimpanzees solve problems such as reaching bananas by joining sticks or stacking boxes, and described solutions that seemed to appear all at once, through insight. Kurt Lewin carried Gestalt ideas into motivation and social psychology.

What did it contribute?

The so-called laws of perceptual organisation describe tendencies now used in graphic design, interfaces and signage:

  • Proximity: things that are close together tend to be seen together.
  • Similarity: things alike in colour, shape or size are grouped.
  • Closure: we complete incomplete outlines.
  • Continuity: we follow smooth lines rather than abrupt changes.
  • Common fate: things moving in the same direction are seen as a group.
  • Figure and ground: we separate what stands out from what lies behind.

What was revised later?

The laws described the phenomena well but explained little about why they happen; Köhler's hypothesis of "electrical fields" in the brain did not hold up. The rise of Nazism scattered the school: in 1933 Köhler published an article defending dismissed colleagues and eventually emigrated, like Wertheimer, Koffka and Lewin, to the United States. Today perception research and visual neuroscience have taken up its questions with new methods, and Gestalt demonstrations remain some of the best ways into psychology.

Gestalt psychology should not be confused with Gestalt therapy, developed decades later by Fritz and Laura Perls. They share the name and some ideas, but they are different traditions.

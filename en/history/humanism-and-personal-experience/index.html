Chapter 18 · Expansion · 1940–2000

Humanism and personal experience

Maslow, Rogers, Frankl and Bühler championed subjective experience, growth and meaning in life against psychoanalysis and behaviourism.

By the mid-twentieth century psychology was dominated by two major approaches: psychoanalysis, focused on conflict and the past, and behaviourism, focused on observable behaviour. A group of psychologists felt that both were missing something essential: the person's experience, their freedom and their capacity to grow. They presented themselves as the "third force".

What were people trying to understand?

What motivates people beyond meeting their basic needs, what makes a life fulfilling, how to help someone change without steering them from outside, and how to find meaning in extreme situations.

With what methods?

More qualitative than those of other schools: the study of life histories, phenomenological reflection and, above all, clinical practice. But there was one important innovation. Carl Rogers was among the first to record psychotherapy sessions, with patients' consent, in order to analyse them and evaluate their outcomes with rating scales and comparison groups. That opened up research on the therapeutic process.

Who took part?

In 1943 Abraham Maslow proposed a hierarchy of needs, from physiological needs to self-actualisation, and studied people he considered fully developed. Rogers developed person-centred therapy: the therapist does not interpret or advise but offers unconditional acceptance, empathy and genuineness, conditions he believed were enough for the client to grow. Charlotte Bühler, who had studied the life course in Vienna, was the first president of the Association for Humanistic Psychology. Rollo May brought European existential psychology to the United States.

The Viennese psychiatrist Viktor Frankl survived four Nazi concentration camps and in 1946 wrote Man's Search for Meaning, one of the most widely read psychology books in the world. His logotherapy holds that the search for meaning is the fundamental human motivation. Decades later, in 1998, Martin Seligman, as APA president, proposed a positive psychology that would take up humanistic themes such as well-being and strengths, but with the methods of experimental research.

What did it contribute?

  • The therapeutic relationship as an agent of change, now one of the best-documented predictors of success in any psychotherapy.
  • Active listening and empathy, taught in medicine, nursing, education and social work.
  • A view of the person centred on their resources, not only their problems.
  • Topics that later research has taken up again: well-being, meaning in life, intrinsic motivation.

What was revised later?

Maslow's hierarchy is more rigid in textbooks than in his own writing, and studies have not confirmed that needs are met in a fixed order. The famous pyramid, incidentally, was not drawn by him. Humanistic psychology was criticised for its thin empirical base and for a view of the individual very much of mid-century American society. Positive psychology, for its part, has been criticised for some commercial applications and for studies that have not replicated. Even so, the idea that the person is the protagonist of their own change sits at the heart of almost every therapy today.

"Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms, to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances" (Viktor Frankl, 1946).

Chapter 12 · Birth of the discipline · 1884–1930

Individual differences and the first assessment instruments

From Galton's measurements to the Binet–Simon scale and the US Army tests: how psychological assessment was born and how it was misused.

Wundt's laboratories looked for general laws of the mind and treated differences between people as noise. Another tradition did exactly the opposite: it made those differences its object of study. From it came statistics applied to psychology, tests and psychological assessment as we know them.

What were people trying to understand?

How people differ, whether those differences can be measured, where they come from and how to use them to make decisions at school, at work or in the army.

With what methods?

Francis Galton, Darwin's cousin, opened an anthropometric laboratory in London in 1884 where, for a few pence, anyone could have their height, strength, eyesight or reaction time measured. He gathered data on more than 9,000 people and, to analyse them, developed the ideas of correlation and regression to the mean, which his student Karl Pearson formalised. James McKeen Cattell coined the term "mental test" in 1890 for similar measures, but in 1901 a study showed that they barely predicted students' grades.

The turning point came from Paris. The French government wanted to identify children who needed extra help at school, and Alfred Binet and the physician Théodore Simon published in 1905 a scale of thirty tasks of increasing difficulty: following an object with the eyes, naming parts of the body, repeating digits, defining abstract words. Instead of simple sensations, they measured judgement, comprehension and reasoning. In 1908 they introduced mental age.

Who took part?

Charles Spearman observed in 1904 that people who do well on one test tend to do well on others and proposed a general factor, the g factor. William Stern divided mental age by chronological age in 1912, and in the United States Lewis Terman multiplied the result by one hundred and called it the intelligence quotient in his Stanford revision (1916). In 1917 Robert Yerkes led the administration of the Alpha and Beta tests to about 1.75 million US Army recruits, the first mass assessment in history. In Spain, Emilio Mira y López directed from 1919 the Institute of Vocational Guidance in Barcelona, which used tests to guide young people and workers.

What did it contribute?

  • Correlation and other statistical tools now used across all the sciences.
  • The first standardised assessment instruments, with norms and reference ages.
  • Educational and vocational guidance, and personnel selection.
  • The scientific study of intelligence, still one of the most data-rich fields in psychology.

What was revised later?

This is also one of the most uncomfortable stories in the discipline. Galton founded eugenics, and many of the testing pioneers believed intelligence was fixed and inherited. Binet explicitly warned against that idea and argued that his scale was meant to help, not to label people for life. He was ignored. The army test results, gathered without regard to language or schooling, were used in the United States to support the restrictive Immigration Act of 1924 and forced sterilisation laws. Decades later it was found that average IQ scores had risen by several points per decade in many countries (the Flynn effect), which would be impossible if they depended on heredity alone. Today's tests are designed and interpreted under far stricter ethical and statistical standards.

Binet wrote in 1909 that some philosophers claimed a person's intelligence is a fixed quantity that cannot be increased, and that one must "protest and react against this brutal pessimism".

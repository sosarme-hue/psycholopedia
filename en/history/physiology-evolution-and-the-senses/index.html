Chapter 8 · Towards a science of the mind · 1800–1880

Physiology, evolution and the study of the senses

Phrenology, Broca and "Tan", Phineas Gage and Darwin: the nineteenth century looked for the mind in the brain and in evolution.

In the nineteenth century the question of the mind changed hands. It was no longer mainly a matter for philosophers but for physiology laboratories, hospital wards and naturalists. Two ideas transformed everything: that mental functions depend on specific areas of the brain, and that the mind, like the body, is a product of evolution.

What were people trying to understand?

Whether the brain works as a whole or as a set of specialised organs, how nerves carry information from the senses and how the human mind relates to that of other animals.

With what methods?

Observation of patients with lesions and examination of their brains after death, animal experiments and comparison between species. Around 1800 the physician Franz Joseph Gall proposed that the brain is made up of organs for different faculties and that their size shows in the bumps of the skull. This phrenology became hugely popular, but Pierre Flourens, removing parts of the brain in pigeons and rabbits, showed in the 1820s that things were not so simple. Johannes Müller formulated the law of specific nerve energies: what we feel depends on the nerve stimulated, not on the stimulus. That is why a blow to the eye makes us "see stars".

Who took part?

In 1861 the surgeon Paul Broca studied the brain of a patient nicknamed "Tan", because that was the only syllable he could say, although he understood what was said to him. He found a lesion in an area of the left frontal lobe that now bears his name. In 1874 Carl Wernicke described the opposite case: patients who spoke fluently but meaninglessly and did not understand, with lesions in the temporal lobe. The physician John Harlow documented the case of Phineas Gage, a railway foreman whose frontal lobe was pierced by an iron rod in 1848; he survived, but those close to him said he was "no longer Gage".

Meanwhile, Charles Darwin published On the Origin of Species (1859) and, in 1872, The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals. In the latter he used photographs, asked observers on several continents about the expressions of the peoples they knew and compared human gestures with those of other animals. He argued that many expressions are universal and have an evolutionary history.

What did it contribute?

  • The localisation of functions in the brain, the basis of neuropsychology.
  • The method of correlating lesions with deficits, still used with imaging techniques.
  • The idea that perception is a construction of the nervous system, not a copy of the world.
  • An evolutionary perspective that inspired functionalism, comparative psychology and the study of emotion.

What was revised later?

Phrenology was a pseudoscience and was used to justify racist and class prejudice; it got one general intuition right, brain specialisation, and almost everything else wrong. Broca's and Wernicke's areas are now seen as nodes in wider networks rather than isolated language "centres". Gage's story was exaggerated over the years; the historian Malcolm Macmillan showed that he probably recovered much of his social and working life. And Darwin's theories of emotion have been qualified by research on the influence of culture.

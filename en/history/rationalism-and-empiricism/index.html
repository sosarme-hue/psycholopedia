Chapter 6 · Towards a science of the mind · 1630–1750

Rationalism and empiricism

Descartes versus Locke: are we born with ideas, or is everything learned from experience? A debate still alive in developmental psychology.

In the seventeenth century philosophy asked a question that still organises much of psychology: where does our knowledge come from? Some answered that it comes from reason and from ideas we are born with; others, that it comes from the experience of the senses.

What were people trying to understand?

How mind and body relate, where ideas come from and how they combine. Also what the passions are and how they can be governed.

With what methods?

Philosophical analysis, thought experiments and, increasingly, comparison with the mechanics of the new science. René Descartes pictured the body as a machine: stimuli pulled on filaments that opened pores in the brain and sent "animal spirits" flowing to the muscles. It is a forerunner of the idea of the reflex. The mind, by contrast, was for him a different substance, which communicated with the body only at the pineal gland. In The Passions of the Soul (1649) he described six basic emotions: wonder, love, hatred, desire, joy and sadness.

Who took part?

On the rationalist side, besides Descartes, Baruch Spinoza analysed the affects with almost geometric rigour and held that mind and body are two aspects of a single reality. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz spoke of "small perceptions" of which we are not aware, an idea that anticipates the study of the unconscious.

On the empiricist side, Thomas Hobbes explained thought as a chain of images. John Locke, in his Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690), compared the newborn's mind to a blank sheet of paper filled in by experience, and in a later edition coined the phrase "association of ideas". George Berkeley explained in 1709 how we learn to see distance by combining sight with touch and movement. David Hume proposed three laws of association: resemblance, contiguity and cause and effect. David Hartley (1749) joined the association of ideas to a theory of nerve vibrations.

What did it contribute?

  • The mind-body problem, still debated in philosophy and neuroscience.
  • A mechanical explanation of behaviour, a forerunner of the physiology of the reflex.
  • Associationism, the basis of theories of learning up to behaviourism.
  • The nature-nurture debate, now addressed with data from genetics and development.

What was revised later?

The pineal gland does not connect any soul to the body: among other things, it regulates melatonin. Cartesian dualism has few defenders among today's scientists. Nor has the "blank slate" fared well: babies arrive in the world with preferences and perceptual abilities that research has documented in detail. The current answer to the old debate is that the question was badly framed: nature and nurture do not compete, they interact.

The Irishman William Molyneux put a famous problem to Locke: if a person born blind regained their sight, would they be able to tell by sight alone a sphere from a cube they had known by touch? In 2011 a study of children in India operated on for congenital cataracts suggested that, at first, they could not.

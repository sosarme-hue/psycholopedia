Chapter 5 · Towards a science of the mind · 1450–1600

The Renaissance and new conceptions of the human being

Vives, Huarte de San Juan, Vesalius and the first time the word "psychology" was written: the human being as an object of observation.

The Renaissance put the human being at the centre. Humanists returned to the classical texts, but they also dared to contradict them. In that climate there appeared works that we now read as forerunners of psychology, and two of the most important were written by Spanish authors.

What were people trying to understand?

Not only what the soul is, but how it works in practice: how we learn, how we remember, why some people are suited to some tasks and others to others, and whether the strange behaviour of some people was the work of the devil or of an illness.

With what methods?

More observation and less authority. Juan Luis Vives (1493–1540), from Valencia but settled in Bruges, wrote in De anima et vita (1538) that knowing what the soul is matters less than knowing how it acts. He described memory and the association of ideas with examples from his own life, analysed the emotions and argued for teaching adapted to each pupil. That is why some historians call him a forerunner of modern psychology.

The Navarrese physician Juan Huarte de San Juan published The Examination of Men's Wits in 1575. His thesis was that each person has a different "wit" and that society would benefit if everyone devoted themselves to what they were most suited for. His explanations rested on the humours, but the question was modern: it is one of the first reflections on individual differences and vocational guidance. The book was translated into several languages and read all over Europe.

Who took part?

The anatomist Andreas Vesalius, in his De humani corporis fabrica (1543), corrected Galen's errors on the basis of human dissection and questioned whether the brain's ventricles housed the mental faculties. The physician Johann Weyer argued in 1563 that many women accused of witchcraft were in fact suffering from melancholic disorders and should be cared for, not burned. Michel de Montaigne turned self-observation into a literary genre in his Essays. And in 1590 Rudolph Goclenius gave a book the title Psychologia, one of the first printed uses of the word, which the Croatian humanist Marko Marulić had already used a few decades earlier.

What did it contribute?

  • A shift towards studying mental functions rather than the essence of the soul.
  • The idea that people differ in their aptitudes and that this should be taken into account in education and work.
  • An anatomy based on direct observation, which would eventually displace Galen.
  • The first voices calling for what was blamed on the devil to be treated as illness.

What was revised later?

Huarte's humoral basis did not stand the test of time, and his work also carried the prejudices of its age, for example about women's abilities. Weyer's was a minority voice: the witch hunts peaked after his time. Even so, the idea of examining people's wits to guide them would return, with other methods, in the tests of the twentieth century.

"What the soul is matters little to us; what it is like and how it works matters a great deal" (Juan Luis Vives, 1538).

Chapter 17 · Expansion · 1898–1975

Social and group psychology

Lewin, Sherif, Asch, Festinger, Milgram and Tajfel: how the presence of others changes what we think and do, and which classic experiments have been called into question.

Almost everything we do, we do surrounded by other people or thinking about them. Social psychology studies how others influence us. It grew in the shadow of the Second World War, with an urgent question: how could ordinary people take part in atrocities?

What were people trying to understand?

Why we change our opinions to match the group, when we obey orders that go against our conscience, how prejudice and conflict between groups arise, and why sometimes nobody helps a person in need.

With what methods?

Mainly laboratory and field experiments, often with some deception so that participants did not know what was being measured. In 1898 Norman Triplett noticed that cyclists rode faster when racing against others and tested it with children winding fishing reels: it is one of the first social psychology experiments. In 1936 Muzafer Sherif showed that, faced with a point of light that seems to move in a dark room, each person's judgements converged with those of the group.

Who took part?

Kurt Lewin, a refugee from Nazism, summed up his approach in a formula: behaviour depends on the person and their environment. He studied leadership styles and founded action research. In the 1950s Solomon Asch asked students to compare the lengths of lines in the presence of confederates who deliberately gave the wrong answer; about a third of the responses went along with the group. Leon Festinger proposed the theory of cognitive dissonance in 1957: when our actions clash with our beliefs, we tend to change the beliefs.

In 1963 Stanley Milgram published his study of obedience: 65% of participants went as far as giving what they believed were 450-volt shocks to another person because a researcher told them to. Henri Tajfel, a Polish Jewish survivor, showed in 1971 that simply dividing strangers into groups on a trivial basis is enough for them to favour their own, and with John Turner developed social identity theory. After the murder of Kitty Genovese in New York, John Darley and Bibb Latané found in 1968 that the more people witness an emergency, the less likely each one is to intervene.

What did it contribute?

  • Evidence that the situation influences behaviour far more than we usually think.
  • Theories of attitudes, persuasion, prejudice and intergroup relations.
  • Applications in public health, education, organisations and conflict resolution.
  • An ethical debate that led to the creation of research ethics committees.

What was revised later?

Several classics have been re-examined using the original archives. Philip Zimbardo's Stanford prison experiment (1971), widely cited, had very few participants and instructions that steered the "guards'" behaviour, and was never published as a controlled study. In the Kitty Genovese case, the number of witnesses who saw the attack and did nothing was exaggerated. Milgram's recordings show that many participants hesitated and protested, and that results varied widely between conditions. The central idea of social influence stands, but today studies are expected to be replicated with large and diverse samples before being turned into general lessons.

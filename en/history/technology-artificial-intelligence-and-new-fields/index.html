Chapter 24 · Diversification and today · 1966–hoy

Technology, artificial intelligence and new fields

From ELIZA to large language models, internet therapy and the psychology of climate change: the discipline's open frontiers.

Psychology and computing have influenced each other for more than half a century. Computers served as a metaphor for the mind, theories of learning inspired artificial neural networks, and today technology is changing how we assess, treat and do research. At the same time, new problems appear that psychology has to study.

What were people trying to understand?

Whether a machine can simulate mental processes, what that teaches us about the human mind, how to use technology to reach more people who need help and how digital life affects well-being.

With what methods?

Computational models, clinical trials of digital interventions, analysis of large amounts of data and longitudinal studies. In 1966 Joseph Weizenbaum created ELIZA at MIT, a program that imitated a Rogerian therapist by turning the user's sentences back into questions. He was surprised and troubled to find that some people, including his secretary, confided intimate matters to it, and he spent the rest of his career warning about the limits of machines.

Who took part?

In 1986 David Rumelhart, James McClelland and the PDP group proposed connectionist models: networks of simple units that learn by adjusting their connections, an idea related to Hebb's. Geoffrey Hinton, an experimental psychologist by training, developed with Rumelhart the backpropagation method that made deep learning possible, and won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2024. That line of work led to today's large language models.

In the clinic, internet-delivered cognitive behavioural therapy programmes have been tested since the 2000s, with demonstrated efficacy for anxiety and mild to moderate depression, especially when they include some human support. The 2020 pandemic made telepsychology widespread. Other growing fields are environmental and climate psychology, which studies how people perceive risks and change their habits, and the study of social media and screen use among adolescents.

What did it contribute?

  • Tools for widening access to psychological care, even in areas without professionals.
  • Computational models that allow theories of learning and perception to be tested.
  • New sources of research data, with much larger samples.
  • New applied fields: user experience, cyberpsychology, climate psychology.

What was revised later?

This chapter is still being written. Many mental health apps available in app stores have no scientific evaluation, and some collect sensitive data without adequate protection. Conversational programs based on artificial intelligence can help, but they can also give inappropriate responses in risky situations, and rigorous studies and clear rules on their use are still lacking. The effect of social media on young people's mental health is the subject of intense debate, with data pointing in different directions. Weizenbaum's warning, that technology does not replace human relationships, still stands.

If you look for psychological help in an app or an artificial intelligence program, check who developed it, whether it has been evaluated and what it does with your data. And in a crisis, always turn to a professional or to your local emergency or crisis line (024 in Spain).

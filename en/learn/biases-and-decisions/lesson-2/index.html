Biases and decisions

Why the mind takes shortcuts, the most common biases, how we perceive risk and how to decide a little better.

Lesson 2. The most common biases

1 · Lesson

Confirmation bias leads us to seek, remember and interpret information in ways that confirm what we already believe. It is probably the most influential, and it affects experts and scientists too.

The availability heuristic makes us judge how frequent something is by how easily examples come to mind. What is dramatic, recent or much talked about seems more frequent than it is. Anchoring means an initial number, even an irrelevant one, drags our later estimates towards it.

Other common biases are the framing effect (the same information presented as a gain or as a loss changes the decision), loss aversion (losing hurts more than gaining the same amount pleases) and hindsight bias ("I knew it all along"), which after an outcome makes us believe it was predictable.

2 · Example

In medicine, saying an operation has a 90% survival rate leads more people to accept it than saying it has a 10% mortality rate, although both statements say the same thing. The framing effect appears even among health professionals.

3 · Activity

Choose an opinion you feel sure about. Spend ten minutes looking for the best argument against it you can find, from a serious source. Write down whether it changes your opinion or how sure you are.

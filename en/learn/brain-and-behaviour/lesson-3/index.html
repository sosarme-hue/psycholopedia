Brain and behaviour

Neurons, brain regions, plasticity and what neuroscience can and cannot tell us.

Lesson 3. A changing brain

1 · Lesson

Brain plasticity is the nervous system's ability to change with experience. When we learn, connections that are used get stronger and those that are not get weaker. Donald Hebb summed it up: neurons that fire together wire together.

Plasticity is greatest in childhood, with sensitive periods when certain experiences (hearing a language, seeing with both eyes) have a special effect. But it continues throughout life: new synapses form and, in some areas such as the hippocampus, probably new neurons too, though the latter is still debated in humans.

Plasticity enables rehabilitation after a stroke or injury, when other areas take over part of the lost functions with intensive training. It also has a less friendly side: repeated habits, including harmful ones, become etched into circuits.

2 · Example

A study of London taxi drivers, who had to memorise thousands of streets, found they had more volume in the rear of the hippocampus than other people, and that the difference grew with years in the job. Intense learning leaves a mark on brain structure.

3 · Activity

Choose a simple new skill, such as writing with your non-dominant hand. Practise it for five minutes a day for a week and note how speed and quality change.

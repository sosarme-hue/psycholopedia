Lesson 4. Protecting yourself from misinformation

1 · Lesson

False information spreads fast because it is often novel, emotional and fits what we already believe. A study of Twitter found that false news spread further and faster than true news, and that it was mainly people, not bots, who shared it.

A useful technique is lateral reading, used by professional fact-checkers: instead of reading an unfamiliar page in depth, open other tabs to see who is behind it and what other sources say. Another is to pause before sharing: strong emotion (outrage, fear) is a signal to check.

Research on inoculation or "prebunking" shows that learning manipulation techniques in advance (fake experts, exaggerated emotions, false dilemmas) helps people recognise them later. And when correcting someone, it works better to do so respectfully, giving the correct explanation rather than just saying they are wrong.

2 · Example

A message in a family group says a home remedy cures a disease and that "doctors don't want you to know". The appeal to a secret, the promise of a cure and distrust of experts are three warning signs together. Searching the remedy's name with "hoax" or on a fact-checking site usually settles it in a minute.

3 · Activity

Next time something online outrages or frightens you, wait a minute before sharing it. Use that minute for lateral reading: who published it? What do other sources say?

Lesson 2. The unconscious and behaviour

1 · Lesson

In the early 20th century, two opposing responses to the psychology of consciousness emerged. Sigmund Freud's psychoanalysis proposed that much of mental life is unconscious and that conflicts between desires and norms influence symptoms, dreams and slips. Its method was clinical: listening to patients and finding meaning in what they said.

Behaviourism, formulated by John B. Watson in 1913, rejected both introspection and the unconscious: psychology should study only observable behaviour and its relationship with the environment. Later, B. F. Skinner developed the experimental analysis of behaviour and operant conditioning.

Both schools were highly influential and heavily criticised. From psychoanalysis remains the idea of non-conscious mental processes and the importance of the therapeutic relationship, although many of its specific hypotheses have not been confirmed. From behaviourism remain rigorous methods and behaviour modification techniques still in use.

2 · Example

Faced with a child afraid of going to school, a classical psychoanalyst would look for the conflict the fear expresses. A behaviourist would analyse what happens before and after: perhaps staying at home brings attention and relief, and that keeps the behaviour going.

3 · Activity

Think of a slip of the tongue or a curious lapse you have had. Write a "psychoanalytic" explanation and a "behaviourist" or cognitive one. Which seems easier to test?

Lesson 2. How memory works

1 · Lesson

Memory is not a single store. A classic model distinguishes sensory memory (a very brief trace of what we see or hear), working memory (what we keep active to use right now, with small capacity) and long-term memory (with no known practical limit).

Remembering has three steps: encoding (turning experience into something that can be stored), consolidation (stabilising it, partly during sleep) and retrieval (bringing it back when needed). Many lapses are not losses but retrieval failures: the memory is there, but we cannot find the cue to reach it.

Hermann Ebbinghaus showed in the 19th century that we forget quickly at first and more slowly later. And Bartlett showed that remembering is reconstructing: we fill gaps with what we expect, which explains why memories can change.

2 · Example

The "tip of the tongue" experience is a retrieval failure: you know you know the name, you recall its first letter or how many syllables it has, but it will not come. Hours later it pops up on its own. The memory had not been erased.

3 · Activity

Try to remember what you had for dinner three days ago. If it does not come, look for cues: where were you? Who with? What day of the week was it? Notice how context cues help retrieval.

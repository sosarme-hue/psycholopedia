Language and thought

How we learn to talk, what is special about human language, whether language shapes thought and what being bilingual brings.

Lesson 4. Being bilingual

1 · Lesson

More than half the world's population uses two or more languages in daily life. For a long time bilingualism was thought to confuse children; we now know that is not the case. Bilingual children may mix languages at first, but this is normal and not a sign of a problem.

They sometimes have a slightly smaller vocabulary in each language separately, but when both languages are counted together their total vocabulary is similar or larger. Language milestones appear at ages similar to those of monolingual children.

It has been claimed that bilingualism improves executive functions (switching tasks, ignoring distractions). Early studies were promising, but larger and more recent ones find small or no effects. The clearest advantages of bilingualism are different: communicating with more people, accessing other cultures and keeping family ties.

2 · Example

Many families who emigrate stop speaking their language at home for fear their children will learn the new country's language less well. Specialists usually recommend the opposite: that each person speak to the child in the language in which they express themselves with most richness and warmth.

3 · Activity

If you speak more than one language, think about when you use each: with whom, for which topics, when counting or when angry. Write whether you feel anything about you changes when you switch.

Lesson 2. A brief history

1 · Lesson

Psychology's questions are very old. Plato and Aristotle already argued about whether knowledge is innate or learned, and in the 17th century Descartes and Locke reopened the debate. But for centuries these were questions for philosophy: they were answered by reasoning, not by measuring.

The change came in the 19th century, when physiology began to measure the speed of nerves and the relationship between the intensity of a stimulus and what we feel (Weber and Fechner's psychophysics). In 1879 Wilhelm Wundt opened a laboratory devoted to experimental psychology in Leipzig, and that date is used as a symbol of the discipline's birth.

From then on, schools appeared that answered the question "what should psychology study?" in different ways: structuralism and functionalism (consciousness), psychoanalysis (the unconscious), behaviourism (observable behaviour), Gestalt (the organisation of perception), humanism (experience and personal growth) and cognitive psychology (information processing). Today psychology is more integrative: few people define themselves by a single school.

2 · Example

The same question, "why is someone afraid of dogs?", gets different answers depending on the school. Psychoanalysis would look for a displaced conflict; behaviourism, a conditioning experience (perhaps a bite); cognitive psychology, the way the person interprets danger. Psychology today combines these views and asks which one best explains the data in each case.

3 · Activity

Choose a question that interests you ("why do I procrastinate?", "why do I remember some things and not others?") and write in one line how a behaviourist would answer it and how a cognitive psychologist would. Do the answers contradict or complement each other?

Lesson 3. Significance and effect size

1 · Lesson

Many studies report that a result is "statistically significant" (for example, p < 0.05). This indicates that, if there were no real effect, data this extreme would be unlikely. It does not indicate that the effect is large, important or certain.

That is why it is worth looking at the effect size: how much something actually changes. With huge samples, tiny differences become significant. A treatment that reduces anxiety by half a point on a 100-point scale may be "significant" and at the same time irrelevant in real life.

Other useful concepts: confidence intervals (the plausible range of the effect), absolute versus relative risk ("doubles the risk" can mean going from 1 in 10,000 to 2 in 10,000) and the difference between what is statistically detectable and what is clinically relevant.

2 · Example

Headline: "Using your phone before bed raises the risk of insomnia by 30%". If the risk goes from 10% to 13%, the relative increase is 30%, but the absolute increase is 3 points. The same figure tells a very different story depending on how it is presented.

3 · Activity

Find a news item that gives a percentage increase or decrease in risk. Try to find the absolute figures in the article or the original study. Does your impression change?

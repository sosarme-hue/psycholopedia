Lesson 2. Nerves, pressure and focus

1 · Lesson

The relationship between arousal and performance has been described as an inverted U: with very little arousal we perform poorly, at an intermediate level we perform best, and with too much we perform worse again. The optimal point depends on the person and the task: a weightlifter tolerates more arousal than a golfer.

Under pressure, performance sometimes collapses abruptly ("choking"). One explanation is that the person starts consciously controlling movements that had become automatic, which makes them worse. Another is that worries take up working memory.

Pre-performance routines (fixed sequences before throwing or serving), focusing attention on external cues and the task, breathing, and reinterpreting arousal as energy rather than threat all help. Practising under simulated pressure also prepares people.

2 · Example

Many tennis players repeat the same sequence before each serve: bouncing the ball a set number of times, looking at the racket, breathing. It looks like a quirk, but it is a routine that anchors attention in the present and keeps out thoughts about the score.

3 · Activity

Design a short 20-second routine for before a pressure moment you face (an exam, a presentation, a match): one breath, one phrase and one point of focus. Practise it several times before the day.

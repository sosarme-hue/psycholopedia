The mind in groups

Social psychology: how others influence us, why we obey, how prejudice arises and when we help.

Lesson 4. Helping others

1 · Lesson

In 1964 the murder of Kitty Genovese in New York was reported as a case in which dozens of neighbours saw the attack and nobody did anything. Later research showed that this account was exaggerated, but the case inspired John Darley and Bibb Latané to study the bystander effect.

Their finding was that, in an emergency, the likelihood that any particular person helps tends to fall when more people are present. Diffusion of responsibility ("someone else will do it") plays a part, as does pluralistic ignorance: if nobody reacts, we infer that it is not serious.

Studies using real footage of street conflicts nuance the picture: in the great majority of cases, at least one person steps in. Knowing how the effect works helps break it: pointing to a specific person ("you, call the emergency number") works better than asking for help in general.

2 · Example

In a classic experiment, students filled in a questionnaire while smoke began to enter the room. Alone, most reported it quickly; with others who did not react (confederates), many kept filling in the questionnaire amid the smoke.

3 · Activity

Plan in your head what you would do if you saw someone collapse in the street: whom you would point to, what you would ask them, and which number you would call. Rehearsing the response makes it easier to act in the moment.

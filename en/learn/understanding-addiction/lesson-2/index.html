Understanding addiction

What addiction is, what happens in the brain, why some people are more vulnerable and which treatments work.

Lesson 2. The brain and addiction

1 · Lesson

Addictive substances act, through different routes, on the brain's reward system, in which dopamine plays a central role. They produce an "this matters, do it again" signal stronger than natural rewards.

With repeated use, the brain adapts. Everyday rewards lose their appeal, cues linked to use (places, people, emotions) gain strength and trigger intense craving, and the frontal lobe's control circuits become less effective. Use shifts from seeking pleasure to avoiding discomfort.

That is why addiction has often been described as a brain disease. It is a useful view for fighting stigma, but incomplete: many people overcome an addiction without formal treatment, and environment, relationships and opportunities weigh as much as brain changes.

2 · Example

During the Vietnam War, many US soldiers used heroin. On returning home, the great majority stopped without treatment. The radical change of context, away from the cues and stress of war, was key. It is a classic example of how much environment matters.

3 · Activity

Think of a habit you have wanted to stop, such as snacking or checking your phone. For three days, note which situations or emotions trigger it. Those are your "cues". Could you avoid or change any?

Lesson 2. Body and culture

1 · Lesson

For decades, it was debated whether emotions are universal or learned. Paul Ekman studied facial expressions in different cultures and proposed that some (happiness, sadness, fear, anger, disgust, surprise) are recognised worldwide. Later research has qualified that idea: recognition is better than chance, but it varies considerably by culture and context.

Today many researchers hold a middle position. There is a shared biological basis (the autonomic nervous system, certain expressions), but culture shapes when it is appropriate to show an emotion, how it is named and what it means. These are called "display rules".

The body, moreover, is not a mere loudspeaker for emotion. Bodily sensations influence how we interpret what we feel: a racing heart can be experienced as fear, excitement or falling in love, depending on the context.

2 · Example

Japanese has the word amae, describing the pleasure of depending on someone who looks after and indulges us. English has no exact word, though we recognise the experience. Languages carve up the emotional world differently.

3 · Activity

Think about your family or surroundings. Which emotions were shown easily and which were hidden? Were there differences by age or gender? Write down two "display rules" you learned without anyone explaining them.

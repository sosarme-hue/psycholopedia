Addictions

Alcohol, tobacco, other drugs and gambling: how addiction develops and what helps people recover.

Medical review Alejandro Rosàs, family doctor

Need help now? In Spain, 024 is free and open 24 hours; in an emergency call 112. Elsewhere, contact your local helpline or emergency services. This text is for general information: it cannot diagnose and does not replace seeing a professional.

What they are

We speak of addiction when use of a substance or a behaviour, such as gambling, becomes hard to control and continues despite harming health, relationships, work or finances. Clinical manuals use the terms substance use disorder and gambling disorder.

Common features include:

  • using more, or for longer, than intended;
  • failed attempts to cut down or stop;
  • intense desire or craving;
  • giving up important activities;
  • tolerance (needing more for the same effect) and withdrawal (discomfort on stopping).

In Spain, alcohol and tobacco cause the most harm to the population, followed by cannabis and sedatives. Online betting is a particular concern among young people.

What they are not

Using a substance is not the same as being addicted, though no level of alcohol or tobacco use is risk-free. Nor is addiction a vice or a moral failing: it involves changes in the brain's reward and control systems, influenced by genetics, age of first use, stress, trauma and environment.

How they are assessed

With a non-judgemental interview about what, how much, how and why the person uses. Questionnaires such as the AUDIT for alcohol are widely used in primary care. Physical consequences and other mental health problems, which are common, are also assessed.

Which interventions are studied

  • Brief intervention in primary care: a few minutes of structured conversation reduce risky drinking.
  • Motivational interviewing: helps the person resolve their ambivalence between changing and staying the same, without confrontation.
  • Cognitive behavioural therapy and relapse prevention, contingency management (incentives for staying abstinent) and family therapies.
  • Medication: to stop smoking (nicotine replacement, varenicline), for alcohol (naltrexone, acamprosate and others) and opioid substitution treatment such as methadone or buprenorphine.
  • Mutual-help groups and harm-reduction programmes.

How to seek help

Your family doctor can assess and refer you to public addiction services, which in Spain are free. Relapses are part of many people's journey and do not mean failure.

How to support someone

Express concern about concrete facts, without labels ("I've noticed that..." rather than "you're a..."). Set clear limits without cutting the bond. Avoid covering up the consequences of use. Find support for yourself: there are programmes for families.

Myths to avoid

  • "You have to hit rock bottom to change". The earlier the intervention, the better.
  • "Cannabis is harmless because it's natural". Frequent use in adolescence is associated with higher risk of psychosis and problems with memory and motivation.
  • "A glass of wine a day is good for your health". Recent reviews do not support this.

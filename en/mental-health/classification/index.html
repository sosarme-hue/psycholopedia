Mental health

Classification of mental disorders

How WHO's ICD-11 and the DSM-5-TR organise mental disorders, what each major group includes and what a diagnosis is for.

To talk about mental health problems, research them and organise care, professionals use two major classification systems: the ICD (International Classification of Diseases) of the World Health Organization, and the DSM (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders) of the American Psychiatric Association. This section explains how they are organised and what each major group includes, in plain language.

ICD-11, from the WHO

The ICD classifies all diseases, not only mental ones, and is the official reference for health statistics in every country. Its eleventh edition, ICD-11, was adopted by the World Health Assembly in 2019 and came into force on 1 January 2022. Mental disorders are in chapter 6, "Mental, behavioural or neurodevelopmental disorders", except sleep disorders (chapter 7) and conditions related to sexual health (chapter 17). ICD-11 is free to consult online, in several languages. Many countries still use ICD-10 or national adaptations in clinical records during the transition.

DSM-5-TR, from the American Psychiatric Association

The DSM was first published in 1952. Its fifth edition, DSM-5, appeared in 2013, and in 2022 a text revision, DSM-5-TR, was published, adding prolonged grief disorder and updating many descriptions. It is widely used in research and clinical training, especially in the United States. It is a copyrighted work available from the association's publisher, so this website does not reproduce its criteria. It remains the current edition: in January 2026 the association published its roadmap for the next edition, which will be mainly digital and updated more often, but it has no release date or final name yet.

Similarities and differences

The two classifications have converged and group disorders in very similar ways. There are notable differences: ICD-11 includes complex post-traumatic stress disorder and gaming disorder, which DSM-5-TR does not list as official diagnoses; it describes personality disorders by severity and traits rather than by types; and it has moved gender incongruence out of the mental disorders chapter. ICD-11 also offers clinical descriptions designed to be flexible, whereas the DSM relies more on criteria lists with a minimum number of symptoms.

What a diagnosis is for, and what it is not

A diagnosis lets professionals speak the same language, allows research into which treatments work and helps the person access care, sick leave or support. For many people, putting a name to what is happening is a relief. But a diagnosis describes a pattern of difficulties; on its own it does not explain the cause or say who the person is. The boundaries between categories are blurred, meeting criteria for more than one disorder is common, and intensity varies along a continuum with everyday distress. That is why current research is also exploring dimensional models, such as HiTOP or the RDoC of the US National Institute of Mental Health.

This section is not for self-diagnosis. Recognising yourself in a description does not mean you have a disorder: diagnosis requires a professional assessment that takes into account intensity, duration, context and other possible causes. If something you read worries you, talk to your doctor.

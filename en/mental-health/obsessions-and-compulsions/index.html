Obsessions and compulsions

Obsessive-compulsive disorder explained: intrusive thoughts, rituals and treatment.

Medical review Alejandro Rosàs, family doctor

Need help now? In Spain, 024 is free and open 24 hours; in an emergency call 112. Elsewhere, contact your local helpline or emergency services. This text is for general information: it cannot diagnose and does not replace seeing a professional.

What it is

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) has two elements:

  • Obsessions: intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images or urges that cause great anxiety. For example, fear of contamination, of having left the gas on, of accidentally harming someone, or thoughts that clash with one's own values.
  • Compulsions: repetitive behaviours or mental acts the person feels driven to perform to reduce that anxiety or prevent something bad happening: washing, checking, ordering, counting, praying, seeking reassurance.

Compulsions bring momentary relief but strengthen the cycle. OCD affects roughly 1% to 3% of people over their lifetime and usually begins in childhood, adolescence or early adulthood.

What it is not

Being tidy or a perfectionist is not having OCD. Almost everyone has strange or unpleasant thoughts at times; what marks OCD is the distress they cause, the time they take (often more than an hour a day) and how much they interfere. Having an intrusive thought about causing harm does not mean wanting to: it is frightening precisely because it goes against what the person wants.

How it is assessed

Through clinical interview and scales such as the Y-BOCS, which measures severity. Many people take years to seek help out of shame, so it is important to ask about it matter-of-factly.

Which interventions are studied

  • Exposure and response prevention (ERP): a form of cognitive behavioural therapy in which the person gradually faces what makes them anxious without performing the compulsion. It is the first-choice psychological treatment.
  • Medication: SSRIs, often at higher doses than for depression, and clomipramine.
  • In severe, resistant cases, specialist treatment at referral centres.

How to seek help

See your family doctor or a psychology professional experienced in OCD. Describing the thoughts in detail, however embarrassing, helps you get the right treatment.

How to support someone

It is natural to want to reassure the person or join in their rituals to calm them, but this usually keeps the problem going. What helps is to agree with them and their therapist how to gradually reduce that help, kindly and without blame.

Myths to avoid

  • "We're all a bit OCD". It trivialises a disorder that can be very disabling.
  • "Obsessive thoughts reveal hidden desires". They do not.
  • "Reassuring the person will make them better". In the short term, yes; in the long run, it maintains the problem.

Mental health

Trauma

Reactions after traumatic experiences, post-traumatic stress, resilience and effective treatments.

Medical review Alejandro Rosàs, family doctor

Need help now? In Spain, 024 is free and open 24 hours; in an emergency call 112. Elsewhere, contact your local helpline or emergency services. This text is for general information: it cannot diagnose and does not replace seeing a professional. Where to get help →

What it is

A traumatic event is an experience in which a person lives through or witnesses a serious threat to their own or others' life or safety: an accident, an assault, sexual violence, a disaster, war or the violent death of a loved one.

After such an event it is normal to have nightmares, intrusive memories, jumpiness, insomnia or an urge to avoid reminders for days or weeks. In most people these symptoms fade with time and support: this is resilience, far more common than was once thought.

In some people the symptoms persist beyond a month and cause significant suffering. This is called post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), which includes:

  • Re-experiencing: intrusive memories, nightmares or the feeling of reliving it.
  • Avoidance of related thoughts, places or people.
  • Hyperarousal: being on guard, easily startled, irritable, sleeping badly.
  • Changes in mood and in how one sees the world, such as guilt, shame or mistrust.

When trauma has been prolonged or repeated, as with childhood abuse or intimate partner violence, complex PTSD may develop, with added difficulties in regulating emotions and relating to others.

What it is not

Not every difficult experience is trauma in the clinical sense, and not everyone exposed to trauma will develop a disorder. Using the word "trauma" for any upset can make those who truly suffer less visible.

How it is assessed

With a careful interview, without forcing the person to recount details, and questionnaires such as the PCL-5. Alcohol or drug use, depression and suicide risk, which are common, are also explored.

Which interventions are studied

  • Trauma-focused psychological therapies: trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure and EMDR are well supported.
  • Medication: some antidepressants can help, as a complement or when psychotherapy is not possible.
  • In the first weeks, psychological first aid is recommended: safety, calm, connection with loved ones and practical help. Compulsory "debriefing", asking everyone to recount what happened immediately afterwards, does not prevent PTSD and may make it worse.

How to seek help

If a month after the event you are still reliving it or it is limiting you, see your family doctor or a psychology professional. In Spain, gender-based violence helpline 016 is free and leaves no trace on the phone bill, and the ANAR Foundation helps children and teenagers on 900 20 20 10.

How to support someone

Offer safety and presence. Let the person tell what they want, when they want, without pressure. Help with practical things and respect their pace. Avoid phrases like "you should be over it by now".

Myths to avoid

  • "Anyone who has lived through trauma is scarred for life". Most people recover, and PTSD has effective treatments.
  • "You have to talk about it straight away". Forcing it does not help; everyone has their own pace.
  • "Traumatic memories are erased and later return intact". Traumatic memory, like all memory, is reconstructive; techniques to "recover" forgotten memories can create false ones.

Abraham Maslow

American psychologist and one of the founders of humanistic psychology. He proposed a hierarchy of needs culminating in self-actualisation, though he never drew it as a pyramid.

Biography

Abraham Harold Maslow was born in 1908 in Brooklyn, New York, the eldest son of Jewish immigrants from Kiev. He described a lonely childhood spent taking refuge in libraries. He studied psychology at the University of Wisconsin, where he took his doctorate under Harry Harlow, studying dominance and sexual behaviour in monkeys.

He taught at Brooklyn College and, from 1951, at Brandeis University. In 1967 he was president of the American Psychological Association. He died of a heart attack in California in 1970.

Questions he investigated

  • What motivates people beyond hunger or fear?
  • What are the most psychologically healthy and fulfilled people like?
  • Can psychology study the best in human beings, not only their problems?

Contributions

In 1943 he proposed that human needs are organised in levels: physiological, safety, belonging and love, esteem and self-actualisation. When the more basic ones are reasonably satisfied, the next ones gain strength. He insisted that this was not a rigid order and that much behaviour responds to several needs at once.

He studied people he considered self-actualised, among them Abraham Lincoln, Eleanor Roosevelt and Albert Einstein, and described common traits such as self-acceptance, spontaneity and the capacity for peak experiences, moments of intense fulfilment.

Collaborations, influences and disagreements

He was influenced by Gestalt psychologists such as Max Wertheimer and by the anthropologist Ruth Benedict, whom he admired and who inspired his idea of self-actualisation. With Carl Rogers he was one of the driving forces of humanistic psychology. Late in life he also promoted transpersonal psychology. His work is one of the forerunners of positive psychology.

A documented anecdote

The famous Maslow pyramid does not appear in any of his works. According to a 2019 study, it was drawn by a management consultant, Charles McDermid, in a business magazine in 1960, and spread through management textbooks.

Criticism, limits and reception

His method for studying self-actualisation was unsystematic: he chose for himself the people who seemed exemplary to him. In 1976 a review by Mahmoud Wahba and Lawrence Bridwell found little support for a strict order of needs. In 2011 Louis Tay and Ed Diener analysed data from 123 countries and found that the needs he proposed are universal and linked to wellbeing, but are not necessarily satisfied in order.

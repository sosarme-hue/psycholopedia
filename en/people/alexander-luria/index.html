Alexander Luria

Soviet neuropsychologist. A colleague of Vygotsky, he studied how culture shapes thinking and founded modern neuropsychology through his work with soldiers wounded in the Second World War.

Biography

Alexander Romanovich Luria was born in Kazan in 1902 into a family of physicians. As a young man he became interested in psychoanalysis, even corresponding with Freud, and in 1924 he moved to Moscow. There he met Lev Vygotsky and, with Alexei Leontiev, formed the "troika" that developed cultural-historical psychology.

In the early 1930s he led expeditions to Uzbekistan to study how the reasoning of newly literate people changed. Under political pressure, he then studied medicine. During the Second World War he ran a rehabilitation hospital for soldiers with brain injuries. Later he worked at the Burdenko Institute of Neurosurgery and at Moscow University. He died in 1977.

Questions he investigated

  • Does the way people think change when schooling and social life change?
  • How are complex functions such as language, planning and memory organised in the brain?
  • How can functions lost after a brain injury be recovered?

Contributions

Luria proposed that higher mental functions are not located at a single point in the brain but depend on functional systems in which several regions work together. That is why the same function can be disturbed by lesions in different places, and in different ways. From that idea he designed methods of qualitative neuropsychological assessment and rehabilitation programmes that made use of the patient's preserved abilities.

He also wrote two books he called "romantic science", combining clinical rigour with the story of a life: one about Shereshevsky, a man with an almost limitless memory, and one about Zasetsky, a soldier who after a head wound spent years trying to rebuild his world by writing.

Collaborations, influences and disagreements

His work continued and extended Vygotsky's. He influenced neuropsychologists all over the world and the neurologist Oliver Sacks, who saw him as a model for his own clinical stories. Several later neuropsychological tests were based on his procedures.

Criticism, limits and reception

The conclusions of his Uzbekistan studies have been debated because of the risk of reading as a deficit what was a different way of reasoning, tied to practical experience. His highly qualitative assessment methods were later standardised in batteries such as the Luria-Nebraska, not without controversy. His idea of functional systems fits well with today's view of the brain as a set of networks.

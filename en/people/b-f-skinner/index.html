B. F. Skinner

American psychologist and the central figure of radical behaviourism. He studied how the consequences of a behaviour make it more or less likely to recur, which he called operant conditioning.

Biography

Burrhus Frederic Skinner was born in 1904 in Susquehanna, Pennsylvania. He wanted to be a writer and, after graduating in English literature, spent a year trying without success. Reading Pavlov and Watson drew him to psychology, and he took his doctorate at Harvard in 1931. He worked at the universities of Minnesota and Indiana and returned to Harvard in 1948, where he spent the rest of his career.

He was one of the best-known psychologists of the 20th century, also outside academia, thanks to his books on society and education. He died in 1990, a few days after giving his last address to the American Psychological Association.

Questions he investigated

  • How do the consequences of a behaviour influence whether it is repeated?
  • What happens when reinforcement comes only sometimes or at a particular rate?
  • Can language and social behaviour be explained by the same principles?

Contributions

Skinner designed a chamber, the Skinner box, in which a rat or pigeon could press a lever or peck a key to receive food, while a device recorded every response. With it he studied operant conditioning: behaviours followed by reinforcement increase, and those that are not reinforced die out.

With Charles Ferster he described schedules of reinforcement. Intermittent reinforcement, for example, produces very persistent behaviour, which helps explain why slot machines are so hard to walk away from. He also developed shaping, reinforcing successive approximations to a behaviour, and applied it to education with his "teaching machines".

Collaborations, influences and disagreements

Skinner started from Pavlov, Watson and Thorndike, but differed from Watson in treating thoughts as behaviours too, albeit private ones. In 1959 the linguist Noam Chomsky published a harsh review of his book Verbal Behavior, regarded as one of the texts that sparked the cognitive revolution. His principles gave rise to applied behaviour analysis and to techniques used today in education and therapy.

A documented anecdote

For his second daughter, Deborah, Skinner designed an enclosed, glass-fronted, temperature-controlled crib. A magazine presented it in 1945 as "Baby in a box", and for decades a rumour circulated that the girl had been raised in an experimental box and had come to a bad end. Deborah, as an adult, denied it in 2004 in an article in The Guardian: the crib was just a crib and she had a happy childhood.

Criticism, limits and reception

He was criticised for setting aside mental processes and biology, and for extrapolating from rats and pigeons to human society. His proposals in Beyond Freedom and Dignity, which questioned the idea of free will, caused great controversy. But the principles of reinforcement are among psychology's most solid findings, and lie behind many effective therapies and the design of apps and video games.

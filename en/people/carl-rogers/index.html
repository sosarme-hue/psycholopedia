Carl Rogers

American psychologist who championed humanistic psychology and person-centred therapy. He argued that the therapist's empathy, acceptance and genuineness foster change.

Biography

Carl Ransom Rogers was born in 1902 in Oak Park, near Chicago, into a very strict Protestant family. He began by studying agriculture and then history at the University of Wisconsin. A trip to China with a Christian group made him rethink his beliefs. He studied at a theological seminary in New York and then switched to psychology at Columbia University.

He worked with children in Rochester for twelve years and was then a professor at the universities of Ohio State, Chicago and Wisconsin. In 1947 he was president of the American Psychological Association. In his final years, in California, he devoted himself to encounter groups and conflict-reduction workshops, for example in Northern Ireland and South Africa. He died in 1987.

Questions he investigated

  • What really helps a person to change in therapy?
  • Can what happens in a session be studied scientifically?
  • What conditions foster personal growth?

Contributions

Rogers proposed a therapy in which the client, not the expert, takes centre stage. The therapist does not interpret or give advice, but helps the person explore their own experience. He trusted in an actualising tendency: we all tend to develop our capacities if the environment allows it.

In 1957 he set out the conditions that, in his view, were enough for therapeutic change. The three best known are empathy, understanding the client's world from the inside; unconditional positive regard, valuing them without judging; and the therapist's congruence or genuineness. He was also a pioneer in recording therapy sessions for research, something then considered almost sacrilegious.

Collaborations, influences and disagreements

With Abraham Maslow and Rollo May he promoted humanistic psychology, the "third force" alongside psychoanalysis and behaviourism. In 1956 he held a public debate with B. F. Skinner about the control of human behaviour and freedom. His ideas influenced counselling, education, nursing and social work.

A documented anecdote

In 1964 Rogers, Fritz Perls and Albert Ellis were filmed, separately, working with the same client, Gloria, to show three forms of therapy. The film, known as "Gloria", was used for decades in therapist training.

Criticism, limits and reception

Later studies did not confirm that his conditions were sufficient on their own to treat every problem. But they have shown that empathy and a good alliance between therapist and patient are linked to better outcomes in almost all therapies. His ideas are now part of the basic training of any professional who listens.

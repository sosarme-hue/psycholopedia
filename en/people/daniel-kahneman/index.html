Daniel Kahneman

Israeli-American psychologist and winner of the 2002 Nobel Prize in Economics. With Amos Tversky he showed that we judge and decide using shortcuts that produce systematic errors, founding behavioural economics.

Biography

Daniel Kahneman was born in 1934 in Tel Aviv, during a visit by his mother to her family, but grew up in Paris. His family was Jewish and spent the Nazi occupation of France in hiding and on the move. His father died in 1944. In 1948 the family emigrated to Palestine, shortly before the founding of the State of Israel.

He studied psychology and mathematics at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and did his military service in the Israeli army's psychology department. He took his doctorate at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1961. He taught in Jerusalem, in Canada, and at the University of California and Princeton. He received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2002. He died in March 2024, aged 90.

Questions he investigated

  • How do we make decisions under uncertainty?
  • Why do we make systematic and predictable errors of judgement?
  • Does what we experience match what we remember experiencing?

Contributions

With Amos Tversky he described the heuristics of representativeness, availability and anchoring, and formulated prospect theory, according to which losses weigh more than equivalent gains. These ideas challenged the economic assumption that people always decide rationally and gave rise to behavioural economics.

He also studied wellbeing. With Donald Redelmeier he showed in 1996 that people's memory of a colonoscopy depended mainly on its most painful moment and its end, and hardly at all on its length: the peak-end rule. From this he distinguished between the "experiencing self" and the "remembering self". In Thinking, Fast and Slow he popularised the distinction between a fast, intuitive System 1 and a slow, deliberate System 2.

Collaborations, influences and disagreements

His collaboration with Tversky is one of the most famous in science. He had a long debate with Gerd Gigerenzer, who defended the value of heuristics, and with Gary Klein, with whom he eventually wrote a joint paper on where they agreed about intuition. He promoted "adversarial collaborations", in which researchers who disagree design a study together to settle the dispute.

A documented anecdote

In his Nobel autobiography he recalled a childhood memory from occupied Paris. Walking home after curfew, with his yellow star hidden under his sweater, he came across an SS soldier. The soldier called him over, hugged him, showed him a photo of a boy and gave him some money. Kahneman wrote that it confirmed what his mother used to say: that people are endlessly complicated and interesting.

Criticism, limits and reception

Some studies he cited in Thinking, Fast and Slow, especially those in the chapter on priming, have not replicated. Kahneman acknowledged this publicly and admitted he had placed too much trust in studies with small samples. His core findings with Tversky, by contrast, have been confirmed very consistently.

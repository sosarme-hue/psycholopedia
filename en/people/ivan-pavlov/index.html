Iván Pávlov

Russian physiologist, winner of the 1904 Nobel Prize in Medicine for his work on digestion. While studying salivation in dogs he described the conditioned reflex, the basis of classical conditioning.

Biography

Ivan Petrovich Pavlov was born in 1849 in Ryazan, the son of an Orthodox priest, and studied at the seminary before switching to natural science in St Petersburg. He trained in medicine and physiology and worked in Germany with Carl Ludwig and Rudolf Heidenhain. From 1891 he headed the physiology department of the Institute of Experimental Medicine in St Petersburg, where he developed surgical techniques for studying digestion in living, healthy animals.

In 1904 he received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, the first awarded to a Russian scientist. He kept working after the 1917 revolution, publicly criticised some of the new regime's excesses and, even so, received Soviet government support for his laboratory. He died in Leningrad in 1936.

Questions he investigated

  • How does the nervous system regulate the secretion of the digestive glands?
  • Why do dogs salivate before receiving food, on seeing the assistant or hearing a sound?
  • What happens when an animal cannot tell two signals apart?

Contributions

Pavlov and his colleagues observed that a neutral stimulus, such as a metronome, eventually triggered salivation if it was repeatedly presented just before food. He called this learned response a conditioned reflex. He measured precisely how it is acquired, how it is extinguished when the stimulus no longer signals food, how it generalises to similar stimuli and how the animal learns to discriminate. When discrimination became impossible, the dogs showed persistent agitation, which he called experimental neurosis.

His method made learning measurable in the laboratory and was taken up by the American behaviourists, especially John B. Watson.

Collaborations, influences and disagreements

Much of the work was carried out by his many collaborators, including several women researchers such as Maria Yerofeeva and Maria Petrova. Pavlov saw himself as a physiologist, not a psychologist, and distrusted explanations based on consciousness. He clashed with Vladimir Bekhterev, who studied conditioned motor reflexes.

A documented anecdote

The popular image of Pavlov ringing a bell is a simplification. In his experiments he mostly used metronomes, buzzers, lights and touch stimuli. The historian Daniel Todes has pointed out that the bell rarely appears in his published work.

Criticism, limits and reception

We now know that classical conditioning is not a simple automatic association by contiguity: it depends on how much a signal tells the animal about what will happen, as Robert Rescorla and Leon Kamin showed in the 1960s. The experiments on dogs, which involved surgery, are now judged by much stricter ethical standards. Even so, his principles still explain phenomena such as phobias, taste aversions and some immune system responses.

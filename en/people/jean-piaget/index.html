Jean Piaget

Swiss biologist and epistemologist. He studied how children construct knowledge and proposed one of the most influential theories of development of the twentieth century.

Biography

Jean Piaget was born in Neuchâtel, Switzerland, in 1896. As a teenager he was already publishing scientific notes on molluscs, and he earned a doctorate in natural sciences in 1918. In Paris he worked on standardising reasoning tests for children and realised that what was interesting was less the right answers than the mistakes: children of the same age went wrong in similar ways. In 1921 he joined the Jean-Jacques Rousseau Institute in Geneva, where he spent almost his whole career. He observed his three children in detail and worked for decades with Bärbel Inhelder. In 1955 he founded the International Centre for Genetic Epistemology. He died in Geneva in 1980.

Questions he investigated

  • How does a baby move from reflexes to logical thought?
  • Why does a young child believe a tall, narrow glass holds more water than a short, wide one?
  • How is knowledge built across a lifetime and across history?

Contributions

Piaget presented the child as an active builder of knowledge. Schemas grow through assimilation (fitting the new into what is known) and change through accommodation (altering what is known when it does not fit). He described four broad stages: sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational and formal operational. His conservation, object-permanence and three-mountains tasks are still used in teaching.

Collaborations, influences and disagreements

His work was in dialogue, sometimes at a distance, with that of Lev Vygotsky, who gave more weight to language and social interaction. He influenced active pedagogy and educational reforms in many countries.

Criticism, limits and reception

Later research showed that babies and young children know more than Piaget assumed when tasks are simplified, that the move between stages is less uniform, and that culture and schooling matter. His methods, based on interviews and few cases, were also questioned. Even so, his questions and many of his observations remain relevant.

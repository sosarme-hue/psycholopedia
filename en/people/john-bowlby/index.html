John Bowlby

British psychiatrist and psychoanalyst who created attachment theory. He argued that a baby's bond with its caregivers is a basic need, not just a by-product of being fed.

Biography

Edward John Mostyn Bowlby was born in London in 1907, into a well-off family. As was usual for his social class, he was raised mainly by nannies and saw little of his parents. At seven he was sent to boarding school. He later said he would not send "a dog" away to boarding school at that age.

He studied at Cambridge, worked as a volunteer at schools for troubled children and then trained in medicine, psychiatry and psychoanalysis. After serving as an army psychiatrist in the Second World War, he headed the children's department of the Tavistock Clinic in London. He died in 1990 on the Scottish island of Skye.

Questions he investigated

  • Why do children separated from their families suffer so much, even when well fed?
  • How do early relationships shape later emotional life?
  • How do we react to separation and loss?

Contributions

In a 1944 study of 44 young people who had committed thefts, Bowlby noticed that many had suffered long separations from their mothers in childhood. In 1951 the World Health Organization commissioned him to report on homeless children after the war, and he argued that a lack of stable maternal care harmed development.

Drawing on ethology, especially the work of Konrad Lorenz and Harry Harlow, he formulated attachment theory. According to it, babies are born predisposed to seek closeness to those who care for them, because this favoured survival. The attachment figure acts as a secure base from which to explore the world. Through experience, the child builds internal working models: expectations about whether others will be available and whether they deserve care.

Collaborations, influences and disagreements

With the social worker James Robertson he filmed the distress of young children admitted to hospital without their parents. The film helped change hospital rules, and today parents can stay with their children. Mary Ainsworth worked with him in London and later tested his ideas with the Strange Situation. Bowlby clashed with the psychoanalysis of his day, particularly Melanie Klein, for focusing on the child's fantasies rather than on what had actually happened to them.

A documented anecdote

Robertson's film A Two-Year-Old Goes to Hospital (1952) follows a girl called Laura through eight days in hospital. At first it was heavily criticised by doctors and nurses, who doubted that what it showed was real.

Criticism, limits and reception

He was criticised for placing too much weight on the mother and on the earliest years. Michael Rutter showed in 1972 that the harm came more from a lack of stable, good-quality relationships than from separation itself, and that children can become attached to several people. It has also been pointed out that child-rearing varies greatly across cultures. Even so, attachment theory is one of the best-supported theories in developmental psychology.

Mary Whiton Calkins

American psychologist and philosopher. She completed every requirement for a Harvard doctorate, which was refused because she was a woman, and was the first woman to preside over the American Psychological Association.

Biography

Mary Whiton Calkins was born in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1863. She studied classics and philosophy at Smith College and began teaching Greek at Wellesley College, a women's college that offered her the new psychology course if she trained in the subject. Harvard did not admit women; after her father and Wellesley's president intervened, she was allowed to attend William James's seminars as a guest and later to work in Hugo Münsterberg's laboratory.

In 1895 she passed the doctoral examination. Her examiners judged her excellent and Münsterberg wrote that she was one of his best students, but Harvard refused to award the degree. In 1902 she declined a doctorate from Radcliffe, Harvard's associated women's college, because she did not want a degree for work that had not been done there. She taught at Wellesley until 1929 and died in 1930.

Questions she investigated

  • What makes us remember an association between two items better: frequency, vividness or recency?
  • Can psychology do without the notion of a "self" who experiences?

Contributions

In 1891 she founded the Wellesley psychology laboratory, one of the first led by a woman. To study memory she developed the paired-associates technique: pairs (for instance a colour and a number) are presented, and later the first item is shown so that the second can be recalled. The technique is still in use. She argued for a self psychology: experience always belongs to someone, and that should be part of the science.

In 1905 she became the first woman president of the American Psychological Association, and in 1918 of the American Philosophical Association.

Collaborations, influences and disagreements

Besides James and Münsterberg, she trained in laboratory methods with Edmund Sanford at Clark University and in 1893 published a statistical study of dreams. She argued against Titchener's structuralism and early behaviourism, which she felt left the person out.

Criticism, limits and reception

Her self psychology found few followers in her lifetime, as behaviourism expanded, and her name stayed in the background for decades. Historians of psychology have since restored her as a memory researcher and as an example of the institutional barriers women faced.

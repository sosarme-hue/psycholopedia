Max Wertheimer

Prague-born psychologist and founder of Gestalt psychology. His study of apparent motion showed that we perceive organised wholes, not sums of separate sensations.

Biography

Max Wertheimer was born in Prague in 1880, into a Jewish family of educators. He studied law, philosophy and psychology in Prague, Berlin and Würzburg, where he took his doctorate in 1904. He worked in Frankfurt and Berlin, and in 1929 he was appointed to a chair at the University of Frankfurt.

In 1933, shortly before Hitler came to power, he emigrated with his family to the United States. He joined the New School for Social Research in New York, which took in many academics fleeing Nazism. He died there in 1943.

Questions he investigated

  • Why do we see movement where there are only still images in succession?
  • How does perception group elements into shapes and objects?
  • What distinguishes thinking that understands a problem from thinking that merely repeats a procedure?

Contributions

In 1912 he published his experiments on apparent motion. If two lines light up one after the other at the right interval, we see a single line moving. He called one form of this effect the phi phenomenon: perceived movement that is not present in either stimulus alone. For him it showed that experience cannot be broken down into elementary sensations, as the psychology of his day assumed. "The whole is different from the sum of its parts" became the motto of Gestalt psychology.

In 1923 he described the grouping laws: we tend to group what is close together, what is similar, what follows the same direction or what forms a closed figure, and we prefer the simplest and most stable organisation possible (Prägnanz). In Productive Thinking he argued that solving a problem well requires grasping its structure, not applying memorised rules.

Collaborations, influences and disagreements

The participants in his first experiments were Wolfgang Köhler and Kurt Koffka, with whom he founded the Gestalt school and the journal Psychologische Forschung. Kurt Lewin, influenced by them, took these ideas into social psychology.

A documented anecdote

Wertheimer was a friend of Albert Einstein in Berlin, and interviewed him for hours about how he had arrived at the theory of relativity. Those conversations make up one of the chapters of Productive Thinking, published after his death.

Criticism, limits and reception

Gestalt psychology was criticised for describing phenomena very well but explaining them little, and its theory of electrical "fields" in the brain was not confirmed. Even so, its grouping laws remain in perception textbooks, are used in graphic and interface design, and current research in vision and neuroscience has taken up many of its questions.

Sigmund Freud

Austrian neurologist and founder of psychoanalysis. He proposed that much of mental life is unconscious and developed a talking treatment that influenced all later psychotherapy.

Biography

Sigmund Freud was born in 1856 in Freiberg, Moravia (now Příbor in the Czech Republic), into a Jewish family that moved to Vienna when he was four. He studied medicine at the University of Vienna and spent several years in neurological research. In 1885 he spent some months in Paris with the neurologist Jean-Martin Charcot, who studied hysteria using hypnosis, and on his return he opened a private practice to treat nervous disorders.

For almost fifty years he lived and worked at Berggasse 19 in Vienna. After Nazi Germany annexed Austria in 1938, and after the Gestapo had questioned his daughter Anna, he emigrated to London. He died there in September 1939, after sixteen years of struggling with cancer of the jaw.

Questions he investigated

  • Why do some people have physical symptoms with no organic cause to explain them?
  • What do dreams, slips of the tongue and lapses of memory mean?
  • How do childhood experiences and unacknowledged wishes shape adult life?

Contributions

With the physician Josef Breuer, who had treated the patient known as Anna O., he published Studies on Hysteria in 1895. He gradually gave up hypnosis and replaced it with free association: the patient says everything that comes to mind, without censoring it.

In The Interpretation of Dreams (1900) he proposed that dreams express unconscious wishes in disguised form. Later he described the mind as three agencies, the id, the ego and the superego, and explained repression and other defence mechanisms with which we protect ourselves from what distresses us. He also described transference: the patient relives with the therapist emotions from past relationships.

Collaborations, influences and disagreements

Freud gathered a circle of followers but broke with several of the most prominent. Alfred Adler left in 1911 and Carl Gustav Jung in 1913, both disagreeing with the weight Freud gave to sexuality. His daughter Anna Freud continued his work, focusing on children and on defence mechanisms. Many different psychoanalytic schools followed.

A documented anecdote

In 1909, invited by G. Stanley Hall, Freud gave five lectures at Clark University in Massachusetts and received an honorary doctorate. It was his only trip to the United States. He later wrote that it was the first official recognition of his work and felt like "the realization of some incredible day-dream".

Criticism, limits and reception

The philosopher Karl Popper criticised many psychoanalytic ideas as untestable, because any result can be interpreted in their favour. Many of Freud's specific claims, such as a universal Oedipus complex or his view of female sexuality, have not found empirical support. His case histories rested on few patients, and historians have critically re-examined several of them.

Even so, his influence is enormous. The idea that much mental processing is unconscious is now accepted in cognitive psychology, though in a very different form from his. Present-day psychodynamic therapies, shorter and more structured, have studies showing they are effective for some problems.

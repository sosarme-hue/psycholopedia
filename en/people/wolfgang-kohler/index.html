Wolfgang Köhler

German Gestalt psychologist. In Tenerife he studied how chimpanzees solved problems and described learning by sudden understanding, or insight.

Biography

Wolfgang Köhler was born in 1887 in Reval (now Tallinn, Estonia), into a German family, and grew up in Germany. He studied physics and psychology and took his doctorate in Berlin in 1909 under Carl Stumpf. In Frankfurt he was a participant in Max Wertheimer's experiments on apparent motion.

In 1913 the Prussian Academy of Sciences appointed him to direct its anthropoid station in Tenerife, where he stayed until 1920, partly because of the First World War. He then directed the Institute of Psychology at the University of Berlin. He stood up to the Nazi regime and in 1935 emigrated to the United States, to Swarthmore College. He was president of the American Psychological Association in 1959 and died in 1967.

Questions he investigated

  • Do chimpanzees solve problems by trial and error or by understanding the situation?
  • How does the organisation of what we perceive relate to brain activity?

Contributions

In Tenerife he placed bananas out of the chimpanzees' reach and watched what they did. Some, after several failed attempts, would sit still and then suddenly solve the problem: stacking boxes to reach the fruit or using a stick to draw it closer. A chimpanzee called Sultan even fitted two sticks together to make a longer one. Köhler called this insight: a sudden grasp of the structure of the problem that restructures the situation. He contrasted it with the gradual trial-and-error learning that Thorndike had described with his cats.

He later devoted himself to Gestalt theory and defended isomorphism: the idea that the organisation of what we perceive corresponds to the organisation of brain processes.

Collaborations, influences and disagreements

With Max Wertheimer and Kurt Koffka he formed the core of the Gestalt school. His book Gestalt Psychology (1929) was the school's best-known account in English. His chimpanzee studies opened the way for research on animal cognition.

A documented anecdote

In April 1933 Köhler published an article in a Berlin newspaper defending the Jewish professors who had been expelled from universities. It is regarded as one of the last public criticisms of the Nazi regime published in Germany. He expected to be arrested, but was not. He went on resisting Nazi interference in his institute until he resigned and emigrated.

Criticism, limits and reception

Some researchers pointed out that chimpanzees with prior experience of playing with sticks solved these tasks better, suggesting that insight builds on earlier learning. His theory of brain fields was not confirmed. But the idea that problem solving can involve restructuring the situation remains alive in the psychology of thinking and creativity.
